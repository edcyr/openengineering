Things to try

  1. A vibration. Type \(s = 0.5\sin 2t\): the acceleration graph is the position graph turned upside down and four times as tall, because \(a = -4s\).
  2. Up and down. A ball thrown up at \(15\ \text{m/s}\) has \(a = -9.81\ \text{m/s}^2\) at every instant, including at the top where \(v = 0\).
  3. Drag. With \(a = -0.01v^2\) the boat of Lesson 5 never quite stops: the distance keeps growing, ever more slowly.
  4. A spring with damping. Add a drag term to the spring: \(a = -4s - 0.5v\). The vibration dies away, and the amber lines mark each turn.
  5. Back and forth. Drag the velocity graph below zero for a stretch: the particle backs up, the position graph turns down, and the distance traveled keeps growing while the position falls.
  6. Overtaking from rest. Car \(B\) starts \(60\ \text{m}\) behind \(A\), at rest, with \(a = 3\ \text{m/s}^2\), while \(A\) cruises at \(20\ \text{m/s}\). Watch from \(A\).
  7. Four parts of rope. The load rises at a quarter of the speed of the free end.

How the lab works

A position formula is differentiated numerically to give \(v\) and \(a\) (Lesson 3). An acceleration formula \(a(t, s, v)\) is integrated in thousands of small time steps with the fourth-order Runge–Kutta method, which handles accelerations that depend on time, velocity or position alike (Lesson 5). Constant acceleration and the velocity graph are solved exactly (Lesson 4 and Lesson 6). The distance traveled adds up every small step as a positive length.

Typing formulas

Use t, s and v as the variables and ^ for powers: t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t, 20 - 2t, -9.81 - 0.02v*abs(v), 5e^(-0.4t), 2sin(3t), ln(1+t). Angles inside sin and cos are in radians.
