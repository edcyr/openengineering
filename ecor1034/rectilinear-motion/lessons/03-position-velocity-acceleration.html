Position, Velocity and Acceleration

A particle that moves along a straight line is in rectilinear motion. A car on a straight road, a piston in its cylinder and a ball dropped from a balcony all move this way. One number, the position \(s\), says where the particle is, and its derivatives say how it moves. This lesson defines position, displacement, velocity and acceleration, and the relation \(a\,ds = v\,dv\) that ties them together without time.

Learning objectives

  • Describe a particle's location by a position coordinate \(s\) measured from a fixed origin, with a chosen positive direction.
  • Tell displacement from distance traveled, and average velocity from average speed.
  • Find the velocity and acceleration from \(v = ds/dt\) and \(a = dv/dt\), and decide when a particle speeds up or slows down.
  • Use \(a\,ds = v\,dv\) when the velocity is known as a function of position.

Position and displacement

Choose a fixed origin \(O\) on the line and a positive direction. The position coordinate \(s\) is the signed distance from \(O\) to the particle: positive on one side, negative on the other, measured in meters. The displacement over an interval is the change in position,

\[ \Delta s = s_2 - s_1 \]

Displacement is a signed quantity: it says how far the particle ended up from where it started, and on which side. The distance traveled \(s_T\) is the total length of path covered, always positive. They agree only if the particle never reverses. A runner who goes \(100\ \text{m}\) out and \(100\ \text{m}\) back has a displacement of zero and a distance traveled of \(200\ \text{m}\).

Velocity and speed

The average velocity over an interval \(\Delta t\) is the displacement divided by the time, \(v_{\text{avg}} = \Delta s/\Delta t\). Let the interval shrink to zero and you get the instantaneous velocity, the derivative of Lesson 1:

Velocity

\[ v = \frac{ds}{dt} \]

Positive when the particle moves in the positive direction, negative when it moves the other way. Its magnitude is the speed.

The average speed is the distance traveled divided by the time, \((v_{sp})_{\text{avg}} = s_T/\Delta t\). It is never negative, and it is at least as large as the magnitude of the average velocity.

Acceleration

The acceleration is the rate of change of the velocity:

Acceleration

\[ a = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2 s}{dt^2} \]

Units: \(\text{m/s}^2\). The sign of \(a\) tells which way the velocity is changing, not whether the particle speeds up.

A negative acceleration does not always mean slowing down. The particle speeds up when \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign, and slows down when they have opposite signs. A ball falling downward, with up positive, has \(v < 0\) and \(a < 0\): it speeds up. The word deceleration means slowing down, whatever the sign convention.

Example 3.1 — A particle that reverses twice

A particle moves along a line with \(s = t^3 - 6t^2 + 9t\), with \(s\) in meters and \(t\) in seconds. (a) Find its velocity and acceleration at \(t = 2\ \text{s}\). Is it speeding up or slowing down? (b) For the interval from \(t = 0\) to \(5\ \text{s}\), find the displacement, the distance traveled, the average velocity and the average speed.

Show solution
\[ v = \frac{ds}{dt} = 3t^2 - 12t + 9 = 3(t - 1)(t - 3)\ \text{m/s} \qquad a = \frac{dv}{dt} = 6t - 12\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

(a) At \(t = 2\ \text{s}\): \(v = 12 - 24 + 9 = -3\ \text{m/s}\) and \(a = 0\). With no acceleration at this instant, the speed is neither growing nor shrinking: it is at its largest for the backward leg, \(3\ \text{m/s}\).

(b) The particle stops where \(v = 0\), at \(t = 1\) and \(t = 3\ \text{s}\). Find the position at the start, at each stop and at the end:

\[ s(0) = 0 \qquad s(1) = 4\ \text{m} \qquad s(3) = 0 \qquad s(5) = 125 - 150 + 45 = 20\ \text{m} \]

Displacement: \(\Delta s = 20 - 0 = 20\ \text{m}\). Distance: \(4\) out, \(4\) back and \(20\) out again, so \(s_T = 28\ \text{m}\).

\[ v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{20}{5} = 4\ \text{m/s} \qquad\qquad (v_{sp})_{\text{avg}} = \frac{28}{5} = 5.6\ \text{m/s} \]

To find a distance traveled, always locate the instants where \(v = 0\) first: the particle turns back there, and the distance counts each leg separately.

Eliminating time: \(a\,ds = v\,dv\)

Sometimes you know the velocity or the acceleration as a function of position, not of time. Divide \(v = ds/dt\) into \(a = dv/dt\): the \(dt\) cancels, and

The relation without time

\[ a\,ds = v\,dv \qquad\text{or}\qquad a = v\,\frac{dv}{ds} \]

The three relations \(v = ds/dt\), \(a = dv/dt\) and \(a\,ds = v\,dv\) are not independent: any two give the third. They hold for every rectilinear motion.

Example 3.2 — Acceleration from v(s)

A particle starts from rest at the origin, and its velocity depends on its position as \(v = 4\sqrt{s}\), with \(v\) in m/s and \(s\) in meters. Find its acceleration, and its position as a function of time.

Show solution
\[ a = v\,\frac{dv}{ds} = 4\sqrt{s}\,\cdot\,\frac{4}{2\sqrt{s}} = 8\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

The acceleration is constant even though \(v\) changes with position. For \(s(t)\), separate the variables in \(ds/dt = 4\sqrt{s}\):

\[ \int_0^s \frac{ds}{\sqrt{s}} = \int_0^t 4\,dt \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2\sqrt{s} = 4t \quad\Rightarrow\quad s = 4t^2\ \text{m} \]

Check: \(v = ds/dt = 8t = 4\sqrt{4t^2}\), and \(a = 8\ \text{m/s}^2\).

Check your understanding

Questions 2 and 3 use the same particle: \(s = 2t^3 - 24t + 6\), with \(s\) in meters and \(t\) in seconds.

Key takeaways

  • Position \(s\) is measured from a fixed origin, with a sign. Displacement \(\Delta s\) is signed; distance traveled \(s_T\) is not.
  • \(v = ds/dt\), \(a = dv/dt\) and \(a\,ds = v\,dv\) hold for every rectilinear motion.
  • Speeding up: \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign. Slowing down: opposite signs.
  • For a distance traveled, find where \(v = 0\) first and add each leg.
