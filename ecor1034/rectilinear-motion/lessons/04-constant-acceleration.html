Constant Acceleration

A car braking hard, a train pulling out of a station and a stone falling from a bridge all move with an acceleration that is nearly constant. For that case the three kinematic relations integrate once and for all into three equations you will use constantly. This lesson derives them, shows how to apply them to braking and free fall, and stresses the condition that comes with them: the acceleration must be constant.

Learning objectives

  • Derive the constant-acceleration equations by integrating with limits.
  • Choose the equation that matches the quantities given and asked for.
  • Solve braking problems with a reaction time, and free-fall problems with \(g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2\).
  • Recognize when the acceleration is not constant and these equations do not apply.

Three equations from integration

Let the acceleration have the constant value \(a_c\), and let the particle start at \(s = s_0\) with \(v = v_0\) when \(t = 0\). Integrate each kinematic relation with matching limits, as in Lesson 2.

Velocity as a function of time. From \(a_c = dv/dt\):

\[ \int_{v_0}^{v} dv = \int_0^t a_c\,dt \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = v_0 + a_c t \]

Position as a function of time. From \(v = ds/dt\), with the result just found:

\[ \int_{s_0}^{s} ds = \int_0^t (v_0 + a_c t)\,dt \quad\Rightarrow\quad s = s_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 a_c t^2 \]

Velocity as a function of position. From \(v\,dv = a_c\,ds\):

\[ \int_{v_0}^{v} v\,dv = \int_{s_0}^{s} a_c\,ds \quad\Rightarrow\quad v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a_c(s - s_0) \]

Constant acceleration

\[ v = v_0 + a_c t \qquad\qquad s = s_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 a_c t^2 \qquad\qquad v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a_c(s - s_0) \]

Valid only when the acceleration is constant. Each equation leaves out one quantity: the first has no \(s\), the second no \(v\), the third no \(t\). Pick the one without the quantity you neither know nor need.

Only for constant acceleration

These equations are the most misused in dynamics. If the acceleration depends on time, velocity or position (a rocket burning fuel, a car with air drag, a mass on a spring), they give wrong answers. Lesson 5 handles those cases by going back to \(a = dv/dt\) and \(a\,ds = v\,dv\).

Braking with a reaction time

A driver who sees a hazard needs a moment to react before the brakes act. During that reaction time the car keeps its speed; after it, the car slows at a constant rate until it stops. Each stage has constant acceleration (zero, then \(a_c\)), so treat them one after the other: the end of the first stage is the start of the second.

Example 4.1 — Stopping distance

A car travels at \(90\ \text{km/h}\). The driver needs \(0.75\ \text{s}\) to react, and the brakes then decelerate the car at \(6\ \text{m/s}^2\). Find the stopping distance and the time to stop.

Show solution

Convert the speed: \(90\ \text{km/h} = 90/3.6 = 25\ \text{m/s}\). Take the direction of travel as positive.

Reaction: \(a = 0\), so \(s_1 = v_0 t_r = 25(0.75) = 18.75\ \text{m}\).

Braking: \(a_c = -6\ \text{m/s}^2\) from \(25\ \text{m/s}\) to \(0\). The time is not needed for the distance, so use the equation without \(t\):

\[ 0 = 25^2 + 2(-6)\,\Delta s_2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta s_2 = \frac{625}{12} = 52.08\ \text{m} \] \[ s_T = 18.75 + 52.08 = 70.8\ \text{m} \qquad\qquad t = 0.75 + \frac{25}{6} = 4.92\ \text{s} \]

Set \(v_0 = 25\), the deceleration to 6 and the reaction time to 0.75 in Figure 4.1 to see it.

Example 4.2 — Acceleration from two measurements

A car accelerates uniformly. It passes marker \(A\) at \(15\ \text{m/s}\) and marker \(B\), \(100\ \text{m}\) farther on, at \(25\ \text{m/s}\). Find its acceleration and the time between the markers.

Show solution

The time is unknown, so start with the equation without \(t\):

\[ 25^2 = 15^2 + 2a_c(100) \quad\Rightarrow\quad a_c = \frac{625 - 225}{200} = 2.00\ \text{m/s}^2 \] \[ t = \frac{v - v_0}{a_c} = \frac{25 - 15}{2} = 5.00\ \text{s} \]

Check with the second equation: \(15(5) + \tfrac12(2)(25) = 75 + 25 = 100\ \text{m}\).

Free fall

Near the Earth's surface, and when air resistance is small, every object falls with the same constant acceleration \(g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2\), directed downward. For vertical motion, measure the height \(y\) upward from a fixed origin. Then \(a_c = -g\), and the three equations become

Free fall (up positive)

\[ v = v_0 - g t \qquad\qquad y = y_0 + v_0 t - \tfrac12 g t^2 \qquad\qquad v^2 = v_0^2 - 2g(y - y_0) \]

The acceleration is \(-g\) at every instant: on the way up, at the top (where \(v = 0\)) and on the way down.

At the top of its flight a ball has zero velocity, but its acceleration is still \(-9.81\ \text{m/s}^2\). If the acceleration were zero there, the ball would stay at the top forever.

Example 4.3 — A ball thrown from a cliff

A ball is thrown straight up at \(12\ \text{m/s}\) from the edge of a cliff \(20\ \text{m}\) high, and falls past the edge to the ground below. Find (a) the greatest height above the ground, (b) the time to reach the ground, and (c) the velocity just before it lands.

Show solution

Origin at the ground, \(y\) up: \(y_0 = 20\ \text{m}\), \(v_0 = 12\ \text{m/s}\), \(a_c = -9.81\ \text{m/s}^2\).

(a) At the top \(v = 0\):

\[ 0 = 12^2 - 2(9.81)(y_{\max} - 20) \quad\Rightarrow\quad y_{\max} = 20 + \frac{144}{19.62} = 27.3\ \text{m} \]

(b) At the ground \(y = 0\):

\[ 0 = 20 + 12t - 4.905t^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad t = \frac{12 + \sqrt{12^2 + 4(4.905)(20)}}{2(4.905)} = 3.58\ \text{s} \]

The other root is negative and has no meaning here.

(c) \(v = 12 - 9.81(3.584) = -23.2\ \text{m/s}\), that is, \(23.2\ \text{m/s}\) downward. Check: \(v^2 = 12^2 + 2(9.81)(20) = 536.4\), and \(\sqrt{536.4} = 23.2\).

Key takeaways

  • For constant acceleration only: \(v = v_0 + a_c t\), \(s = s_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 a_c t^2\), \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a_c(s - s_0)\).
  • Choose the equation that leaves out the quantity you neither know nor need.
  • Split a motion into stages of constant acceleration; the end of one stage is the start of the next.
  • Free fall: \(a = -g = -9.81\ \text{m/s}^2\) with up positive, at every instant, including at the top.
  • Next, Lesson 5 handles accelerations that depend on time, velocity or position.
