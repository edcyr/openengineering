Relative Motion Along a Line

From the window of a moving train, a train on the next track seems to crawl past even though both are moving fast. Motion looks different from a moving observer, and often it is simpler. This lesson describes the motion of one particle relative to another moving along the same line: the relative position, velocity and acceleration, and how they solve overtaking and elevator problems.

Learning objectives

  • Define the position, velocity and acceleration of particle \(B\) relative to particle \(A\).
  • Relate absolute and relative motion: \(s_B = s_A + s_{B/A}\), and the same for \(v\) and \(a\).
  • Solve overtaking and meeting problems in the frame of one of the particles.
  • Analyze motion inside a moving or accelerating elevator, and relative motion within a pulley system.

Relative position, velocity and acceleration

Let particles \(A\) and \(B\) move along the same straight line (or along parallel lines), with absolute positions \(s_A\) and \(s_B\) measured from the same fixed origin \(O\) and in the same positive direction. The relative position of \(B\) with respect to \(A\), written \(s_{B/A}\) and read "B relative to A", is where \(B\) is as seen by an observer riding on \(A\):

Relative motion along a line

\[ s_B = s_A + s_{B/A} \qquad\qquad v_B = v_A + v_{B/A} \qquad\qquad a_B = a_A + a_{B/A} \]

The second and third equations come from differentiating the first. \(s_{B/A}\) is positive when \(B\) is ahead of \(A\) in the positive direction.

The observer on \(A\) uses a frame of reference that translates with \(A\): its origin moves with \(A\), and its axis stays parallel to the fixed one. In that frame \(A\) is at rest, the ground slides backward, and \(B\) moves with the relative velocity \(v_{B/A} = v_B - v_A\). Swapping the roles changes only the sign: \(v_{A/B} = -v_{B/A}\).

Example 8.1 — Overtaking

Car \(A\) cruises at a steady \(20\ \text{m/s}\). Car \(B\) is \(60\ \text{m}\) behind it in the next lane, moving at \(15\ \text{m/s}\), when it starts to accelerate at \(2\ \text{m/s}^2\). When does \(B\) draw level with \(A\), and how fast is it then moving relative to \(A\)?

Show solution

Work in the frame of \(A\). The relative motion has constant acceleration, so the equations of Lesson 4 apply to it:

\[ (s_{B/A})_0 = -60\ \text{m} \qquad (v_{B/A})_0 = 15 - 20 = -5\ \text{m/s} \qquad a_{B/A} = 2 - 0 = 2\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

\(B\) is level with \(A\) when \(s_{B/A} = 0\):

\[ 0 = -60 - 5t + \tfrac12(2)t^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad t^2 - 5t - 60 = 0 \]\[ t = \frac{5 + \sqrt{25 + 240}}{2} = 10.6\ \text{s} \] \[ v_{B/A} = -5 + 2(10.64) = 16.3\ \text{m/s} \]

At first \(B\) falls farther behind (\(v_{B/A} < 0\)); after \(2.5\ \text{s}\) it starts to gain. Meanwhile \(A\) has traveled \(20(10.64) = 213\ \text{m}\). Choose "car A" in Figure 8.1 to watch it from \(A\).

Inside an elevator

An elevator is a moving frame. Anything that happens inside it, such as a dropped coin or a ball tossed up, is easiest to describe relative to the elevator floor. Gravity gives the coin an absolute acceleration of \(-g\); relative to the elevator it is \(a_{C/E} = a_C - a_E = -g - a_E\). If the elevator moves at a constant velocity, \(a_E = 0\) and everything inside happens exactly as on the ground.

Example 8.2 — A coin dropped in an elevator

A passenger drops a coin from \(1.5\ \text{m}\) above the floor of an elevator. How long does it take to reach the floor (a) if the elevator rises at a constant \(2\ \text{m/s}\), and (b) if it rises while accelerating upward at \(1.2\ \text{m/s}^2\)?

Show solution

Up positive, relative to the floor: the coin starts at \(s_{C/E} = 1.5\ \text{m}\) at rest relative to the elevator, because it was in the passenger's hand.

(a) \(a_{C/E} = -9.81 - 0 = -9.81\ \text{m/s}^2\):

\[ 0 = 1.5 - \tfrac12(9.81)t^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad t = \sqrt{\frac{2(1.5)}{9.81}} = 0.553\ \text{s} \]

the same as in a building at rest. Seen from the ground, the coin starts moving up at \(2\ \text{m/s}\), rises a little and falls, ending \(0.394\ \text{m}\) below its release point; the floor rises to meet it.

(b) \(a_{C/E} = -9.81 - 1.2 = -11.01\ \text{m/s}^2\):

\[ t = \sqrt{\frac{2(1.5)}{11.01}} = 0.522\ \text{s} \]

The floor accelerates up toward the coin, so it lands sooner, as if gravity were stronger.

Relative motion with cords

Relative motion combines naturally with the dependent motion of Lesson 7. Find the absolute velocities from the cord equations, then subtract. In a block and tackle with two parts of rope, pulling the free end \(A\) down at \(v_A\) raises the load at \(v_B = -v_A/2\), so the rope end moves relative to the load at

\[ v_{A/B} = v_A - v_B = v_A + \tfrac12 v_A = \tfrac32 v_A \]

Example 8.3 — Rope relative to the load

The free end of a two-part tackle is pulled down at \(1.2\ \text{m/s}\). Find the velocity of the free end relative to the load.

Show solution

Down positive. From \(2v_B + v_A = 0\): \(v_B = -0.6\ \text{m/s}\) (up). Then

\[ v_{A/B} = v_A - v_B = 1.2 - (-0.6) = 1.80\ \text{m/s} \]

A worker standing on the rising load would see the rope end move down past them at \(1.8\ \text{m/s}\).

The same relations hold in two and three dimensions, with every quantity a vector: \(\vvec_B = \vvec_A + \vvec_{B/A}\). Relative motion in the plane, such as a boat crossing a river with a current, is treated with the motion of systems of particles.

Key takeaways

  • \(s_B = s_A + s_{B/A}\), \(v_B = v_A + v_{B/A}\), \(a_B = a_A + a_{B/A}\), with all positions from the same fixed origin and the same positive direction.
  • In the frame of \(A\), \(A\) is at rest and \(B\) moves with the relative motion, often a simpler one.
  • Overtaking: set \(s_{B/A} = 0\). A relative motion with constant \(a_{B/A}\) uses the equations of Lesson 4.
  • In an elevator with constant velocity, everything happens as on the ground; an accelerating elevator changes the relative acceleration to \(-g - a_E\).
  • This completes the kinematics of rectilinear motion. Review with the Practice Lab, the Self-Check Quiz and the Motion Lab.
