Absolute Dependent Motion

A crane lifts a load with a cable that runs around several pulleys; an elevator is balanced by a counterweight on the same cable. The particles in such a system cannot move independently: a cord of fixed length ties their positions together, so the motion of one decides the motion of the others. This lesson shows how to write that tie as an equation between position coordinates and differentiate it to relate the velocities and accelerations.

Learning objectives

  • Choose position coordinates measured from fixed datums along each particle's line of motion.
  • Write the total length of a cord as a sum of segments in terms of those coordinates.
  • Differentiate the length equation to relate velocities and accelerations, and interpret the signs.
  • Handle several cords, and a cord whose length depends on the coordinates in a way that is not linear.

The procedure

The motions are dependent when the particles are connected by inextensible cords. The method is called absolute because every position is measured from a fixed point, not from another moving particle. Follow the same steps every time:

  1. Position coordinates. Give each particle a coordinate \(s\), measured from a fixed datum (a fixed pulley axle, a ceiling) along the particle's own line of motion. Choose the positive direction for each.
  2. Total length. Write the length of each cord, \(l_T\), as the sum of its segments in terms of the coordinates. Segments that wrap around pulleys or never change length add only a constant; leave them out, or lump them into \(l_T\).
  3. Differentiate. \(l_T\) is constant, so its time derivative is zero. Differentiating once relates the velocities; differentiating again relates the accelerations.

One cord, straight segments

\[ \sum_i k_i\,s_i = l_T \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sum_i k_i\,v_i = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sum_i k_i\,a_i = 0 \]

\(k_i\) counts the segments of the cord whose length changes with \(s_i\). A negative answer means the particle moves opposite to its own positive direction.

A block and tackle

In a block and tackle, a load hangs from movable pulleys held by several parts of one cord, which runs over fixed pulleys to a free end. Measure everything down from the ceiling: \(s_B\) to the load and \(s_A\) to the free end. If \(n\) parts of the cord hold the load, each of their lengths is \(s_B\) plus a constant, and the free part is \(s_A\) plus a constant, so

\[ n\,s_B + s_A = l_T \quad\Rightarrow\quad n\,v_B + v_A = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v_B = -\frac{v_A}{n} \]

Pull the free end down a meter and the load rises \(1/n\) meter. That is why a tackle with more parts lifts a heavier load with the same pull: the work done by the pull is spread over a longer stroke.

Example 7.1 — Three parts of cord

The free end \(A\) of the tackle in Figure 7.1, with three parts holding the load, is pulled down at \(1.5\ \text{m/s}\) and is speeding up at \(0.6\ \text{m/s}^2\). Find the velocity and acceleration of the load \(B\).

Show solution

Down is positive for both coordinates. Three segments change with \(s_B\), one with \(s_A\):

\[ 3s_B + s_A = l_T \quad\Rightarrow\quad 3v_B + v_A = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v_B = -\frac{1.5}{3} = -0.5\ \text{m/s} \] \[ 3a_B + a_A = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad a_B = -\frac{0.6}{3} = -0.2\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

The minus signs mean upward: the load rises at \(0.5\ \text{m/s}\) and speeds up at \(0.2\ \text{m/s}^2\).

Two cords

Each cord gives its own length equation. A system with two cords has two equations, so if you know the motions of all but two of the particles, the equations give those two.

In Figure 7.2, cord 1 runs from block \(A\) over the fixed pulley \(D\) to the axle of a movable pulley \(P\); cord 2 runs over \(P\) from block \(B\) to block \(C\). With every coordinate measured down from \(D\)'s axle, cord 2 has two segments, of lengths \(s_B - s_P\) and \(s_C - s_P\):

\[ \text{cord 1:}\ \ s_A + s_P = l_1 \qquad\qquad \text{cord 2:}\ \ (s_B - s_P) + (s_C - s_P) = l_2 \] \[ v_A + v_P = 0 \qquad\qquad v_B + v_C - 2v_P = 0 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad v_B + v_C + 2v_A = 0 \]

Example 7.2 — Two cords

In Figure 7.2, block \(A\) moves down at \(0.8\ \text{m/s}\) and block \(B\) moves up at \(0.4\ \text{m/s}\). Find the velocities of the pulley \(P\) and of block \(C\).

Show solution

Down positive: \(v_A = 0.8\ \text{m/s}\), \(v_B = -0.4\ \text{m/s}\).

\[ v_P = -v_A = -0.8\ \text{m/s} \qquad\qquad v_C = 2v_P - v_B = 2(-0.8) - (-0.4) = -1.2\ \text{m/s} \]

\(P\) rises at \(0.8\ \text{m/s}\) and \(C\) rises at \(1.2\ \text{m/s}\). Check with the combined equation: \(-0.4 - 1.2 + 2(0.8) = 0\).

A cord at an angle

When a segment of the cord is not along the particle's line of motion, its length is not a simple sum of coordinates. In Figure 7.3 a truck at \(A\) pulls a cord over a pulley at height \(h\) to lift a crate \(B\). The vertical segment has length \(h - y_B\) (the crate's top measured up from the ground), and the slanted segment, by Pythagoras, \(\sqrt{x_A^2 + h^2}\):

\[ l_T = (h - y_B) + \sqrt{x_A^2 + h^2} \]

Differentiate with the chain rule of Lesson 1, remembering that \(x_A\) changes with time:

Truck and crate

\[ 0 = -v_B + \frac{x_A\,v_A}{\sqrt{x_A^2 + h^2}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad v_B = \frac{x_A\,v_A}{\sqrt{x_A^2 + h^2}} \]

The ratio of the speeds changes with position. Even with the truck at a steady speed, the crate accelerates.

Example 7.3 — The crate speeds up

In Figure 7.3, \(h = 8\ \text{m}\). When \(x_A = 6\ \text{m}\), the truck moves away at a constant \(2\ \text{m/s}\). Find the velocity and acceleration of the crate.

Show solution

The slanted cord is \(\sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10\ \text{m}\) long.

\[ v_B = \frac{6(2)}{10} = 1.20\ \text{m/s} \]

Differentiate \(v_B = x_A v_A/\sqrt{x_A^2 + h^2}\) again with the quotient rule, with \(a_A = 0\) and \(\dot x_A = v_A\):

\[ a_B = \frac{v_A^2\sqrt{x_A^2 + h^2} - x_A v_A\,\dfrac{x_A v_A}{\sqrt{x_A^2 + h^2}}}{x_A^2 + h^2} = \frac{v_A^2\,h^2}{(x_A^2 + h^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{4(64)}{1000} = 0.256\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

The crate speeds up upward at \(0.256\ \text{m/s}^2\), although the truck does not accelerate.

Check your understanding

In questions 2 and 3, positions are measured down from the ceiling, so down is positive.

Key takeaways

  • Measure every position from a fixed datum, along the particle's own line of motion.
  • Write each cord's total length in the coordinates and differentiate: \(\sum k_i s_i = l_T\) gives \(\sum k_i v_i = 0\) and \(\sum k_i a_i = 0\).
  • A load held by \(n\) parts of a cord moves at \(1/n\) of the free end's speed.
  • Each cord gives one equation. A slanted segment gives a relation that changes with position, and accelerations even when the driving speed is steady.
