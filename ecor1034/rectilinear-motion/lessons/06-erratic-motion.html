Erratic Motion: The Graphical Method

A car in city traffic speeds up, cruises, brakes and stops again. No single formula describes its whole trip, but a graph does: data from a GPS, a speed sensor or an accelerometer usually comes as a graph, made of pieces. When the motion changes from one stage to the next it is called erratic, and the graphical method handles it with two ideas from calculus: the slope of a graph is a derivative, and the area under it is an integral.

Learning objectives

  • Build the \(v\)–\(t\) graph from the slopes of the \(s\)–\(t\) graph, and the \(a\)–\(t\) graph from the slopes of the \(v\)–\(t\) graph.
  • Find changes in velocity from areas under the \(a\)–\(t\) graph, and displacements from areas under the \(v\)–\(t\) graph.
  • Work through a motion made of stages, using the end of each stage as the start of the next.
  • Use the \(a\)–\(s\) graph to find velocities from \(\tfrac12(v_2^2 - v_1^2) = \int a\,ds\).

Slopes and areas

The kinematic relations of Lesson 3 read directly as statements about graphs. Going down the chain from position to acceleration, take slopes. Going up the chain, add areas.

Reading the graphs

\[ \begin{aligned} v &= \frac{ds}{dt} && \text{slope of the \(s\)–\(t\) graph} \\[10pt] a &= \frac{dv}{dt} && \text{slope of the \(v\)–\(t\) graph} \\[8pt] \Delta s &= \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v\,dt && \text{area under the \(v\)–\(t\) graph} \\[6pt] \Delta v &= \int_{t_1}^{t_2} a\,dt && \text{area under the \(a\)–\(t\) graph} \end{aligned} \]

Areas below the axis are negative, as in Lesson 2.

The graphs are linked by shape, too. Where the \(a\)–\(t\) graph is a constant, the \(v\)–\(t\) graph is a straight line and the \(s\)–\(t\) graph a parabola. Where \(a = 0\), \(v\) is constant and \(s\) a straight line. Where \(v = 0\), the \(s\)–\(t\) graph has a level tangent: a peak, a valley or a pause. And because the position changes smoothly, the \(s\)–\(t\) graph never jumps, even where the acceleration does.

Example 6.1 — From the s–t graph down

A cyclist starts from rest. For the first \(10\ \text{s}\) the \(s\)–\(t\) graph is the parabola \(s = 0.3t^2\); from \(t = 10\) to \(30\ \text{s}\) it is the straight line \(s = 6t - 30\) (\(s\) in meters, \(t\) in seconds). Draw the \(v\)–\(t\) and \(a\)–\(t\) graphs.

Show solution

Take the slope of each piece.

\[ 0 \le t \le 10\ \text{s}:\quad v = \frac{ds}{dt} = 0.6t,\quad a = \frac{dv}{dt} = 0.6\ \text{m/s}^2 \] \[ 10 \le t \le 30\ \text{s}:\quad v = 6\ \text{m/s},\quad a = 0 \]

The \(v\)–\(t\) graph rises in a straight line from 0 to \(6\ \text{m/s}\) and then stays level; the \(a\)–\(t\) graph is a step from \(0.6\ \text{m/s}^2\) down to zero at \(t = 10\ \text{s}\). The two pieces of \(s\) join smoothly: both give \(s = 30\ \text{m}\) and \(v = 6\ \text{m/s}\) at \(t = 10\ \text{s}\). At \(t = 30\ \text{s}\) the cyclist is at \(s = 150\ \text{m}\).

Example 6.2 — From the v–t graph

A car starts from rest, accelerates uniformly to \(20\ \text{m/s}\) in \(10\ \text{s}\), cruises for \(20\ \text{s}\), then brakes uniformly to a stop in another \(10\ \text{s}\). Find its acceleration in each stage and the total distance. This is the starting motion of Figure 6.1.

Show solution

Slopes of the \(v\)–\(t\) graph:

\[ a_1 = \frac{20 - 0}{10} = 2\ \text{m/s}^2 \qquad a_2 = 0 \qquad a_3 = \frac{0 - 20}{10} = -2\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

The area under the \(v\)–\(t\) graph is a trapezoid made of two triangles and a rectangle:

\[ \Delta s = \tfrac12(10)(20) + (20)(20) + \tfrac12(10)(20) = 100 + 400 + 100 = 600\ \text{m} \]

The car is at \(100\ \text{m}\) after \(10\ \text{s}\), at \(500\ \text{m}\) after \(30\ \text{s}\), and stops at \(600\ \text{m}\).

Example 6.3 — From the a–t graph up

A rocket sled starts from rest with \(a = 4\ \text{m/s}^2\) for \(5\ \text{s}\), then brakes with \(a = -2\ \text{m/s}^2\) until it stops. When does it stop, and how far has it gone?

Show solution

Area under the \(a\)–\(t\) graph for the first stage: \(\Delta v = 4(5) = 20\ \text{m/s}\), so \(v = 20\ \text{m/s}\) at \(t = 5\ \text{s}\). Braking removes \(2\ \text{m/s}\) each second, so it takes \(10\ \text{s}\) more: the sled stops at \(t = 15\ \text{s}\).

The \(v\)–\(t\) graph is a triangle with base \(15\ \text{s}\) and height \(20\ \text{m/s}\); its area is the distance:

\[ \Delta s = \tfrac12(15)(20) = 150\ \text{m} \]

The triangle splits at \(t = 5\ \text{s}\): \(\tfrac12(5)(20) = 50\ \text{m}\) while speeding up and \(\tfrac12(10)(20) = 100\ \text{m}\) while braking. The \(s\)–\(t\) graph is two parabolas that join smoothly at \(s = 50\ \text{m}\): one curving up, one curving down and leveling off at \(150\ \text{m}\).

The a–s graph

When the acceleration is known as a function of position, for example from markers along a test track, integrate \(a\,ds = v\,dv\) between two positions:

Velocity from the a–s graph

\[ \tfrac12\left(v_2^2 - v_1^2\right) = \int_{s_1}^{s_2} a\,ds = \text{area under the } a\text{–}s \text{ graph} \]

The area gives the change in \(\tfrac12 v^2\), not in \(v\). Find \(v_2 = \sqrt{v_1^2 + 2(\text{area})}\).

The reverse also works: on a \(v\)–\(s\) graph, the acceleration at a point is \(a = v\,dv/ds\), the velocity there times the slope of the graph.

Example 6.4 — A test track

A car starts from rest. Its acceleration is \(2\ \text{m/s}^2\) for the first \(50\ \text{m}\), then decreases linearly to zero at \(s = 100\ \text{m}\). Find its speed at \(s = 100\ \text{m}\).

Show solution

The area under the \(a\)–\(s\) graph from 0 to \(100\ \text{m}\) is a rectangle plus a triangle:

\[ \int_0^{100} a\,ds = 2(50) + \tfrac12(50)(2) = 150\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 \] \[ \tfrac12 v^2 - 0 = 150 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{300} = 17.3\ \text{m/s} \]

This is the starting graph of Figure 6.2: move the position slider to \(100\ \text{m}\) to read it.

Key takeaways

  • Down the chain, take slopes: \(v\) is the slope of \(s\)–\(t\), and \(a\) the slope of \(v\)–\(t\).
  • Up the chain, add areas: \(\Delta s\) is the area under \(v\)–\(t\), and \(\Delta v\) the area under \(a\)–\(t\). Areas below the axis count as negative.
  • Constant \(a\): straight \(v\)–\(t\), parabolic \(s\)–\(t\). Zero \(a\): level \(v\)–\(t\), straight \(s\)–\(t\).
  • The area under the \(a\)–\(s\) graph is \(\tfrac12(v_2^2 - v_1^2)\).
