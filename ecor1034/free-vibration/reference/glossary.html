A

Amplitude \(C\)
The largest displacement from equilibrium. The body swings between \(-C\) and \(+C\). In free vibration it is set by the initial conditions: \(C = \sqrt{x_0^2 + (\dot x_0/\omega_n)^2}\).
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 4Related: Period \(\tau\), Phase angle \(\phi\), Initial conditions

C

Back to top
Circular frequency \(\omega\)
The rate at which the reference-circle vector turns, in rad/s: \(\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi/\tau\). It is the coefficient of \(t\) in \(\sin(\omega t + \phi)\).
See: Lesson 1Related: Frequency \(f\), Natural frequency \(\omega_n\), Reference circle
Conservation of energy
With no friction or damping, the kinetic energy plus the potential energy stays constant during a free vibration: \(\tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 kx^2 = \tfrac12 kC^2\).
See: Lesson 8Related: Kinetic energy \(T\), Potential energy \(V\), Energy method
Cord tension (pendulum)
From the normal equation of a pendulum: \(T = mg\cos\theta + ml\dot\theta^2\). It is largest at the bottom of the swing.
See: Lesson 7, Lesson 7Related: Simple pendulum, Normal and tangential components
Cycle
One complete repetition of a periodic motion, for example from one extreme to the other and back. It takes one period.
See: Lesson 1Related: Period \(\tau\), Periodic motion

D

Back to top
Damping
Anything that removes energy from a vibration, such as friction, air resistance or a shock absorber, so that the motion dies out. This module treats undamped vibration.
See: Lesson 1Related: Undamped, Free vibration
Degree of freedom
An independent coordinate needed to fix the position of a system. A single-degree-of-freedom system, such as a block on a spring or a pendulum, needs only one: \(x\) or \(\theta\).
See: Lesson 1Related: Simple pendulum, Spring–mass system
Dot notation \(\dot x,\ \ddot x\)
A dot means a derivative with respect to time: \(\dot x = dx/dt\) is the velocity, \(\ddot x = d^2x/dt^2\) the acceleration.
See: Lesson 2Related: Largest speed and acceleration, \(\ddot x = -\omega^2 x\)

E

Back to top
Energy method
A way to find the equation of motion, and so \(\omega_n\), without a free-body diagram: write \(T + V\) in one coordinate and set \(d(T + V)/dt = 0\).
See: Lesson 8Related: Conservation of energy, Natural frequency \(\omega_n\)
Equation of motion
Newton\'s second law written for the system in its coordinate. For a spring–mass system, \(m\ddot x + kx = 0\); for a simple pendulum, \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\sin\theta = 0\).
See: Lesson 3, Lesson 7Related: Spring–mass system, \(\ddot x = -\omega^2 x\), Simple pendulum
Equilibrium position
The position where the system can stay at rest, because the forces on it balance. Displacements in vibration are measured from it, so constant forces drop out.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 3, Lesson 5Related: Restoring force, Static deflection \(\delta_{st}\)
Equivalent stiffness \(k_{eq}\)
The stiffness of the single spring that would push back on the mass with the same force for the same displacement as a set of springs.
See: Lesson 6Related: Springs in parallel, Springs in series

F

Back to top
Free vibration
A vibration that continues on its own after the system is displaced or struck; no force keeps driving it.
See: Lesson 1Related: Undamped, Natural frequency \(\omega_n\)
Frequency \(f\)
The number of cycles per second, in hertz: \(f = 1/\tau = \omega/2\pi\).
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 3Related: Period \(\tau\), Circular frequency \(\omega\)

G

Back to top
General solution
Every motion that satisfies \(m\ddot x + kx = 0\): \(x = A\sin\omega_n t + B\cos\omega_n t = C\sin(\omega_n t + \phi)\). Its two constants are fixed by the initial conditions.
See: Lesson 3, Lesson 4Related: Initial conditions, Time response
Guess and check
Solving an equation of motion by trying a function and substituting it. For \(m\ddot x + kx = 0\), only a sine or cosine with \(p = \sqrt{k/m}\) works.
See: Lesson 3Related: General solution, Natural frequency \(\omega_n\)

I

Back to top
Independence of the amplitude
The period of a linear spring–mass system, or of a pendulum swinging through small angles, is the same for every amplitude.
See: Lesson 3, Lesson 7Related: Period \(\tau\), Small-angle approximation
Initial conditions
The displacement \(x_0\) and the velocity \(\dot x_0\) at \(t = 0\). They fix the constants of the solution: \(B = x_0\), \(A = \dot x_0/\omega_n\).
See: Lesson 4Related: General solution, Amplitude \(C\), Phase angle \(\phi\)

K

Back to top
Kinetic energy \(T\)
\(T = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2\); for a pendulum, \(\tfrac12 ml^2\dot\theta^2\). Largest at equilibrium, zero at the extremes.
See: Lesson 8Related: Potential energy \(V\), Conservation of energy

L

Back to top
Largest speed and acceleration
In simple harmonic motion, \(v_{\max} = C\omega\), reached at equilibrium, and \(a_{\max} = C\omega^2\), reached at the extremes.
See: Lesson 2Related: Amplitude \(C\), Circular frequency \(\omega\)

N

Back to top
Natural frequency \(\omega_n\)
The circular frequency at which a system vibrates on its own: \(\sqrt{k/m}\) for a spring–mass system, \(\sqrt{g/\delta_{st}}\) from the static deflection, \(\sqrt{g/l}\) for a pendulum in small swings.
See: Lesson 3, Lesson 5, Lesson 7Related: Circular frequency \(\omega\), Period \(\tau\), Equivalent stiffness \(k_{eq}\)
Normal and tangential components
Components along the path (\(t\)) and toward its center of curvature (\(n\)). For a pendulum, \(a_t = l\ddot\theta\) and \(a_n = l\dot\theta^2\).
See: Lesson 7Related: Simple pendulum, Cord tension (pendulum)

O

Back to top
Oscillation
Another word for a vibration: a repeated motion back and forth about a position of equilibrium.
See: Lesson 1Related: Vibration, Periodic motion

P

Back to top
Period \(\tau\)
The time for one cycle, in seconds: \(\tau = 1/f = 2\pi/\omega\). For a spring–mass system, \(\tau = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\).
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 3Related: Frequency \(f\), Cycle, Independence of the amplitude
Periodic motion
A motion that repeats itself exactly after a fixed time, the period.
See: Lesson 1Related: Cycle, Period \(\tau\)
Phase angle \(\phi\)
The angle in \(x = C\sin(\omega t + \phi)\) that sets where the motion starts: \(x(0) = C\sin\phi\). From initial conditions, \(\sin\phi = B/C\) and \(\cos\phi = A/C\); check the quadrant.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 4Related: Amplitude \(C\), Reference circle
Potential energy \(V\)
Energy stored by position: \(\tfrac12 kx^2\) in a spring (with \(x\) from equilibrium, also for a vertical spring), \(mgl(1 - \cos\theta)\) for a pendulum.
See: Lesson 8, Lesson 8Related: Kinetic energy \(T\), Conservation of energy

R

Back to top
Reference circle
A circle of radius \(C\) around which a point turns at \(\omega\). The projection of the point moves in simple harmonic motion, which explains the sine, the period and the phase angle.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 4Related: Simple harmonic motion, Phase angle \(\phi\)
Restoring force
A force that pushes a displaced body back toward equilibrium, such as a spring force or part of a pendulum\'s weight. Without one there is no vibration.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 3Related: Spring force \(F_s\), Equilibrium position

S

Back to top
Simple harmonic motion
A vibration whose displacement is a sinusoid in time, \(x = C\sin(\omega t + \phi)\); equivalently, one with \(\ddot x = -\omega^2 x\).
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 2Related: Reference circle, \(\ddot x = -\omega^2 x\)
Simple pendulum
A particle (the bob) hanging on a light, inextensible cord from a fixed point. For small swings its period is \(2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\), independent of the mass.
See: Lesson 7Related: Small-angle approximation, Cord tension (pendulum), Normal and tangential components
Small-angle approximation
\(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) for small angles in radians. It turns the pendulum\'s equation into simple harmonic motion; the period is within 1% up to about \(20^\circ\).
See: Lesson 7, Lesson 7Related: Simple pendulum, Independence of the amplitude
Spring force \(F_s\)
The force of a linear spring, proportional to its stretch or compression: \(kx\) for a horizontal spring, \(k(\delta_{st} + y)\) for a vertical one with \(y\) from static equilibrium.
See: Lesson 3, Lesson 5Related: Stiffness \(k\), Restoring force
Spring–mass system
A block of mass \(m\) held by a spring of stiffness \(k\): the basic model of vibration. Its equation of motion is \(m\ddot x + kx = 0\).
See: Lesson 3Related: Natural frequency \(\omega_n\), Equation of motion
Springs in parallel
Springs that share the same displacement, so their forces add: \(k_{eq} = k_1 + k_2\). This includes springs on opposite sides of a mass.
See: Lesson 6Related: Equivalent stiffness \(k_{eq}\), Springs in series
Springs in series
Springs joined end to end that carry the same force, so their deflections add: \(1/k_{eq} = 1/k_1 + 1/k_2\). Softer than either spring.
See: Lesson 6Related: Equivalent stiffness \(k_{eq}\), Springs in parallel
Static deflection \(\delta_{st}\)
How far a spring stretches, or a support sags, under a weight at rest: \(k\,\delta_{st} = mg\). Then \(\omega_n = \sqrt{g/\delta_{st}}\).
See: Lesson 5, Lesson 5Related: Equilibrium position, Natural frequency \(\omega_n\)
Stiffness \(k\)
The force per unit deflection of a linear spring, in N/m. A stiffer spring makes a system vibrate faster.
See: Lesson 3Related: Spring force \(F_s\), Equivalent stiffness \(k_{eq}\)

T

Back to top
Time response
The displacement as a function of time for a given start: \(x = (\dot x_0/\omega_n)\sin\omega_n t + x_0\cos\omega_n t\).
See: Lesson 4Related: Initial conditions, General solution

U

Back to top
Undamped
Without damping: no energy is lost, and a free vibration keeps the same amplitude forever. A good model for the frequency of many lightly damped systems.
See: Lesson 1Related: Damping, Conservation of energy

V

Back to top
Vibration
A repeated motion back and forth about a position of equilibrium, driven by a restoring force.
See: Lesson 1Related: Oscillation, Restoring force, Free vibration

X

Back to top
\(\ddot x = -\omega^2 x\)
The property that defines simple harmonic motion: the acceleration is proportional to the displacement and opposite to it. Any system whose equation of motion has this form vibrates harmonically, with \(\omega^2\) the constant.
See: Lesson 2, Lesson 3Related: Simple harmonic motion, Equation of motion
