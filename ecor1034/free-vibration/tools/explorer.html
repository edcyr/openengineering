Things to try

  1. Amplitude does not matter. Release the block from \(20\ \text{mm}\), then from \(150\ \text{mm}\): the period readout does not move.
  2. Struck, not pulled. Start the block at equilibrium with a velocity of \(1\ \text{m/s}\): the response is a sine, and \(C = \dot x_0/\omega_n\).
  3. Series versus parallel. Two equal springs end to end give a quarter of the stiffness, and twice the period, of the same springs side by side.
  4. A soft hanging spring. A large static deflection means a slow vibration: \(\omega_n = \sqrt{g/\delta_{st}}\).
  5. A big swing. Release the pendulum from \(150^\circ\): it lingers near the top, and its period is 76% longer than \(2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\).
  6. Over the top. Give the pendulum enough speed at the bottom and it goes all the way round: no longer a vibration.
  7. A phase angle in the second quadrant. Start with a positive displacement and a negative velocity: \(\phi\) is between \(90^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\).

How the lab works

The spring systems are solved exactly: \(x = (\dot x_0/\omega_n)\sin\omega_n t + x_0\cos\omega_n t\), with \(\omega_n = \sqrt{k_{eq}/m}\) (Lesson 4). The pendulum follows the full equation \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\sin\theta = 0\), integrated in thousands of small time steps with the fourth-order Runge–Kutta method; its dashed twin follows the small-angle model (Lesson 7). The energy bars use \(T = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2\) with \(V = \tfrac12 k_{eq}x^2\), or \(V = mgl(1 - \cos\theta)\) for the pendulum (Lesson 8).
