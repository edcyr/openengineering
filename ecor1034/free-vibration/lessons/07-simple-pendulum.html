The Simple Pendulum

A pendulum has no spring: its restoring force is part of its own weight. Newton's second law gives an equation that is not quite the simple harmonic one, because of a sine. For small swings the sine is very nearly its angle, and the pendulum becomes a harmonic oscillator whose period depends only on its length. This lesson derives that result, and shows how small "small" has to be.

Learning objectives

  • Draw the free-body diagram of a simple pendulum and write its equation of motion in normal and tangential components.
  • Apply the small-angle approximation \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\) to obtain \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\,\theta = 0\).
  • Find the natural frequency and the period, \(\tau = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\), and explain why they do not depend on the mass.
  • Judge when the small-angle model is accurate enough.

The model and its free-body diagram

A simple pendulum is a particle, the bob, of mass \(m\), hanging from a fixed point \(O\) on a light, inextensible cord of length \(l\). Its position is fixed by one coordinate, the angle \(\theta\) between the cord and the vertical. The bob moves on a circle of radius \(l\), so use normal and tangential components: \(t\) along the path in the direction of increasing \(\theta\), \(n\) along the cord toward \(O\).

Two forces act on the bob: the cord tension \(T\), along \(n\), and the weight \(W = mg\), straight down. The weight has a tangential component \(mg\sin\theta\) that points back toward the bottom, and a normal component \(mg\cos\theta\) away from \(O\).

The bob's tangential acceleration is \(a_t = l\ddot\theta\) and its normal acceleration \(a_n = l\dot\theta^2\). Newton's second law:

\[ \Sigma F_t = m a_t: \quad -mg\sin\theta = m l\ddot\theta \qquad\qquad \Sigma F_n = m a_n: \quad T - mg\cos\theta = m l\dot\theta^2 \]

The tangential equation governs the motion; the normal one then gives the tension. Divide the tangential one by \(ml\):

Equation of motion of a simple pendulum

\[ \ddot\theta + \frac{g}{l}\sin\theta = 0 \]

The mass has cancelled: a heavier bob feels a larger restoring force, but needs a larger force to accelerate by the same amount. This equation is not \(\ddot\theta = -\omega^2\theta\): the restoring term is proportional to \(\sin\theta\), not to \(\theta\).

Small swings: the small-angle approximation

For small angles in radians, \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\): at \(10^\circ = 0.1745\ \text{rad}\), \(\sin\theta = 0.1736\), only half a percent less. Replacing \(\sin\theta\) by \(\theta\) gives the equation of simple harmonic motion:

\[ \ddot\theta + \frac{g}{l}\,\theta = 0 \]

Simple pendulum, small swings

\[ \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{g}{l}} \qquad \tau = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}} \qquad \theta = A\sin\omega_n t + B\cos\omega_n t \]

with \(B = \theta_0\) and \(A = \dot\theta_0/\omega_n\), exactly as in Lesson 4.

The period depends only on the length and on \(g\): not on the mass, and, for small swings, not on the amplitude either. A pendulum that swings a little less as it loses energy keeps the same period, which is why a pendulum clock keeps good time.

How small is small?

The real pendulum swings slower than the small-angle model, because \(\sin\theta < \theta\): its restoring force is a little weaker than the model's. The period of the full equation can be computed exactly [3]; the table compares it with \(2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\).

Worked examples

Example 7.1 — The period of a pendulum

Find the period of a simple pendulum \(0.8\ \text{m}\) long swinging through small angles.

Show solution
\[ \tau = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.8}{9.81}} = 1.79\ \text{s} \]

Example 7.2 — A pendulum that beats seconds

A clock pendulum should take exactly one second for each swing from one side to the other. How long must it be?

Show solution

One swing is half a cycle, so \(\tau = 2\ \text{s}\):

\[ l = g\left(\frac{\tau}{2\pi}\right)^2 = 9.81\left(\frac{2}{2\pi}\right)^2 = 0.994\ \text{m} \]

Example 7.3 — Measuring \(g\)

A student times 20 small swings (20 cycles) of a pendulum \(0.550\ \text{m}\) long at \(30.0\ \text{s}\). What value of \(g\) does this give?

Show solution
\[ \tau = \frac{30.0}{20} = 1.50\ \text{s} \qquad g = \frac{4\pi^2 l}{\tau^2} = \frac{4\pi^2(0.550)}{1.50^2} = 9.65\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

Timing many cycles reduces the effect of the reaction time at the start and the end.

Example 7.4 — The tension at the bottom

A \(0.5\ \text{kg}\) bob on a \(1.2\ \text{m}\) cord is released from rest at \(\theta_0 = 15^\circ\). Use the small-angle model to find its largest angular velocity, then the cord tension as the bob passes the bottom.

Show solution
\[ \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{9.81}{1.2}} = 2.859\ \text{rad/s} \qquad \dot\theta_{\max} = \theta_0\,\omega_n = 0.2618(2.859) = 0.749\ \text{rad/s} \]

At the bottom \(\theta = 0\), so the normal equation gives

\[ T = mg + ml\dot\theta^2 = 0.5(9.81) + 0.5(1.2)(0.749)^2 = 4.91 + 0.34 = 5.24\ \text{N} \]

The cord pulls a little more than the weight at the bottom, because the bob's path curves up there.

Key takeaways

  • Tangential equation: \(-mg\sin\theta = ml\ddot\theta\), so \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\sin\theta = 0\); the normal equation gives the tension.
  • For small angles, \(\sin\theta \approx \theta\): \(\omega_n = \sqrt{g/l}\), \(\tau = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\), independent of the mass and the amplitude.
  • Within 1% up to about \(20^\circ\); larger swings take longer than the small-angle period.
