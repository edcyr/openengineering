Energy in Free Vibration

In an undamped vibration no energy is lost: it passes back and forth between kinetic energy, largest at equilibrium, and potential energy, largest at the extremes. Following the energy gives the largest speed or the amplitude in one line, and gives a second way to find a natural frequency, often quicker than drawing a free-body diagram.

Learning objectives

  • Write the kinetic and potential energy of a spring–mass system and of a pendulum, and explain how they exchange during a cycle.
  • Use conservation of energy to find the largest speed, the amplitude, or the speed at a given position.
  • Find the equation of motion and the natural frequency by setting \(\dfrac{d}{dt}(T + V) = 0\).

Kinetic and potential energy

For a block of mass \(m\) on a spring of stiffness \(k\), with \(x\) measured from equilibrium:

\[ \colKE{T = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2} \qquad \colVe{V = \tfrac12 k x^2} \]

The spring force and the weight are conservative and nothing else does work, so the total stays constant: \(T + V = \text{constant}\). In simple harmonic motion, \(x = C\sin(\omega_n t + \phi)\) and \(\dot x = C\omega_n\cos(\omega_n t + \phi)\), so

\[ T + V = \tfrac12 m C^2\omega_n^2\cos^2(\omega_n t + \phi) + \tfrac12 k C^2\sin^2(\omega_n t + \phi) = \tfrac12 kC^2 \]

since \(m\omega_n^2 = k\) and \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\).

Energy of a free vibration

\[ \tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 k x^2 = \tfrac12 kC^2 = \tfrac12 m\,v_{\max}^2 \]

All potential at the extremes, all kinetic at equilibrium. The total grows as the square of the amplitude.

Using conservation of energy

Example 8.1 — Largest speed and speed on the way

A \(2\ \text{kg}\) block on a spring with \(k = 800\ \text{N/m}\) is pulled \(50\ \text{mm}\) from equilibrium and released from rest. Find the energy of the vibration, the block's largest speed, and its speed \(30\ \text{mm}\) from equilibrium.

Show solution
\[ E = \tfrac12 kC^2 = \tfrac12(800)(0.05)^2 = 1.00\ \text{J} \qquad \tfrac12(2)v_{\max}^2 = 1.00 \;\Rightarrow\; v_{\max} = 1.00\ \text{m/s} \]

At \(x = 0.03\ \text{m}\):

\[ \tfrac12(2)\dot x^2 + \tfrac12(800)(0.03)^2 = 1.00 \;\Rightarrow\; \dot x^2 = 0.64 \;\Rightarrow\; |\dot x| = 0.800\ \text{m/s} \]

The same as \(\omega_n\sqrt{C^2 - x^2} = 20\sqrt{0.0025 - 0.0009}\) from Lesson 2: the two are the same statement.

Example 8.2 — Amplitude from a general start

The same block starts \(30\ \text{mm}\) from equilibrium, moving at \(0.6\ \text{m/s}\). Find the amplitude.

Show solution
\[ E = \tfrac12(2)(0.6)^2 + \tfrac12(800)(0.03)^2 = 0.36 + 0.36 = 0.720\ \text{J} \qquad C = \sqrt{\frac{2E}{k}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.44}{800}} = 0.0424\ \text{m} \]

Lesson 4's formula agrees: \(\sqrt{0.03^2 + (0.6/20)^2} = 0.0424\ \text{m}\).

The pendulum's energy

For a simple pendulum, the bob moves at \(l\dot\theta\) and rises \(l(1 - \cos\theta)\) above its lowest point:

\[ \colKE{T = \tfrac12 m l^2\dot\theta^2} \qquad \colVg{V = mgl(1 - \cos\theta)} \]

Released from rest at \(\theta_0\), it reaches the bottom with \(\tfrac12 m v^2 = mgl(1 - \cos\theta_0)\), at any amplitude.

Example 8.3 — Speed at the bottom

A pendulum \(1.2\ \text{m}\) long is released from rest at \(10^\circ\). Find the bob's speed at the bottom, exactly by energy and with the small-angle model.

Show solution
\[ \text{Energy:}\quad v = \sqrt{2gl(1 - \cos\theta_0)} = \sqrt{2(9.81)(1.2)(1 - \cos 10^\circ)} = 0.598\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \text{Small angles:}\quad v = l\,\dot\theta_{\max} = l\,\theta_0\,\omega_n = 1.2(0.1745)\sqrt{\frac{9.81}{1.2}} = 0.599\ \text{m/s} \]

At \(10^\circ\) the two differ by about one part in a thousand.

The energy method for the natural frequency

Since \(T + V\) is constant, its time derivative is zero. Differentiating gives the equation of motion directly, without a free-body diagram:

Energy method

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\left(T + V\right) = 0 \]

Write \(T\) and \(V\) in terms of one coordinate, differentiate (with the chain rule), and cancel the common velocity factor.

For the spring–mass system: \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 kx^2\right) = m\dot x\ddot x + kx\dot x = \dot x\,(m\ddot x + kx) = 0\). The velocity is not always zero, so \(m\ddot x + kx = 0\): Lesson 3's equation.

For the pendulum: \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\left[\tfrac12 ml^2\dot\theta^2 + mgl(1 - \cos\theta)\right] = ml^2\dot\theta\ddot\theta + mgl\sin\theta\,\dot\theta = 0\), so \(\ddot\theta + (g/l)\sin\theta = 0\): Lesson 7's equation, without finding the tension.

Show why a vertical spring has \(V = \tfrac12 ky^2\)

With \(y\) measured down from the static equilibrium position, the spring's stretch is \(\delta_{st} + y\) and the weight is \(y\) below its datum:

\[ V = \tfrac12 k(\delta_{st} + y)^2 - mgy = \tfrac12 k\delta_{st}^2 + (k\delta_{st} - mg)\,y + \tfrac12 ky^2 = \text{constant} + \tfrac12 ky^2 \]

since \(k\delta_{st} = mg\). A constant does not change \(d(T + V)/dt\): measured from static equilibrium, the weight and the spring together act like the spring alone, as Lesson 5 found.

Example 8.4 — A collar between two springs

A \(1.5\ \text{kg}\) collar slides on a smooth horizontal rod between two springs, \(k_1 = 250\ \text{N/m}\) and \(k_2 = 350\ \text{N/m}\), attached to the ends of the rod. Use the energy method to find the natural frequency.

Show solution

When the collar is \(x\) from equilibrium, one spring is stretched and the other compressed by \(x\):

\[ T + V = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 k_1 x^2 + \tfrac12 k_2 x^2 \] \[ \frac{d}{dt}(T + V) = \dot x\left[m\ddot x + (k_1 + k_2)\,x\right] = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k_1 + k_2}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{600}{1.5}} = 20.0\ \text{rad/s} \]

The springs act in parallel, as Lesson 6 predicted.

Check your understanding

Key takeaways

  • Spring–mass: \(T = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2\), \(V = \tfrac12 kx^2\), \(T + V = \tfrac12 kC^2 = \tfrac12 m v_{\max}^2\).
  • Pendulum: \(T = \tfrac12 ml^2\dot\theta^2\), \(V = mgl(1 - \cos\theta)\).
  • \(d(T + V)/dt = 0\) gives the equation of motion, hence \(\omega_n\), without a free-body diagram.
  • You can now model a free, undamped vibration, find its natural frequency, and predict its motion from any start. Test yourself in the Practice Lab and the Self-Check Quiz.
