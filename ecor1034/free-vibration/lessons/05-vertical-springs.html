Lesson 5 · 30 min

Vertical Springs and Static Deflection

Hang a block from a spring and gravity joins in: the spring stretches until it holds the weight, and the block then vibrates about that new position. This lesson shows that the weight shifts the equilibrium position but leaves the natural frequency unchanged, and that the natural frequency can be found from the static stretch alone.

Learning objectives

  • Find the static deflection \(\delta_{st}\) of a spring that supports a weight.
  • Write the equation of motion of a block on a vertical spring, with \(y\) measured from the static equilibrium position, and show that the weight cancels.
  • Find the natural frequency from the static deflection: \(\omega_n = \sqrt{g/\delta_{st}}\).
  • Find the largest and smallest spring forces during the vibration.

The static equilibrium position

A spring of stiffness \(k\) hangs from a ceiling. Attach a block of mass \(m\) and let it settle. The spring stretches by the static deflection \(\delta_{st}\), until its pull balances the weight:

Static deflection

\[ k\,\delta_{st} = W = mg \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \delta_{st} = \frac{mg}{k} \]

This is the static equilibrium position: the block can rest there. Pull it down and let go, and it vibrates about this position, not about the end of the unstretched spring.

The equation of motion

Measure the displacement \(y\) from the static equilibrium position, positive downward. When the block is at \(y\), the spring is stretched by \(\delta_{st} + y\) and pulls up with \(\colSpr{F_s = k(\delta_{st} + y)}\); the weight \(W = mg\) acts down. Newton's second law, down positive:

\[ +\!\downarrow \Sigma F_y = m a_y: \qquad mg - k(\delta_{st} + y) = m\ddot y \]

Since \(k\,\delta_{st} = mg\), the first two terms cancel:

Vertical spring–mass system

\[ m\ddot y + ky = 0 \qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \]

The same equation, and the same natural frequency, as the horizontal system of Lesson 3.

The weight is balanced by the static part of the spring force, \(k\,\delta_{st}\), at every instant; only the extra stretch \(y\) produces a net force. This is why the displacement of a vibrating system is always measured from its static equilibrium position: constant forces then drop out of the equation of motion.

Natural frequency from the static deflection

Divide \(k\,\delta_{st} = mg\) by \(m\,\delta_{st}\): \(k/m = g/\delta_{st}\). So

Natural frequency from the static deflection

\[ \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta_{st}}} \qquad f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta_{st}}} \]

Neither the mass nor the stiffness is needed: measure how far the support sags under the load, and the natural frequency follows. A soft mount (large sag) gives a low natural frequency; a stiff one (small sag) a high one.

Example 5.1 — From the static stretch

A \(3\ \text{kg}\) block stretches a spring \(40\ \text{mm}\) when it hangs at rest. Find the spring's stiffness, and the natural frequency and frequency of the block's vibration.

Show solution
\[ k = \frac{mg}{\delta_{st}} = \frac{3(9.81)}{0.040} = 736\ \text{N/m} \] \[ \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta_{st}}} = \sqrt{\frac{9.81}{0.040}} = 15.7\ \text{rad/s} \qquad f_n = \frac{15.66}{2\pi} = 2.49\ \text{Hz} \]

Check: \(\sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{735.75/3} = 15.7\ \text{rad/s}\).

Example 5.2 — The spring force during the vibration

The block of Example 5.1 is pulled \(30\ \text{mm}\) below its equilibrium position and released from rest. Write \(y(t)\), and find the largest and smallest forces in the spring.

Show solution
\[ y = 0.030\cos 15.66t\ \text{m} \]

The spring is stretched by \(\delta_{st} + y\), which varies between \(0.040 - 0.030 = 0.010\ \text{m}\) and \(0.040 + 0.030 = 0.070\ \text{m}\):

\[ F_{\max} = 735.75(0.070) = 51.5\ \text{N} \qquad F_{\min} = 735.75(0.010) = 7.36\ \text{N} \]

The weight is \(29.4\ \text{N}\): the spring force swings \(22.1\ \text{N}\) above and below it. If the amplitude were larger than \(\delta_{st}\), the spring would be compressed near the top of each cycle; a spring that cannot push, such as a rubber cord, would go slack, and the motion would no longer be simple harmonic.

Example 5.3 — A car on its suspension

When a car is lowered off a lift, its body sinks \(120\ \text{mm}\) on its springs. Estimate the natural frequency of the body bouncing on the suspension.

Show solution
\[ \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{9.81}{0.120}} = 9.04\ \text{rad/s} \qquad f_n = \frac{9.04}{2\pi} = 1.44\ \text{Hz} \]

A frequency between about 1 and 1.5 Hz is typical of a passenger car's body. The real suspension also has dampers, which make the bouncing die out after a cycle or two.

Key takeaways

  • Static deflection: \(k\,\delta_{st} = mg\).
  • Measured from the static equilibrium position, \(m\ddot y + ky = 0\): gravity shifts the equilibrium but not the frequency.
  • \(\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{g/\delta_{st}}\).
  • The spring force is \(k(\delta_{st} + y)\); it stays a pull only while the amplitude is less than \(\delta_{st}\).
