Springs in Parallel and in Series

Real systems are often held by more than one spring: a machine on four mounts, a vehicle on its tires and suspension springs, a mass between two springs on opposite sides. When the springs move with a single coordinate, they can be replaced by one equivalent spring, and the natural frequency follows from Lesson 3. This lesson finds the equivalent stiffness for springs in parallel and in series, and shows how to tell the two apart.

Learning objectives

  • Define the equivalent stiffness of a set of springs.
  • Show that springs in parallel add, \(k_{eq} = k_1 + k_2\), and springs in series add as reciprocals, \(1/k_{eq} = 1/k_1 + 1/k_2\).
  • Decide whether springs act in parallel or in series from how they share the deflection and the force, not from how they look.
  • Find the natural frequency of a mass supported by a combination of springs.

The equivalent spring

The equivalent stiffness \(k_{eq}\) of a set of springs is the stiffness of the single spring that would push back on the mass with the same force for the same displacement: \(F = k_{eq}\,x\). Once \(k_{eq}\) is known, the system is a spring–mass system and

\[ \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k_{eq}}{m}} \]

To find \(k_{eq}\), ask two questions: do the springs share the displacement, or do they share the force? [2]

Springs in parallel: the same displacement

Two springs that both connect the mass to the support, side by side, are stretched by the same amount \(x\) when the mass moves. Each pulls with its own force, and the forces add:

\[ F = k_1 x + k_2 x = (k_1 + k_2)\,x \]

Springs in parallel

\[ k_{eq} = k_1 + k_2 + \cdots \]

Parallel springs share the displacement; the stiffer combination is stiffer than any one spring.

Springs on opposite sides of a mass are also in parallel, though they do not look it. Move the mass a distance \(x\) to the right: the left spring stretches by \(x\) and pulls left with \(k_1 x\), the right spring compresses by \(x\) and pushes left with \(k_2 x\). The same displacement, and forces that add: \(k_{eq} = k_1 + k_2\).

Springs in series: the same force

Two springs joined end to end, with the mass at the far end, carry the same force \(F\) (a light connector between them has no mass, so the forces on it must balance). Each stretches by its own amount, and the stretches add:

\[ x = x_1 + x_2 = \frac{F}{k_1} + \frac{F}{k_2} = F\left(\frac{1}{k_1} + \frac{1}{k_2}\right) \]

Springs in series

\[ \frac{1}{k_{eq}} = \frac{1}{k_1} + \frac{1}{k_2} + \cdots \qquad\text{for two springs:}\quad k_{eq} = \frac{k_1 k_2}{k_1 + k_2} \]

Series springs share the force; the combination is softer than either spring.

The softer spring takes the larger part of the deflection: \(x_1/x_2 = k_2/k_1\). In Figure 6.1, watch the connector between two series springs: it moves less than the mass, by the fraction \(k_{eq}/k_1\).

Worked examples

Example 6.1 — Parallel and series

A \(2\ \text{kg}\) block is supported by two springs, \(k_1 = 300\ \text{N/m}\) and \(k_2 = 500\ \text{N/m}\). Find its natural frequency when the springs are (a) side by side, (b) end to end.

Show solution

(a) Parallel:

\[ k_{eq} = 300 + 500 = 800\ \text{N/m} \qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{800}{2}} = 20.0\ \text{rad/s} \qquad f_n = 3.18\ \text{Hz} \]

(b) Series:

\[ k_{eq} = \frac{300(500)}{300 + 500} = 187.5\ \text{N/m} \qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{187.5}{2}} = 9.68\ \text{rad/s} \qquad \tau = 0.649\ \text{s} \]

The same two springs give natural frequencies that differ by a factor of two.

Example 6.2 — A combination

A \(4\ \text{kg}\) mass hangs from a light bar supported by two springs of \(400\ \text{N/m}\) side by side; the bar hangs from a single spring of \(800\ \text{N/m}\) attached to the ceiling. Find the natural frequency of the mass.

Show solution

Work from the inside out. The two \(400\ \text{N/m}\) springs are in parallel:

\[ k_p = 400 + 400 = 800\ \text{N/m} \]

That pair is in series with the \(800\ \text{N/m}\) spring, since the same force passes through both:

\[ k_{eq} = \frac{800(800)}{800 + 800} = 400\ \text{N/m} \qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{400}{4}} = 10.0\ \text{rad/s} \]

As Lesson 5 showed, hanging the system vertically changes the equilibrium position, not the frequency.

Example 6.3 — Cutting a spring

A spring of stiffness \(k\) is cut into two equal halves. What is the stiffness of each half?

Show solution

Think of the whole spring as its two halves in series, each with stiffness \(k_h\):

\[ \frac{1}{k} = \frac{1}{k_h} + \frac{1}{k_h} = \frac{2}{k_h} \quad\Rightarrow\quad k_h = 2k \]

Each half is twice as stiff: under the same force it stretches half as much. A block on one half vibrates \(\sqrt2\) times faster than on the whole spring.

Key takeaways

  • Same displacement, forces add: parallel, \(k_{eq} = k_1 + k_2\). Springs on opposite sides of a mass are in parallel.
  • Same force, deflections add: series, \(1/k_{eq} = 1/k_1 + 1/k_2\); softer than either spring.
  • Reduce a combination step by step from the inside out, then \(\omega_n = \sqrt{k_{eq}/m}\).
