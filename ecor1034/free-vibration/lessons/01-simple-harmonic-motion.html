Oscillations and Simple Harmonic Motion

A guitar string, a car bouncing on its springs, a swinging pendulum and a tall building swaying in the wind all move back and forth about a position of equilibrium. This lesson names the quantities that describe such a motion (the period, the frequency and the amplitude) and introduces the simplest and most important oscillation of all, simple harmonic motion, through a point turning around a circle.

Learning objectives

  • Describe a vibration as a motion about a position of equilibrium, driven by a restoring force.
  • Define the period, the frequency, the circular frequency and the amplitude of an oscillation, and convert between them.
  • Write a simple harmonic motion as \(x = C\sin(\omega t + \phi)\) and explain each symbol with the reference circle.
  • Find the displacement at a given time, and write \(x(t)\) from a description of the motion.

What is a vibration?

A body is in equilibrium when the forces on it balance. Displace it slightly, and in many systems a restoring force appears that pushes it back toward equilibrium: a stretched spring pulls, a pendulum's weight swings it down, a plucked string's tension straightens it. The body arrives back at equilibrium moving, overshoots, is pushed back again, and keeps going back and forth. This repeated motion about a position of equilibrium is a vibration, or oscillation.

Vibrations matter in engineering in two ways. Some are wanted: a clock's pendulum, a loudspeaker cone, a vibrating screen that sorts gravel. Many are not: an engine that shakes its mounts, a bridge deck that sways under footsteps, a machine part that cracks from fatigue after millions of cycles. Either way, the first question an engineer asks is how fast the system vibrates on its own.

This module studies the simplest case:

  • Single degree of freedom. One coordinate, such as the displacement \(x\) of a block or the angle \(\theta\) of a pendulum, fixes the position of the whole system.
  • Free vibration. The system is displaced or struck and then left alone; no force keeps driving it.
  • Undamped. No friction or air resistance takes energy away, so the motion goes on forever with the same size. Real vibrations die out slowly; for many systems the undamped model still predicts the frequency very well.

Describing a periodic motion

A motion that repeats itself exactly is periodic. One complete repetition, for example from the far right to the far left and back to the far right, is a cycle.

Period, frequency and amplitude

  • The period \(\tau\) is the time for one cycle, in seconds.
  • The frequency \(f\) is the number of cycles per second, in hertz (\(1\ \text{Hz} = 1\ \text{cycle/s}\)): \[ f = \frac{1}{\tau} \]
  • The amplitude \(C\) is the largest displacement from equilibrium. The body swings between \(x = -C\) and \(x = +C\), so the total travel from one extreme to the other is \(2C\).

Example 1.1 — Period and frequency

(a) A guitar string sounds the note A at \(440\ \text{Hz}\). What is its period? (b) A car bounces on its suspension through 5 complete cycles in \(4\ \text{s}\). What are its period and frequency?

Show solution

(a) \(\tau = 1/f = 1/440 = 2.27 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{s}\), or \(2.27\ \text{ms}\).

(b) \(\tau = 4/5 = 0.800\ \text{s}\), and \(f = 1/\tau = 1.25\ \text{Hz}\).

The two differ by a factor of about 350, yet both are described by the same few quantities.

Simple harmonic motion and the reference circle

Many vibrations trace out a sine wave in time. Such a motion is simple harmonic motion (SHM):

Simple harmonic motion

\[ x = C\sin(\omega t + \phi) \]

\(C\) is the amplitude, \(\omega\) the circular frequency in rad/s, and \(\phi\) the phase angle in radians, which sets where the motion is at \(t = 0\).

A sine function has a clear geometric meaning. Picture a point \(P\) moving around a circle of radius \(C\) at a constant angular speed \(\omega\). Its angle from the horizontal axis is \(\omega t + \phi\), and its height above the horizontal axis is \(C\sin(\omega t + \phi)\). The shadow of \(P\) on the vertical axis therefore moves in simple harmonic motion. This circle is the reference circle: the oscillating body moves like the projection of a point going steadily around it.

The reference circle explains the units. One cycle of the motion is one turn of the vector, \(2\pi\) radians, so the vector turns through \(2\pi\) radians in one period:

Circular frequency

\[ \omega = \frac{2\pi}{\tau} = 2\pi f \qquad \tau = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} \qquad f = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} \]

Keep \(\omega\) (rad/s) and \(f\) (Hz) apart: they differ by the factor \(2\pi\), and mixing them up is the most common slip in vibration problems. Always use radians in \(\sin(\omega t + \phi)\).

The phase angle

At \(t = 0\), \(x = C\sin\phi\). The phase angle therefore records where the motion starts:

  • \(\phi = 0\): the body starts at equilibrium, moving in the positive direction, and \(x = C\sin\omega t\).
  • \(\phi = \pi/2\) (\(90^\circ\)): the body starts at the positive extreme, \(x = C\) and at rest; since \(\sin(\omega t + \pi/2) = \cos\omega t\), the motion is \(x = C\cos\omega t\).
  • \(\phi = \pi\) or \(-\pi/2\): the body starts at equilibrium moving in the negative direction, or at the negative extreme.

Changing \(\phi\) only slides the same sine wave along the time axis. Try it in Figure 1.1.

Example 1.2 — Reading a harmonic motion

A particle moves with \(x = 0.05\sin(8t + 0.6)\), with \(x\) in meters and \(t\) in seconds. Find its amplitude, circular frequency, frequency and period, and its position at \(t = 0\) and at \(t = 1\ \text{s}\).

Show solution

Compare with \(x = C\sin(\omega t + \phi)\): \(C = 0.05\ \text{m}\), \(\omega = 8\ \text{rad/s}\) and \(\phi = 0.6\ \text{rad}\).

\[ f = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{8}{2\pi} = 1.27\ \text{Hz} \qquad \tau = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = 0.785\ \text{s} \] \[ x(0) = 0.05\sin 0.6 = 0.0282\ \text{m} \qquad x(1) = 0.05\sin 8.6 = 0.0367\ \text{m} \]

The angle \(8.6\ \text{rad}\) is more than one turn: the particle has completed one cycle and is part way through the second.

Example 1.3 — Writing the motion from a description

A small part vibrates with an amplitude of \(30\ \text{mm}\) and completes 12 cycles in \(5\ \text{s}\). At \(t = 0\) it passes through equilibrium, moving in the positive direction. Write \(x(t)\).

Show solution
\[ f = \frac{12}{5} = 2.4\ \text{Hz} \qquad \omega = 2\pi f = 15.08\ \text{rad/s} \]

At equilibrium and moving in the positive direction at \(t = 0\): \(\phi = 0\). So

\[ x = 0.030\sin(15.08\,t)\ \text{m} \]

Key takeaways

  • A vibration is a motion back and forth about equilibrium, driven by a restoring force. This module treats free, undamped vibration with one degree of freedom.
  • Period \(\tau\) (s), frequency \(f = 1/\tau\) (Hz), circular frequency \(\omega = 2\pi f\) (rad/s), amplitude \(C\).
  • Simple harmonic motion: \(x = C\sin(\omega t + \phi)\), the projection of a point turning steadily around a circle of radius \(C\).
  • The phase angle \(\phi\) sets the starting point: \(x(0) = C\sin\phi\).
