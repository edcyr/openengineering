Plastic Impact and Impulsive Forces

When two bodies collide, the forces between them are enormous and last a few milliseconds. You never need their size or their history: they are internal to the pair, so the momentum of the pair is the same just before and just after. If the bodies stick together, that is all it takes to find how they move off, and how much kinetic energy the impact destroys.

Learning objectives

  • Find the common velocity after a plastic impact from conservation of momentum.
  • Find the kinetic energy lost in a plastic impact, and explain why it is the energy of the relative motion.
  • Tell impulsive forces from nonimpulsive ones, and keep only the impulsive ones in an impact.
  • Split a problem into an impact (momentum) and the motion before or after it (energy), as in the ballistic pendulum.

Plastic impact

In a plastic impact the colliding bodies do not separate: they move off with a common velocity \(\vvec_2\). Railroad cars that couple, a bullet that embeds in a block, two lumps of clay. During the brief impact the contact forces are internal to the pair; if the external forces give no impulse in that time, the momentum is conserved:

Plastic impact

\[ m_A(\vvec_A)_1 + m_B(\vvec_B)_1 = (m_A + m_B)\,\vvec_2 \qquad\qquad \Delta T_{\text{lost}} = \tfrac12\,\frac{m_A m_B}{m_A + m_B}\,\lvert(\vvec_A)_1 - (\vvec_B)_1\rvert^2 \]

The subscript 1 marks velocities just before the impact, and 2 just after. The lost kinetic energy becomes heat, sound and permanent deformation.

Where does the loss formula come from? Lesson 5 split the kinetic energy into \(\tfrac12 mv_G^2\), that of the center of mass, and the energy of the motion relative to it. The impact cannot change \(\vvec_G\), so it keeps \(\tfrac12 mv_G^2\). After a plastic impact there is no relative motion left, so the impact destroyed all of the rest, which for two particles works out to the formula above: it depends only on how fast they approach each other.

Example 7.1 — Railroad cars that couple

A \(15\ \text{Mg}\) railroad car rolling at \(2\ \text{m/s}\) strikes a \(10\ \text{Mg}\) car at rest, and they couple. Find their velocity after coupling, and the kinetic energy lost.

Show solution
\[ 15(2) + 10(0) = (15 + 10)v_2 \;\Rightarrow\; v_2 = 1.20\ \text{m/s} \]

Before: \(T_1 = \tfrac12(15\,000)(2^2) = 30.0\ \text{kJ}\). After: \(T_2 = \tfrac12(25\,000)(1.2^2) = 18.0\ \text{kJ}\). The loss is \(12.0\ \text{kJ}\), \(40\%\). From the formula:

\[ \Delta T_{\text{lost}} = \tfrac12\,\frac{(15)(10)}{25}(10^3)(2 - 0)^2 = 12.0\ \text{kJ} \]

The rails, gravity and the cars' rolling resistance act during the impact too, but they are ordinary, finite forces: over the fraction of a second the coupling lasts, their impulse is negligible next to that of the coupler.

The ballistic pendulum

A bullet fired into a block that hangs on cords is the classic way to measure a bullet's speed. It is two problems in a row. The impact is plastic and brief: during it the block hardly moves, the cords stay vertical and pull only vertically, so the horizontal momentum of bullet and block is conserved. Energy is not: almost all of it is lost. The swing that follows is slow and smooth: the cords do no work, so \(T + V\) is conserved, and the height reached gives the speed just after the impact.

Example 7.2 — The bullet's speed from the swing

In Figure 7.1 the block, with the bullet in it, swings up until the cords make \(25^\circ\) with the vertical. Find the bullet's speed, and the fraction of its kinetic energy lost in the impact.

Show solution

Swing (energy): \(h = 1.5(1 - \cos 25^\circ) = 0.1405\ \text{m}\), and \(\tfrac12(m + M)v_2^2 = (m + M)gh\):

\[ v_2 = \sqrt{2(9.81)(0.1405)} = 1.661\ \text{m/s} \]

Impact (momentum, horizontally):

\[ 0.02\,v_1 = (4.02)(1.661) \;\Rightarrow\; v_1 = 334\ \text{m/s} \]

Kinetic energy: \(\tfrac12(0.02)(334^2) = 1114\ \text{J}\) before the impact, \(\tfrac12(4.02)(1.661^2) = 5.54\ \text{J}\) after. The impact destroys \(99.5\%\) of it, the fraction \(M/(m + M)\). Using energy across the impact would be wrong by a factor of 200.

Impulsive forces

An impulsive force is one that is very large and acts for a very short time \(\Delta t\), yet gives a finite impulse: the contact force in a collision, the jerk of a cord that snaps taut, the push of a rigid stop. A nonimpulsive force stays finite: a weight, a spring, or friction from a normal force that is itself finite. Over a time \(\Delta t \to 0\), a finite force gives no impulse. That justifies three working rules for impulsive forces:

  1. Include only the impulsive forces in the impulse-momentum equations for the instant of the impact; leave out the nonimpulsive ones.
  2. The positions do not change during the impact (they would move \(v\,\Delta t \to 0\)); the velocities change abruptly.
  3. A support, a stop or a cord that prevents a motion can exert an impulsive reaction. Momentum is conserved only in the directions in which no external impulsive force acts.

In the ballistic pendulum the cords are vertical, so their pull is not impulsive horizontally; had the bullet struck a block resting against a wall, the wall's reaction would have been. A slack cord that suddenly comes taut is an impulsive force of its own: it acts like a plastic impact, through a cord.

Example 7.3 — The cord jerks taut

In Figure 7.2, \(m_A = 3\ \text{kg}\) slides at \(4\ \text{m/s}\) and \(m_B = 1\ \text{kg}\) is at rest. The cord is inextensible and does not rebound. Find the velocity of the blocks after the jerk, the impulse the cord gives \(B\), and the energy lost.

Show solution

The cord's tension is internal and impulsive; the floor and the weights are vertical. So the horizontal momentum is conserved, and after the jerk the blocks move together:

\[ 3(4) + 1(0) = (3 + 1)v_2 \;\Rightarrow\; v_2 = 3\ \text{m/s} \]

For \(B\) alone the cord is external: \(\int T\,dt = m_B v_2 - 0 = 3\ \text{N·s}\), and \(A\) receives \(-3\ \text{N·s}\).

\[ \Delta T_{\text{lost}} = \tfrac12(3)(4^2) - \tfrac12(4)(3^2) = 24 - 18 = 6\ \text{J} \]

The same as \(\tfrac12\frac{(3)(1)}{4}(4^2) = 6\ \text{J}\): the cord made the blocks' relative motion stop, as a plastic impact would.

Key takeaways

  • Plastic impact: \(m_A(\vvec_A)_1 + m_B(\vvec_B)_1 = (m_A + m_B)\vvec_2\); the bodies move off together.
  • It loses \(\tfrac12\frac{m_A m_B}{m_A + m_B}\lvert(\vvec_A)_1 - (\vvec_B)_1\rvert^2\): all the energy of the relative motion.
  • In an impact, keep impulsive forces (contacts, stops, cords that snap taut) and drop nonimpulsive ones (weights, springs, ordinary friction).
  • Never use energy across an impact; use momentum across it and energy before and after it.
