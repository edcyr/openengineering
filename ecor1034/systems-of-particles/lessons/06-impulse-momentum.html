Impulse and Momentum

Integrate \(\sum\Fvec = m\avec_G\) over time and you get the principle of impulse and momentum for a system. Internal forces drop out again, so whenever the external forces give no impulse, in all directions or in one, the total momentum cannot change. That single fact settles recoils, throws, bursts and collisions without knowing anything about the forces in between.

Learning objectives

  • Find the linear momentum of a system, \(\Lvec = \sum m_i\vvec_i = m\vvec_G\).
  • Apply the principle of linear impulse and momentum, \(\Lvec_1 + \sum\int\Fvec\,dt = \Lvec_2\), with the impulse of the external forces only.
  • Recognize when momentum is conserved, in every direction or only in one, and use it with relative velocities.
  • Combine conservation of momentum with the work-energy principle when a problem has two unknown velocities.

The principle for a system

The linear momentum of a particle is \(\Lvec = m\vvec\), and that of a system is the sum over its particles, which by Lesson 4 is the total mass times the velocity of the center of mass:

\[ \Lvec = \sum_i m_i\vvec_i = m\vvec_G \]

Differentiate: \(\dot\Lvec = m\avec_G = \sum\Fvec\). Multiply by \(dt\) and integrate from \(t_1\) to \(t_2\). The time integral of a force is its linear impulse, \(\Ivec = \int\Fvec\,dt\).

Principle of linear impulse and momentum for a system

\[ \Lvec_1 + \textstyle\sum\displaystyle\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\Fvec\,dt = \Lvec_2 \]

\(\sum\int\Fvec\,dt\) is the impulse of the external forces. Each internal pair gives equal and opposite impulses on its two particles, because the two forces act for the same time, so the internal impulses cancel. In components: \((L_x)_1 + \sum\int F_x\,dt = (L_x)_2\), and the same for \(y\).

If the external forces give no impulse, the momentum is conserved:

Conservation of linear momentum

\[ \sum\int\Fvec\,dt = \mathbf 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Lvec_1 = \Lvec_2, \qquad \sum_i m_i(\vvec_i)_1 = \sum_i m_i(\vvec_i)_2 \]

It may hold in one direction only: \(L_x\) is conserved when \(\sum\int F_x\,dt = 0\), whatever the vertical forces do.

Pushed apart by a spring

Two carts at rest on a smooth track are pushed together, compressing a spring between them, and released. The spring is internal, the track's push and the weights are vertical, so the horizontal momentum stays zero: the carts move off with equal and opposite momenta. The spring's energy, by the work-energy principle of Lesson 5, sets how fast.

Example 6.1 — The speeds after release

In Figure 6.1, \(m_A = 2\ \text{kg}\) and \(m_B = 3\ \text{kg}\). Find the speed of each cart after the spring has reached its free length.

Show solution

Momentum, horizontally, positive to the right: \(0 = 2v_A + 3v_B\), so \(v_B = -\tfrac23 v_A\). Energy, for the carts and the spring:

\[ \tfrac12(600)(0.1^2) = \tfrac12(2)v_A^2 + \tfrac12(3)v_B^2 \;\Rightarrow\; 3 = v_A^2 + 1.5\left(\tfrac49 v_A^2\right) = 1.667v_A^2 \] \[ v_A = -1.34\ \text{m/s (left)} \qquad v_B = 0.894\ \text{m/s (right)} \]

The kinetic energies are not equal: \(1.8\ \text{J}\) for \(A\) and \(1.2\ \text{J}\) for \(B\). With equal momenta, the lighter cart takes the larger share of the energy.

Momentum with relative velocities

Throws, jumps and launches are often described relative to the body left behind: a skater throws a ball at \(8\ \text{m/s}\) relative to herself. Momentum must be added with velocities over the ground, so convert first, with Lesson 1: \(\vvec_{\text{ball}} = \vvec_{\text{skater}} + \vvec_{\text{ball}/\text{skater}}\), where the skater's velocity is the one just after the throw, because that is when the relative velocity is measured.

Example 6.2 — A throw on smooth ice

A \(70\ \text{kg}\) skater standing on smooth ice throws a \(5\ \text{kg}\) ball horizontally forward at \(8\ \text{m/s}\) relative to herself. Find her velocity and the ball's velocity over the ice just after the throw.

Show solution

The throw is internal and the ice smooth, so the horizontal momentum stays zero. With \(v_s\) the skater's velocity after the throw, the ball moves at \(v_s + 8\):

\[ 0 = 70v_s + 5(v_s + 8) \;\Rightarrow\; v_s = -\frac{40}{75} = -0.533\ \text{m/s} \qquad v_{\text{ball}} = 7.47\ \text{m/s} \]

The skater slides back at \(0.533\ \text{m/s}\); the ball moves forward at \(7.47\ \text{m/s}\) over the ice, a little less than \(8\ \text{m/s}\). Writing \(5(8)\) for the ball's momentum would be the common mistake: it ignores the skater's recoil.

Conserved in one direction only

Return to the block on the free wedge of Lesson 3. The floor and gravity are vertical, so no external force acts horizontally: the horizontal momentum of the block and the wedge stays zero. Vertically, the block's weight is not balanced by the floor while the block accelerates downward, so the vertical momentum grows.

Example 6.3 — The wedge's speed

The \(5\ \text{kg}\) block of Example 3.2 starts from rest on the smooth \(30^\circ\) face of the \(20\ \text{kg}\) wedge, on a smooth floor. Find the speed of the wedge, and the speed of the block relative to it, after the block has slid \(1\ \text{m}\) along the face.

Show solution

Let the wedge move at \(V\) and the block slide down the face at \(u\) relative to it. Over the ground the block moves at \(V\ihat + u(-\cos 30^\circ\ihat - \sin 30^\circ\jhat)\).

Momentum, horizontally: \(0 = 20V + 5(V - u\cos 30^\circ)\), so \(V = 0.1732u\).

Energy, for the block and the wedge (the internal normal force does no net work: it is perpendicular to the relative motion): the block drops \(1\sin 30^\circ = 0.5\ \text{m}\).

\[ 5(9.81)(0.5) = \tfrac12(20)V^2 + \tfrac12(5)\left[(u\cos 30^\circ - V)^2 + (u\sin 30^\circ)^2\right] \] \[ 24.53 = 10(0.1732u)^2 + 2.5\left[(0.6928u)^2 + (0.5u)^2\right] = 2.125u^2 \] \[ u = 3.40\ \text{m/s} \qquad V = 0.588\ \text{m/s} \]

Check with Lesson 3: constant accelerations give \(u = \sqrt{2(5.77)(1)} = 3.40\ \text{m/s}\) and \(V = (1.00/5.77)\,u = 0.588\ \text{m/s}\).

Key takeaways

  • \(\Lvec = \sum m_i\vvec_i = m\vvec_G\), and \(\Lvec_1 + \sum\int\Fvec\,dt = \Lvec_2\) with the external forces only.
  • With no external impulse, \(\Lvec_1 = \Lvec_2\); check each direction separately.
  • Add momenta with velocities over the ground: convert relative velocities first, using the velocities just after the event.
  • Momentum gives one equation per direction. When there are more unknowns, add the work-energy principle.
  • Next, Lesson 7 applies these ideas to impacts, where the forces are large and brief.
