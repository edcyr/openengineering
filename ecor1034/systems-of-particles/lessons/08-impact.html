Central and Oblique Impact

Most colliding bodies do not stick: they squash, then spring back and separate. Momentum still holds across the impact, but it gives one equation for two unknown velocities. The second equation comes from how much the bodies spring back, measured by one number, the coefficient of restitution, which runs from zero for a plastic impact to one for a perfectly elastic one.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the deformation and restitution phases of an impact, and define the coefficient of restitution \(e\).
  • Solve a direct central impact with conservation of momentum and \(e\), and find the energy lost.
  • Find \(e\) from a bounce, and handle an impact with a fixed surface.
  • Solve an oblique impact by working along and across the line of impact.
  • Choose between the equations of motion, work and energy, and impulse and momentum for a system.

Deformation, restitution and \(e\)

The line of impact is the common normal to the two surfaces at the point of contact. When it passes through both centers of mass and the velocities lie along it, the impact is direct central impact. An impact has two phases. During deformation the bodies squash together until, at the instant of maximum deformation, they share one velocity; the contact force gives each an impulse \(\int P\,dt\). During restitution they spring back apart, with a smaller impulse \(\int R\,dt\). Their ratio is the coefficient of restitution; applying impulse and momentum to each body in each phase turns it into a ratio of velocities.

Coefficient of restitution, along the line of impact

\[ e = \frac{\int R\,dt}{\int P\,dt} = \frac{(v_B)_2 - (v_A)_2}{(v_A)_1 - (v_B)_1} \]

The relative velocity of separation (after, subscript 2) over the relative velocity of approach (before, subscript 1). \(e = 1\): perfectly elastic, no energy lost. \(e = 0\): plastic, the bodies move on together (Lesson 7). Real impacts lie between, and \(e\) depends on the materials and on the speed.

With conservation of momentum, that gives two equations for the two velocities after the impact:

Direct central impact

\[ m_A(v_A)_1 + m_B(v_B)_1 = m_A(v_A)_2 + m_B(v_B)_2 \qquad\qquad (v_B)_2 - (v_A)_2 = e[(v_A)_1 - (v_B)_1] \] \[ \Delta T_{\text{lost}} = \tfrac12\,\frac{m_A m_B}{m_A + m_B}\,(1 - e^2)[(v_A)_1 - (v_B)_1]^2 \]

Take one positive direction along the line of impact for all four velocities. The energy lost is the fraction \(1 - e^2\) of the relative-motion energy of Lesson 5.

Example 8.1 — A head-on impact

In Figure 8.1, \(m_A = 2\ \text{kg}\) moves at \(6\ \text{m/s}\) to the right and \(m_B = 3\ \text{kg}\) at \(2\ \text{m/s}\) to the left, and \(e = 0.6\). Find their velocities after the impact and the kinetic energy lost.

Show solution

Positive to the right: \((v_A)_1 = 6\), \((v_B)_1 = -2\ \text{m/s}\).

\[ \text{momentum: } 2(6) + 3(-2) = 6 = 2(v_A)_2 + 3(v_B)_2 \qquad \text{restitution: } (v_B)_2 - (v_A)_2 = 0.6(6 + 2) = 4.8 \]

Put \((v_B)_2 = (v_A)_2 + 4.8\) into the momentum equation: \(5(v_A)_2 + 14.4 = 6\), so

\[ (v_A)_2 = -1.68\ \text{m/s} \qquad (v_B)_2 = 3.12\ \text{m/s} \]

Both carts rebound. \(T_1 = \tfrac12(2)(36) + \tfrac12(3)(4) = 42\ \text{J}\) and \(T_2 = \tfrac12(2)(1.68^2) + \tfrac12(3)(3.12^2) = 17.4\ \text{J}\), so \(24.6\ \text{J}\) is lost: \(\tfrac12(1.2)(1 - 0.36)(8^2) = 24.6\ \text{J}\).

Impact with a fixed surface

A fixed wall or floor is a body of infinite mass: its velocity stays zero, and the restitution equation alone gives the rebound, \((v_n)_2 = -e\,(v_n)_1\) along the normal. A ball dropped from a height \(h_1\) hits the floor at \(\sqrt{2gh_1}\) and rebounds at \(e\) times that, to a height \(h_2 = e^2 h_1\). That is the simplest way to measure \(e\):

\[ e = \sqrt{h_2/h_1} \]

Example 8.2 — Measuring \(e\) with a bounce

A ball dropped from \(2\ \text{m}\) rebounds to \(1.28\ \text{m}\). Find \(e\), and the height of the next bounce.

Show solution
\[ e = \sqrt{\frac{1.28}{2}} = 0.8 \qquad h_3 = e^2 h_2 = 0.64(1.28) = 0.819\ \text{m} \]

Each bounce keeps \(e^2 = 64\%\) of the height, and of the kinetic energy at impact.

Oblique impact

When the velocities are not along the line of impact, the impact is oblique. Take the axis \(n\) along the line of impact and \(t\) along the tangent to the contact. If the surfaces are smooth, the contact forces act along \(n\) only. That gives four equations for the four components after the impact:

Oblique impact of smooth bodies

\[ t{:}\quad (v_A)_{t2} = (v_A)_{t1} \qquad (v_B)_{t2} = (v_B)_{t1} \] \[ n{:}\quad m_A(v_A)_{n1} + m_B(v_B)_{n1} = m_A(v_A)_{n2} + m_B(v_B)_{n2} \qquad (v_B)_{n2} - (v_A)_{n2} = e\,((v_A)_{n1} - (v_B)_{n1}) \]

Along \(t\), no impulse acts on either body, so each keeps its own \(t\) component. Along \(n\), the impact is a direct central impact.

Example 8.3 — Two pucks, off center

In Figure 8.2, \(e = 0.8\). Find the velocity of each puck after the impact, and the energy lost.

Show solution

Before: \((v_A)_{n1} = 4\cos 30^\circ = 3.464\ \text{m/s}\), \((v_A)_{t1} = 4\sin 30^\circ = 2\ \text{m/s}\); \(B\) is at rest. Along \(t\): \((v_A)_{t2} = 2\ \text{m/s}\), \((v_B)_{t2} = 0\). Along \(n\):

\[ 0.2(3.464) = 0.2(v_A)_{n2} + 0.3(v_B)_{n2} \qquad (v_B)_{n2} - (v_A)_{n2} = 0.8(3.464) = 2.771 \] \[ (v_A)_{n2} = -0.277\ \text{m/s} \qquad (v_B)_{n2} = 2.494\ \text{m/s} \]

\(B\) moves off along the line of impact at \(2.49\ \text{m/s}\). \(A\) keeps its \(2\ \text{m/s}\) along \(t\) and moves slightly back along \(n\): \((v_A)_2 = \sqrt{2^2 + 0.277^2} = 2.02\ \text{m/s}\), at \(7.9^\circ\) behind the tangent. The loss, \(\tfrac12\frac{(0.2)(0.3)}{0.5}(1 - 0.64)(3.464^2) = 0.259\ \text{J}\), comes from the \(n\) motion only.

Choosing a method for a system

Which principle to use for a system of particles
The question asks for…UseInternal forces
accelerations, tensions, contact forces at an instant\(\sum\Fvec = m\avec\) per particle, with constraints (Lessons 3, 4)on each FBD, in pairs
how the center of mass moves\(\sum\Fvec = m\avec_G\) (Lesson 4)cancel
speeds after a change of position\(T_1 + \sum U_{1-2} = T_2\), or \(T_1 + V_1 = T_2 + V_2\) (Lesson 5)count their work (springs, friction)
velocities after a time, a push, a burst\(\Lvec_1 + \sum\int\Fvec\,dt = \Lvec_2\) (Lesson 6)cancel
velocities just after an impactmomentum and \(e\) (Lessons 7, 8)cancel; never use energy across the impact

Problems with an impact usually chain the methods: energy up to the impact, momentum and \(e\) across it, energy after it, as in the ballistic pendulum.

Key takeaways

  • \(e = [(v_B)_2 - (v_A)_2]/[(v_A)_1 - (v_B)_1]\) along the line of impact: separation over approach, from \(0\) (plastic) to \(1\) (perfectly elastic).
  • Direct central impact: momentum plus \(e\) give the two velocities after; the loss is \(\tfrac12\frac{m_A m_B}{m_A + m_B}(1 - e^2)[(v_A)_1 - (v_B)_1]^2\).
  • On a fixed surface, \((v_n)_2 = -e\,(v_n)_1\) along the normal, and \(e = \sqrt{h_2/h_1}\) for a bounce.
  • Oblique impact of smooth bodies: the \(t\) components do not change; along \(n\) it is a direct impact.
