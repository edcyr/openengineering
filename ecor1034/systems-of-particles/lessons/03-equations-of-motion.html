Equations of Motion for a System

Each particle of a system obeys Newton's second law on its own. What makes a system harder than one particle is that the particles push and pull on each other, and that their motions are tied by constraints. The method is always the same: one free-body diagram and one kinetic diagram per particle, the constraint equations of Lesson 2, and a count of unknowns against equations before you start solving.

Learning objectives

  • Separate the forces on a system into internal forces, which act between its particles, and external forces.
  • Draw a free-body diagram (FBD) and a kinetic diagram for each particle, with each internal force acting equal and opposite on the two particles it joins.
  • Combine the equations of motion with the constraint equations, and check that the unknowns and the equations balance.
  • Solve systems joined by cords and pulleys, and a body sliding on another body that is free to move.

Internal and external forces

Draw a boundary around the particles you choose as the system. A force on a particle is external if it comes from outside the boundary: gravity, the floor's push, a hand. It is internal if it comes from another particle of the system: the tension in a cord joining two of them, the push between a block and the wedge it rests on, the force between two colliding balls.

By Newton's third law, internal forces come in pairs: if particle \(j\) pushes on particle \(i\) with \(\Fvec_{ij}\), then \(i\) pushes on \(j\) with \(\Fvec_{ji} = -\Fvec_{ij}\), along the same line. So the internal forces of a system always add up to zero. They still change how each particle moves; they cannot appear on a free-body diagram of the whole system.

Newton's second law for particle \(i\) of a system of \(n\)

\[ \Fvec_i + \sum_{j \ne i} \colInt{\Fvec_{ij}} = m_i \avec_i \qquad i = 1, 2, \ldots, n \]

\(\Fvec_i\) is the resultant external force on particle \(i\), and \(\Fvec_{ij}\) the internal force that particle \(j\) exerts on it, with \(\Fvec_{ji} = -\Fvec_{ij}\).

The method, step by step

  1. Kinematics first. Choose coordinates, and write the constraint equations between the accelerations (Lesson 2) and any relative accelerations (Lesson 1).
  2. One FBD and one kinetic diagram per particle. The FBD shows every force on the particle, internal ones included; the kinetic diagram shows \(m\avec\), drawn in terms of the accelerations you chose in step 1. Draw each internal force on both particles it joins, equal and opposite.
  3. Write the equations: FBD = kinetic diagram, component by component, for every particle. A light pulley or a light cord has no mass, so its FBD must balance.
  4. Count. The unknowns (accelerations, tensions, contact forces) must equal the equations (motion and constraints). If there are more unknowns, you have missed a constraint or a relation such as friction's \(F = \mu_k N\).
  5. Solve and check the signs: a negative tension means a cord went slack; a negative normal force means two bodies separate.

Three blocks on two cords

The system of Lesson 2 has two degrees of freedom. With masses on it, which way does each block go? Release it from rest in Figure 3.1.

Example 3.1 — The double Atwood machine

In Figure 3.1, \(m_A = 5\ \text{kg}\), \(m_B = 2\ \text{kg}\) and \(m_C = 3\ \text{kg}\); the cords and pulleys are light and the axles smooth. Find the acceleration of each block and the tension in each cord.

Show solution

Kinematics (down positive, from Lesson 2): \(a_P = -a_A\) and \(a_B + a_C = 2a_P = -2a_A\).

FBD = kinetic diagram for each block, along the vertical:

\[ A{:}\ \ m_A g - T_1 = m_A a_A \qquad B{:}\ \ m_B g - T_2 = m_B a_B \qquad C{:}\ \ m_C g - T_2 = m_C a_C \]

The light pulley \(P\): \(T_1\) up, two parts of cord 2 down, no mass, so \(T_1 = 2T_2\).

Count: six unknowns (\(a_A, a_B, a_C, a_P, T_1, T_2\)) and six equations (three blocks, the pulley, two cords). Put each acceleration in terms of \(T_2\) and use \(a_B + a_C = -2a_A\):

\[ \Bigl(g - \frac{T_2}{m_B}\Bigr) + \Bigl(g - \frac{T_2}{m_C}\Bigr) = -2\Bigl(g - \frac{2T_2}{m_A}\Bigr) \;\Rightarrow\; T_2 = \frac{4g}{1/m_B + 1/m_C + 4/m_A} \] \[ T_2 = \frac{4(9.81)}{0.5 + 0.333 + 0.8} = 24.0\ \text{N} \qquad T_1 = 48.0\ \text{N} \] \[ a_A = 9.81 - \frac{48.05}{5} = 0.200\ \text{m/s}^2 \qquad a_B = 9.81 - \frac{24.02}{2} = -2.20\ \text{m/s}^2 \qquad a_C = 9.81 - \frac{24.02}{3} = 1.80\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

\(A\) and \(C\) go down and \(B\) goes up. Check the cord: \(a_B + a_C = -0.40 = -2a_A\). Although \(m_A = m_B + m_C\), the system does not balance: the uneven pair \(B\), \(C\) accelerates, so it pulls up on \(P\) with less than its weight, \(2T_2 = 48.0\ \text{N} < 49.05\ \text{N}\).

A body on a moving body

A block slides down the smooth face of a wedge, and the wedge rests on a smooth floor. As the block slides, it pushes the wedge away. The block's motion is easiest to describe relative to the wedge, along the face, so take the unknowns to be \(a_W\), the wedge's acceleration, and \(a_{B/W}\), the block's acceleration along the face relative to it. Then, from Lesson 1,

\[ \avec_B = \avec_W + \avec_{B/W} \]

The normal force \(N\) between block and wedge is internal to the pair: it pushes each of them, equal and opposite, perpendicular to the face. Figure 3.2 shows the two FBDs and kinetic diagrams. The block's kinetic diagram has two parts, \(m_B\avec_W\) and \(m_B\avec_{B/W}\), because its acceleration has two.

Write FBD = kinetic diagram for the block along the face and across it, which keeps each equation short, and for the wedge horizontally:

\[ B,\ \text{along the face:}\ \ m_B g\sin\theta = m_B(a_{B/W} - a_W\cos\theta) \qquad B,\ \text{across:}\ \ N - m_B g\cos\theta = -m_B a_W\sin\theta \] \[ W,\ \text{horizontal:}\ \ N\sin\theta = m_W a_W \]

Three unknowns, \(a_W\), \(a_{B/W}\) and \(N\), and three equations:

A block on a smooth wedge, on a smooth floor

\[ \colRA{a_W} = \frac{m_B g\sin\theta\cos\theta}{m_W + m_B\sin^2\theta} \qquad \colRel{a_{B/W}} = \frac{(m_W + m_B)\,g\sin\theta}{m_W + m_B\sin^2\theta} \qquad \colInt{N} = \frac{m_W m_B g\cos\theta}{m_W + m_B\sin^2\theta} \]

A heavy wedge (\(m_W \to \infty\)) gives back the fixed incline: \(a_W = 0\), \(a_{B/W} = g\sin\theta\), \(N = m_B g\cos\theta\). A free wedge lets the block fall faster and press less.

Example 3.2 — The free wedge

A \(5\ \text{kg}\) block is released on the smooth \(30^\circ\) face of a \(20\ \text{kg}\) wedge, which stands on a smooth floor. Find the acceleration of the wedge, the acceleration of the block relative to the wedge and over the ground, and the normal force between them.

Show solution

\(m_W + m_B\sin^2 30^\circ = 20 + 5(0.25) = 21.25\ \text{kg}\).

\[ a_W = \frac{5(9.81)(0.5)(0.866)}{21.25} = 1.00\ \text{m/s}^2 \qquad a_{B/W} = \frac{25(9.81)(0.5)}{21.25} = 5.77\ \text{m/s}^2 \qquad N = \frac{20(5)(9.81)(0.866)}{21.25} = 40.0\ \text{N} \]

Over the ground, with \(x\) toward the wedge's motion and the face falling the other way:

\[ \avec_B = 1.00\ihat + 5.77(-\cos 30^\circ\ihat - \sin 30^\circ\jhat) = -4.00\ihat - 2.89\jhat\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

so \(a_B = 4.93\ \text{m/s}^2\), \(35.8^\circ\) below the horizontal: steeper than the \(30^\circ\) face. On a fixed wedge the block would slide at \(g\sin 30^\circ = 4.91\ \text{m/s}^2\) with \(N = 42.5\ \text{N}\).

Key takeaways

  • Internal forces act in equal and opposite pairs between particles of the system; external forces come from outside it.
  • Draw an FBD and a kinetic diagram for every particle, with each internal force on both particles it joins.
  • Add the constraints of Lesson 2 and the relative accelerations of Lesson 1, and count: unknowns = equations.
  • A light pulley or cord must balance: \(T_1 = 2T_2\) for a light movable pulley.
  • For a body on a moving body, use the relative acceleration along the contact as an unknown.
  • Next, Lesson 4 adds the equations of all the particles together: the internal forces cancel, and what remains moves the center of mass.
