Work and Energy for a System

The principle of work and energy for a single particle adds up over a system as easily as Newton's second law did, with one important difference: internal forces drop out of \(\sum\Fvec = m\avec_G\), but they do not always drop out of the work. A cord that cannot stretch does no net work; a spring between two particles, or friction between two parts that slide on each other, does.

Learning objectives

  • Apply \(T_1 + \sum U_{1-2} = T_2\) to a system, counting the work of external and internal forces.
  • Find the net work of a pair of internal forces from the relative motion of the two particles: zero for an inextensible cord or a rigid link, \(-\Delta V_e\) for a spring, negative for kinetic friction between sliding parts.
  • Use conservation of mechanical energy, \(T_1 + V_1 = T_2 + V_2\), for a conservative system.
  • Split the kinetic energy of a system into that of its center of mass and that of the motion relative to it.

The principle for a system

For each particle, the work done on it by every force, external and internal, equals the change in its kinetic energy: \((T_i)_1 + (U_i)_{1-2} = (T_i)_2\). Add these equations over all the particles:

Principle of work and energy for a system of particles

\[ \textstyle\sum T_1 + \sum U_{1-2} = \sum T_2 \qquad\qquad T = \sum_i \tfrac12 m_i v_i^2 \]

\(\sum U_{1-2}\) is the work of all the forces on all the particles, external and internal, between positions 1 and 2.

Why can the internal forces not be left out, as they were in \(\sum\Fvec = m\avec_G\)? Take one internal pair: \(\Fvec_{ij}\) on particle \(i\) and \(-\Fvec_{ij}\) on particle \(j\). The forces cancel, but they act on particles that move differently. Their total work is

\[ dU = \Fvec_{ij}\cdot d\rvec_i - \Fvec_{ij}\cdot d\rvec_j = \Fvec_{ij}\cdot d\rvec_{i/j} \]

It depends only on the relative displacement of the two particles. That gives three common cases:

  • Zero net work: an inextensible cord over a smooth pulley, a rigid link, two bodies that touch without sliding. The relative displacement along the force is zero.
  • A spring between two particles: its net work is \(-\Delta V_e\), with \(V_e = \tfrac12 ks^2\) and \(s\) the spring's change of length, however the two ends move.
  • Kinetic friction between two sliding parts: \(-F_f d\), where \(d\) is the distance one part slides relative to the other. It is always negative: that energy becomes heat.

A cord does no net work

Block \(A\) on an incline is tied over a light pulley to block \(B\), which hangs. Released, \(B\) falls and pulls \(A\) up the slope. The tension does positive work on \(A\), which moves the way the cord pulls it, and exactly as much negative work on \(B\), which moves against it. For the system of both blocks and the cord, only the weights and friction count.

Example 5.1 — Speed after the cord has run \(2\ \text{m}\)

In Figure 5.1, \(m_A = 10\ \text{kg}\), \(m_B = 8\ \text{kg}\), the slope is \(30^\circ\) and smooth, and the blocks start from rest. Find their speed when \(B\) has fallen \(2\ \text{m}\).

Show solution

Take the system to be both blocks and the cord. The cord is inextensible and the pulley light and smooth, so the tension does no net work; neither does the normal force. Both blocks share the speed \(v\).

\[ \textstyle\sum U_{1-2} = m_B g(2) - m_A g\sin 30^\circ(2) = 8(9.81)(2) - 10(9.81)(0.5)(2) = 156.96 - 98.10 = 58.86\ \text{J} \] \[ T_1 + \textstyle\sum U_{1-2} = T_2{:}\quad 0 + 58.86 = \tfrac12(m_A + m_B)v^2 \;\Rightarrow\; v = \sqrt{\frac{2(58.86)}{18}} = 2.56\ \text{m/s} \]

No tension and no acceleration were needed. To find the tension, use one block alone, where it is external: for \(B\), \(m_B g(2) - T(2) = \tfrac12 m_B v^2\) gives \(T = 65.4\ \text{N}\).

Friction between two moving parts

Kinetic friction between two bodies that both move is an internal pair, and it does not cancel in the work. In Figure 5.2 a block slides onto a plank that rests on a smooth floor. Friction pushes the block back and the plank forward with the same force, but the block, which slides, travels farther over the ground than the plank does. Friction does more negative work on the block than positive work on the plank.

Example 5.2 — Energy lost on the plank

In Figure 5.2, the block and the plank end up moving together at \(1\ \text{m/s}\). Find the energy lost to friction, the distance the block slides along the plank, and the work done by friction on each body.

Show solution

For the system, the floor and gravity do no work (they are vertical), so all of \(\sum U_{1-2}\) is the internal work of friction:

\[ T_1 = \tfrac12(2)(4^2) = 16\ \text{J} \qquad T_2 = \tfrac12(2 + 6)(1^2) = 4\ \text{J} \qquad \textstyle\sum U_{1-2} = T_2 - T_1 = -12\ \text{J} \]

\(12\ \text{J}\) are lost. With \(F_f = \mu_k m_b g = 0.3(2)(9.81) = 5.886\ \text{N}\) and \(\sum U_{1-2} = -F_f d\):

\[ d = \frac{12}{5.886} = 2.04\ \text{m along the plank} \]

On each body alone, friction is external: on the block \(U_b = (T_b)_2 - (T_b)_1 = \tfrac12(2)(1^2 - 4^2) = -15\ \text{J}\); on the plank \(U_p = (T_p)_2 - (T_p)_1 = \tfrac12(6)(1^2) = +3\ \text{J}\). They add to \(-12\ \text{J}\): the block moves \(15/5.886 = 2.55\ \text{m}\) over the ground while the plank moves \(0.51\ \text{m}\), and the difference is \(d\).

Conservative systems

If every force that does work on the system is conservative, the work can be written as a loss of potential energy, \(U_{1-2} = V_1 - V_2\), where \(V\) is the total potential energy: the gravitational energy \(V_g = mgy\) of every particle, and the elastic energy \(V_e = \tfrac12 ks^2\) of every spring, those between particles included.

Conservation of mechanical energy

\[ T_1 + V_1 = T_2 + V_2 \]

Forces that do no work, such as the tension of an inextensible cord or a smooth normal force on a fixed surface, may act; friction between sliding parts may not.

Example 5.1 is a conservative system: \(V_g\) falls by \(58.86\ \text{J}\) and \(T\) rises by the same. Example 5.2 is not.

Kinetic energy relative to the center of mass

Write each particle's velocity as the velocity of the center of mass plus its velocity relative to it, \(\vvec_i = \vvec_G + \vvec_{i/G}\). Then \(v_i^2 = v_G^2 + 2\vvec_G\cdot\vvec_{i/G} + v_{i/G}^2\), and the middle terms add to zero, because \(\sum m_i\vvec_{i/G} = \mathbf 0\) (the center of mass does not move relative to itself):

Kinetic energy of a system

\[ T = \tfrac12 mv_G^2 + \sum_i \tfrac12 m_i v_{i/G}^2 \]

The kinetic energy of the center of mass, as if all the mass moved with it, plus the kinetic energy of the motion relative to the center of mass.

The first part is tied to \(\vvec_G\), which only external forces can change (Lesson 4). The second part is the only one that internal forces such as friction or a collision can take away. That is why, in Example 5.2, \(4\ \text{J}\) had to remain: the system's center of mass moves at \(v_G = 2(4)/8 = 1\ \text{m/s}\) throughout, and \(\tfrac12(8)(1^2) = 4\ \text{J}\).

Example 5.3 — Two particles, two parts of \(T\)

Two \(1\ \text{kg}\) particles move along a line: one at \(4\ \text{m/s}\), the other at rest. Split their kinetic energy into its two parts.

Show solution

\(T = \tfrac12(1)(4^2) = 8\ \text{J}\). The center of mass moves at \(v_G = 4/2 = 2\ \text{m/s}\), so \(\tfrac12 mv_G^2 = \tfrac12(2)(2^2) = 4\ \text{J}\). Relative to \(G\), the particles move at \(+2\) and \(-2\ \text{m/s}\):

\[ \textstyle\sum \tfrac12 m_i v_{i/G}^2 = \tfrac12(1)(2^2) + \tfrac12(1)(2^2) = 4\ \text{J} \qquad 4 + 4 = 8\ \text{J} \]

If the two particles stick together when they meet (Lesson 7), they lose the \(4\ \text{J}\) of relative motion and keep the \(4\ \text{J}\) of the center of mass.

Key takeaways

  • \(T_1 + \sum U_{1-2} = T_2\) for a system, with the work of external and internal forces.
  • An internal pair does work \(\Fvec_{ij}\cdot d\rvec_{i/j}\): zero for an inextensible cord or a rigid link, \(-\Delta V_e\) for a spring, \(-F_f d\) for friction, with \(d\) the relative sliding distance.
  • For a conservative system, \(T_1 + V_1 = T_2 + V_2\), with \(V\) the potential energy of every particle and every spring.
  • \(T = \tfrac12 mv_G^2 + \sum\tfrac12 m_i v_{i/G}^2\); internal forces can change only the second part.
