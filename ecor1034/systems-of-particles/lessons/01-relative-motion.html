Relative Motion

A system of particles is any group of particles you choose to study together: two cars at an intersection, a block and the wedge it slides on, the fragments of a shell. Before the forces, the kinematics: how does one particle move as seen from another? The answer is a vector subtraction, and it turns problems that look hard from the ground, such as a near miss or a crossing in a current, into straight lines.

Learning objectives

  • Define the position, velocity and acceleration of one particle relative to another, \(\rvec_{B/A}\), \(\vvec_{B/A}\) and \(\avec_{B/A}\).
  • Use \(\vvec_B = \vvec_A + \vvec_{B/A}\) and \(\avec_B = \avec_A + \avec_{B/A}\), with the velocity triangle, to find an unknown velocity.
  • Find the closest approach of two particles by following one of them in a translating frame.
  • Solve crossing problems: a boat in a current, an aircraft in a wind, rain seen from a moving car.

Position, velocity and acceleration of B relative to A

Measure the positions of two particles \(A\) and \(B\) from a fixed point \(O\). The vector from \(A\) to \(B\) is the position of \(B\) relative to \(A\), \(\rvec_{B/A}\) (read "\(B\) relative to \(A\)"), so

\[ \colRB{\rvec_B} = \colRA{\rvec_A} + \colRel{\rvec_{B/A}} \]

\(\rvec_{B/A}\) is what an observer riding on \(A\) would measure with a set of axes that translate with \(A\): they move with it but keep pointing north and east, without turning. Differentiate with respect to time. Because the axes do not turn, the derivatives of the components are the components of the derivatives, and the same relation holds for velocities and accelerations.

Relative motion in a translating frame

\[ \colRB{\vvec_B} = \colRA{\vvec_A} + \colRel{\vvec_{B/A}} \qquad\qquad \colRB{\avec_B} = \colRA{\avec_A} + \colRel{\avec_{B/A}} \]

\(\vvec_{B/A} = \vvec_B - \vvec_A\) is the velocity of \(B\) as seen from \(A\). Seen from \(B\), \(A\) moves the opposite way: \(\vvec_{A/B} = -\vvec_{B/A}\).

The relation is a vector triangle: start with \(\vvec_A\), add \(\vvec_{B/A}\) head to tail, and you reach the tip of \(\vvec_B\). Any two of the three vectors give the third. Before you open Figure 1.1, make a prediction.

Example 1.1 — A near miss at an intersection

In Figure 1.1, find the velocity of \(B\) relative to \(A\), and how close the two cars come to each other.

Show solution

Take \(x\) east and \(y\) north, with the intersection at the origin.

\[ \vvec_{B/A} = \vvec_B - \vvec_A = 15\jhat - 20\ihat = -20\ihat + 15\jhat\ \text{m/s} \]

so \(v_{B/A} = \sqrt{20^2 + 15^2} = 25\ \text{m/s}\), pointing \(\tan^{-1}(15/20) = 36.9^\circ\) north of west. In \(A\)'s frame, \(B\) starts at \(\rvec_{B/A} = \rvec_B - \rvec_A = (0, -240) - (-200, 0) = 200\ihat - 240\jhat\ \text{m}\) and moves in a straight line:

\[ \rvec_{B/A}(t) = (200 - 20t)\ihat + (-240 + 15t)\jhat \]

The distance is smallest when \(\rvec_{B/A}\) is perpendicular to \(\vvec_{B/A}\), that is when \(\rvec_{B/A}\cdot\vvec_{B/A} = 0\):

\[ -20(200 - 20t) + 15(-240 + 15t) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 625t = 7600 \;\Rightarrow\; t = 12.16\ \text{s} \] \[ \rvec_{B/A} = -43.2\ihat - 57.6\jhat\ \text{m} \qquad r_{B/A} = 72.0\ \text{m} \]

Neither car changes speed, yet they pass \(72\ \text{m}\) apart, \(12.2\ \text{s}\) from now, with \(A\) past the intersection and \(B\) still short of it. From the ground you would need two moving positions; from \(A\), one straight line.

Moving through a moving medium

A boat's engine and rudder act on the water, so they set the boat's velocity relative to the water, \(\vvec_{B/W}\). An aircraft's airspeed and heading set its velocity relative to the air. The current or the wind, \(\vvec_W\), carries both along, and the velocity over the ground is the sum:

\[ \colRB{\vvec_B} = \colRA{\vvec_W} + \colRel{\vvec_{B/W}} \]

Two questions come up again and again. Where do you land if you aim straight across? And which way must you aim to go straight across?

Example 1.2 — Crossing a river

A boat moves at \(3\ \text{m/s}\) relative to the water in a river \(100\ \text{m}\) wide with a current of \(1.5\ \text{m/s}\). (a) If it is pointed straight across, how long does it take to cross, and how far downstream does it land? (b) In which direction must it be pointed to land directly opposite, and how long does that crossing take?

Show solution

Take \(x\) downstream and \(y\) across. (a) \(\vvec_B = 1.5\ihat + 3\jhat\ \text{m/s}\), a speed of \(3.35\ \text{m/s}\). Only the \(y\) part carries the boat across:

\[ t = \frac{100}{3} = 33.3\ \text{s} \qquad x = 1.5(33.3) = 50.0\ \text{m downstream} \]

(b) To go straight across, the \(x\) components must cancel: \(3\sin\phi = 1.5\), so the boat points \(\phi = 30.0^\circ\) upstream of straight across. Then

\[ v_B = 3\cos 30^\circ = 2.60\ \text{m/s} \qquad t = \frac{100}{2.60} = 38.5\ \text{s} \]

Pointing straight across is the fastest crossing, because all of \(\vvec_{B/W}\) goes into crossing; the current only adds drift. Try both in Figure 1.2.

Relative acceleration

The same triangle works for accelerations. It is most useful when \(B\) moves along a body that itself accelerates, because the motion relative to that body is easy to describe: a block on the face of a wedge can only slide along the face, so \(\avec_{B/W}\) is parallel to it.

Example 1.3 — A block on an accelerating wedge

In Figure 1.3, the wedge accelerates to the right at \(2\ \text{m/s}^2\), and the block slides down the face with an acceleration of \(3\ \text{m/s}^2\) relative to the wedge. Find the acceleration of the block.

Show solution

Down the face is the direction \(-\cos 30^\circ\,\ihat - \sin 30^\circ\,\jhat\).

\[ \avec_B = \avec_W + \avec_{B/W} = 2\ihat + 3(-0.866\ihat - 0.5\jhat) = -0.598\ihat - 1.50\jhat\ \text{m/s}^2 \] \[ a_B = \sqrt{0.598^2 + 1.50^2} = 1.61\ \text{m/s}^2, \quad \tan^{-1}\frac{1.50}{0.598} = 68.3^\circ \text{ below the horizontal, to the left} \]

The block falls more steeply than the face, because the face is moving out from under it. Lesson 3 finds both accelerations from the forces.

Key takeaways

  • \(\rvec_{B/A}\), \(\vvec_{B/A}\) and \(\avec_{B/A}\) are measured by an observer on \(A\) whose axes translate with \(A\) without turning.
  • \(\vvec_B = \vvec_A + \vvec_{B/A}\) and \(\avec_B = \avec_A + \avec_{B/A}\): a vector triangle, and \(\vvec_{A/B} = -\vvec_{B/A}\).
  • Seen from \(A\), a particle with a constant \(\vvec_{B/A}\) moves in a straight line; its closest approach is where \(\rvec_{B/A} \perp \vvec_{B/A}\).
  • In a current or a wind, the engine sets the velocity relative to the medium; the medium adds its own velocity.
