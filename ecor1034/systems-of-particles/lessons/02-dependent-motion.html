Dependent Motion

The particles of a system are seldom free. A cord of fixed length, a pulley, a rigid link or a guide ties their motions together, so knowing how some of them move tells you how the others must. Write each tie as an equation between position coordinates, differentiate it, and you have the relations between velocities and accelerations that every kinetics problem in this module needs. Motion tied together this way is called dependent motion.

Learning objectives

  • Count the degrees of freedom of a system: its coordinates minus its independent constraints.
  • Choose position coordinates measured from fixed points, and write the total length \(l_T\) of each cord in terms of them.
  • Differentiate constraint equations to relate the velocities and the accelerations of the particles.
  • Handle a constraint that is not linear in the coordinates, such as a rigid link between two sliders.

Constraints and degrees of freedom

A kinematic constraint is a geometric condition that the positions of the particles must satisfy at every instant: a cord keeps its length, a collar stays on its rod, a block stays on its incline. If you need \(n\) coordinates to describe the positions and they are tied by \(c\) independent constraint equations, the system has

Degrees of freedom

\[ \text{DOF} = n - c \]

The number of coordinates you can choose freely. Give the motion of that many of them, and the constraints fix the rest.

A block sliding down a fixed incline has one degree of freedom: its distance along the slope. Two blocks joined by a cord over a pulley also have one: when one moves, the other must. The system in Figure 2.1 has two, so it takes two independent velocities to set it moving.

Cords and pulleys

For bodies joined by inextensible cords, follow the same four steps every time:

  1. Pick a fixed datum, such as the axle of a pulley that does not move.
  2. Give each body a position coordinate \(s\), measured from the datum along that body's line of motion, positive in a direction you choose.
  3. Write the total length \(l_T\) of each cord as a sum of its parts in terms of the coordinates. The parts wrapped around pulleys, and any parts of fixed length, add only a constant.
  4. Differentiate each length once for the velocities and again for the accelerations.

One cord, one equation

\[ \sum_i k_i s_i + \text{constant} = l_T \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sum_i k_i v_i = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sum_i k_i a_i = 0 \]

\(k_i\) counts the straight parts of the cord whose length grows with \(s_i\) (positive) or shrinks with it (negative). A load held by a movable pulley and two parts of cord has \(k = 2\): pull the free end \(1\ \text{m}\), and the load rises \(0.5\ \text{m}\).

A system with two cords has two constraint equations. In Figure 2.1, cord 1 runs from block \(A\) over the fixed pulley \(D\) to the axle of the movable pulley \(P\); cord 2 runs over \(P\) from block \(B\) to block \(C\). All four coordinates are measured down from \(D\)'s axle. Cord 2 has two straight parts, of lengths \(s_B - s_P\) and \(s_C - s_P\). With the constant parts around the pulleys left out of the lengths \(l_1\) and \(l_2\),

\[ \text{cord 1:}\ \ s_A + s_P = l_1 \qquad\qquad \text{cord 2:}\ \ (s_B - s_P) + (s_C - s_P) = l_2 \] \[ v_A + v_P = 0 \qquad\qquad v_B + v_C - 2v_P = 0 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad v_B + v_C + 2v_A = 0 \]

Four coordinates and two cords: two degrees of freedom. Choose \(v_A\) and \(v_B\) in the figure, and the cords decide \(v_P\) and \(v_C\).

Example 2.1 — Two cords, two degrees of freedom

In Figure 2.1, block \(A\) moves down at \(0.6\ \text{m/s}\) and block \(B\) moves up at \(0.3\ \text{m/s}\). Find the velocities of the pulley \(P\) and of block \(C\). If at the same instant \(A\) has a downward acceleration of \(0.4\ \text{m/s}^2\) and \(B\) a downward acceleration of \(0.2\ \text{m/s}^2\), what is the acceleration of \(C\)?

Show solution

With down positive, \(v_A = 0.6\ \text{m/s}\) and \(v_B = -0.3\ \text{m/s}\).

\[ v_P = -v_A = -0.6\ \text{m/s} \qquad v_C = 2v_P - v_B = 2(-0.6) - (-0.3) = -0.9\ \text{m/s} \]

\(P\) moves up at \(0.6\ \text{m/s}\) and \(C\) up at \(0.9\ \text{m/s}\). The accelerations obey the same equations:

\[ a_C = -2a_A - a_B = -2(0.4) - 0.2 = -1.0\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

\(C\) accelerates upward at \(1.0\ \text{m/s}^2\). Set \(v_A = 0.6\) and \(v_B = -0.3\) in Figure 2.1 to watch it.

A rigid link

Not every constraint is a sum of lengths. Collar \(A\) slides on a horizontal rod and collar \(B\) on a vertical rod, and a rigid link of length \(L\) joins them. With the origin where the rods cross,

\[ x_A^2 + y_B^2 = L^2 \]

Differentiate with the chain rule: \(2x_A\dot x_A + 2y_B\dot y_B = 0\). Differentiate again, remembering that \(v_A\) and \(v_B\) change too:

Two sliders and a link

\[ x_A v_A + y_B v_B = 0 \qquad\qquad v_A^2 + x_A a_A + v_B^2 + y_B a_B = 0 \]

The relation between the speeds changes with the position. As the link nears the horizontal (\(y_B \to 0\)), a steady \(v_A\) needs an ever larger \(v_B\).

There is a second way to see the first equation. The link keeps \(\lvert\rvec_{B/A}\rvert = L\), so \(\rvec_{B/A}\) can only turn, and its rate of change, \(\vvec_{B/A}\), must be perpendicular to the link. Figure 2.2 draws that triangle: \(\vvec_B = \vvec_A + \vvec_{B/A}\), with \(\vvec_{B/A}\) across the link.

Example 2.2 — The sliding link

In Figure 2.2, \(L = 1.3\ \text{m}\). At the instant when \(x_A = 0.5\ \text{m}\), collar \(A\) moves to the right at a constant \(2\ \text{m/s}\). Find the velocity and the acceleration of collar \(B\).

Show solution

First the position: \(y_B = \sqrt{1.3^2 - 0.5^2} = 1.2\ \text{m}\). Then

\[ v_B = -\frac{x_A v_A}{y_B} = -\frac{0.5(2)}{1.2} = -0.833\ \text{m/s} \]

so \(B\) moves down at \(0.833\ \text{m/s}\). With \(a_A = 0\):

\[ a_B = -\frac{v_A^2 + v_B^2}{y_B} = -\frac{2^2 + 0.833^2}{1.2} = -3.91\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

\(B\) speeds up downward at \(3.91\ \text{m/s}^2\), although \(A\) does not accelerate at all. A constraint that is not linear makes accelerations out of steady speeds.

Key takeaways

  • Degrees of freedom = coordinates − independent constraints: the number of motions you can choose.
  • Measure every position from a fixed datum, along the body's own line of motion.
  • Each inextensible cord gives one equation, \(\sum k_i s_i + \text{constant} = l_T\), and so \(\sum k_i v_i = 0\) and \(\sum k_i a_i = 0\).
  • A constraint that is not linear, such as \(x_A^2 + y_B^2 = L^2\), gives speed ratios that change with position, and accelerations even when the driving speed is steady.
