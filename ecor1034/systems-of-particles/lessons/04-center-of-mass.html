Motion of the Center of Mass

Add up the equations of motion of every particle in a system, and the internal forces drop out in pairs. What is left is one vector equation that looks exactly like Newton's second law for a single particle: the external forces move one special point, the center of mass, as if all the mass were there. A shell can burst into pieces, a person can walk on a boat, and the center of mass does not notice.

Learning objectives

  • Locate the center of mass of a system of particles, and find its velocity and acceleration.
  • Derive \(\sum\Fvec = m\avec_G\) by adding the equations of the particles, and explain why only external forces appear.
  • Use the motion of the center of mass to predict where the pieces of a system go, even when the internal forces are unknown.
  • Recognize when the center of mass stays at rest or moves at a constant velocity.

The center of mass

A system of \(n\) particles has a total mass \(m = \sum m_i\). Its center of mass \(G\) is the mass-weighted average of their positions. Because the masses do not change, differentiating once and twice gives the velocity and the acceleration of \(G\):

The center of mass \(G\)

\[ m\rvec_G = \sum_i m_i \rvec_i \qquad\qquad m\vvec_G = \sum_i m_i \vvec_i \qquad\qquad m\avec_G = \sum_i m_i \avec_i \]

\(\rvec_G\), \(\vvec_G\) and \(\avec_G\) are the position, velocity and acceleration of \(G\). \(G\) need not sit on any particle: the center of mass of two equal balls is the point halfway between them.

Example 4.1 — Three particles

Three particles move in a plane. \(m_1 = 2\ \text{kg}\) is at \((1, 0)\ \text{m}\) moving at \(3\ihat\ \text{m/s}\); \(m_2 = 3\ \text{kg}\) is at \((0, 2)\ \text{m}\) moving at \(-2\jhat\ \text{m/s}\); \(m_3 = 5\ \text{kg}\) is at \((2, 1)\ \text{m}\) moving at \(\ihat + \jhat\ \text{m/s}\). Find the position and the velocity of the center of mass.

Show solution

\(m = 10\ \text{kg}\).

\[ \rvec_G = \frac{2(1, 0) + 3(0, 2) + 5(2, 1)}{10} = \frac{(12, 11)}{10} = (1.2, 1.1)\ \text{m} \] \[ \vvec_G = \frac{2(3\ihat) + 3(-2\jhat) + 5(\ihat + \jhat)}{10} = \frac{11\ihat - \jhat}{10} = 1.1\ihat - 0.1\jhat\ \text{m/s} \]

The equation of motion of the center of mass

Write Newton's second law for each particle (Lesson 3), with \(\Fvec_i\) the external force on it and \(\Fvec_{ij}\) the internal force from particle \(j\), and add all \(n\) equations:

\[ \sum_i \Fvec_i + \sum_i \sum_{j \ne i} \colInt{\Fvec_{ij}} = \sum_i m_i\avec_i \]

The double sum holds every internal pair twice, once as \(\Fvec_{ij}\) and once as \(\Fvec_{ji} = -\Fvec_{ij}\), so it is zero. The right side is \(m\avec_G\).

The center of mass moves like one particle

\[ \sum\Fvec = m\avec_G \]

\(\sum\Fvec\) is the resultant of the external forces only. The center of mass moves as if the whole mass were concentrated there and all the external forces acted on it.

Two consequences follow at once:

  • Internal forces cannot move \(G\). An explosion, a spring between two carts, a person walking: all are internal, and none changes \(\avec_G\).
  • If \(\sum\Fvec = \mathbf{0}\), then \(\vvec_G\) is constant. A center of mass at rest stays at rest. If only one component of \(\sum\Fvec\) is zero, that component of \(\vvec_G\) is constant.

The equation says nothing about how the individual particles move: for that you still need Lesson 3. But it can check a solution. In Example 3.2 nothing outside the block and the wedge pushes them horizontally, so \(m_B a_{Bx} + m_W a_W = 0\): indeed \(5(-4.00) + 20(1.00) = 0\).

A shell that bursts in flight

A shell flies on a parabola, pulled only by its weight. If it bursts, the forces of the explosion are internal, so its center of mass keeps flying on the same parabola, as long as the only external force is still the total weight \(m\mathbf g\). Once a fragment hits the ground, the ground pushes on it, and the center of mass leaves the parabola.

Example 4.2 — Half falls straight down

A \(10\ \text{kg}\) shell is fired at \(50\ \text{m/s}\) at \(60^\circ\) above the horizontal. At the top of its flight it bursts into two equal halves. Just after the burst, one half has zero velocity, and falls straight down. Where does the other half land?

Show solution

At the top the shell moves horizontally at \(50\cos 60^\circ = 25\ \text{m/s}\), after \(t = 50\sin 60^\circ/9.81 = 4.41\ \text{s}\) and \(x = 25(4.41) = 110.3\ \text{m}\). The burst does not change \(\vvec_G\):

\[ m\vvec_G = m_A\vvec_A + m_B\vvec_B: \qquad 10(25) = 5(0) + 5v_B \;\Rightarrow\; v_B = 50\ \text{m/s, horizontal} \]

Both halves start at the same height with no vertical velocity, so both fall for the same \(4.41\ \text{s}\). \(A\) lands at \(x_A = 110.3\ \text{m}\), and

\[ x_B = 110.3 + 50(4.41) = 331\ \text{m} \]

Check with the center of mass: the unburst shell would have landed at \(R = 2(110.3) = 220.7\ \text{m}\), and \((5(110.3) + 5(331))/10 = 220.7\ \text{m}\). The two halves land together, so \(G\) lands at \(R\), as it must.

When nothing pushes from outside

A person stands at the back of a boat floating on still water. When they walk forward, the friction of the deck on their feet pushes them forward, and their feet push the boat backward with an equal and opposite force. Both forces are internal to the person and the boat together. Neglect the water's drag, and nothing outside pushes them horizontally: their center of mass, at rest, must stay at rest.

Example 4.3 — Walking the length of a boat

An \(80\ \text{kg}\) person walks \(4\ \text{m}\) along a \(120\ \text{kg}\) boat, from the stern to the bow. The boat starts at rest, and the water's drag is negligible. How far do the boat and the person move relative to the shore?

Show solution

With no horizontal external force and \(\vvec_G = 0\) at the start, \(x_G\) does not change: \(m_p\Delta x_p + m_b\Delta x_b = 0\). The walk along the boat is relative motion: \(\Delta x_p - \Delta x_b = 4\ \text{m}\).

\[ 80(4 + \Delta x_b) + 120\Delta x_b = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \Delta x_b = -\frac{80(4)}{200} = -1.6\ \text{m} \qquad \Delta x_p = 2.4\ \text{m} \]

The boat moves \(1.6\ \text{m}\) backward and the person \(2.4\ \text{m}\) forward. The result does not depend on how the person walks, fast or slow, steady or not: only on the masses and the distance.

Key takeaways

  • \(m\rvec_G = \sum m_i\rvec_i\), \(m\vvec_G = \sum m_i\vvec_i\), \(m\avec_G = \sum m_i\avec_i\).
  • \(\sum\Fvec = m\avec_G\), with only the external forces: the internal forces cancel in pairs.
  • Internal forces, however large, cannot change the motion of the center of mass.
  • With no external force in a direction, that component of \(\vvec_G\) is constant; a center of mass at rest stays put.
