Wing and Tail Weights

The statistical group weight equations are power laws fitted to the weights of real aircraft, one equation per group. Each term says how strongly one design choice drives the weight. This lesson reads them for the heaviest lifting surface, the wing, and for the tails, and works out the business jet's.

Learning objectives

  • Read a power-law weight equation: units, exponents as sensitivities, and its range of validity.
  • Estimate a transport aircraft's wing weight and explain the effect of each term.
  • Estimate the horizontal and vertical tail weights, and the effect of the tail arm and of a T-tail.

Reading a statistical weight equation

Lessons 3 to 5 use Raymer's statistical equations for cargo and transport aircraft [1], Section 15.3, which the open-source design codes AeroSandbox [5] and SUAVE [6] also implement. Each equation has the form \(W = c\,x_1^{a_1}x_2^{a_2}\cdots\), fitted to the group weights of a set of real aircraft. Three things follow:

  • The equations are in their original units: weights in pounds, lengths in feet, areas in square feet, landing gear lengths in inches and speeds in knots. Convert the inputs, then convert the answer back to kilograms.
  • Each exponent is a sensitivity: a 1% change in \(x_i\) changes \(W\) by about \(a_i\)%. A term with a small exponent barely matters; a large one is where the weight is.
  • A fit is good only inside the range of aircraft it came from. These came from aircraft of the 1950s and 1960s [4]. Lesson 6 checks the total against modern aircraft and calibrates it.

The wing

Wing weight, transport aircraft (lb)

\[ \begin{aligned} W_{\text{wing}} = 0.0051\,(W_{dg}N_z)^{0.557}\,S_w^{0.649}\,A^{0.5} \\ \times\left(t/c\right)_{\text{root}}^{-0.4}\,(1+\lambda)^{0.1}\,(\cos\Lambda)^{-1}\,S_{csw}^{0.1} \end{aligned} \]

\(W_{dg}\), the design gross weight (lb); \(N_z\), the ultimate load factor; \(S_w\), the trapezoidal wing area (ft²); \(\Lambda\), the quarter-chord sweep; \(S_{csw}\), the area of the wing's control surfaces and flaps (ft²). Source: [1], Section 15.3.

Each term has a physical reading:

  • \((W_{dg}N_z)^{0.557}\): the load the wing must carry at ultimate load. Weight follows load, but less than in proportion.
  • \(S_w^{0.649}\) and \(A^{0.5}\): area is skin, and aspect ratio is span, the lever arm of the bending moment.
  • \((t/c)^{-0.4}\): a thicker wing has deeper spars, so less material carries the same bending. Thicker is lighter.
  • \((\cos\Lambda)^{-1}\): a swept wing's structure runs along the sweep, so it is longer and carries more twisting.
  • \((1+\lambda)^{0.1}\) and \(S_{csw}^{0.1}\) matter little: taper moves the lift inboard, and control surfaces bring hinges, tracks and actuators.

Example 3.1 — The business jet's wing

The business jet: \(W_{dg} = 15\,307\) lb, \(N_z = 4.882\) (Lesson 2: Module 11's gust), \(S_w = 28\) m² \(= 301.4\) ft², \(A = 8\), \(t/c = 0.12\), \(\lambda = 0.35\), \(\Lambda = 25^\circ\). Its Fowler flaps span 30% of the chord out to 67.5% of the semispan, and its ailerons 25% of the chord from 70% to 95%: \(S_{csw} = 6.48\) m² \(= 69.7\) ft². Find the wing's weight.

Show solution
\[ (W_{dg}N_z)^{0.557} = (74\,731)^{0.557} = 518.2, \qquad S_w^{0.649} = 40.64, \qquad A^{0.5} = 2.828 \] \[ (0.12)^{-0.4} = 2.334, \qquad 1.35^{0.1} = 1.031 \] \[ \frac{1}{\cos 25^\circ} = 1.103, \qquad 69.7^{0.1} = 1.529 \] \[ W_{\text{wing}} = 0.0051(518.2)(40.64)(2.828)(2.334)(1.031)(1.103)(1.529) = 1233\ \text{lb} \]

That is 559 kg, 8.1% of \(\Wo\), or 23.6 kg per square meter of exposed wing: less than half the transport value of Lesson 1. The general-aviation wing equation [1], [2], a different fit with the cruise dynamic pressure and the wing's fuel as inputs, gives 556 kg.

The tails

Horizontal and vertical tail weights, transport aircraft (lb)

\[ \begin{aligned} W_{\text{ht}} = 0.0379\,K_{uht}\left(1 + \frac{F_w}{B_h}\right)^{-0.25}W_{dg}^{0.639}N_z^{0.10}S_{ht}^{0.75} \\ \times L_t^{-1}K_y^{0.704}(\cos\Lambda_{ht})^{-1}A_h^{0.166}\left(1 + \frac{S_e}{S_{ht}}\right)^{0.1} \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} W_{\text{vt}} = 0.0026\left(1 + \frac{H_t}{H_v}\right)^{0.225}W_{dg}^{0.556}N_z^{0.536}L_t^{-0.5}S_{vt}^{0.5} \\ \times K_z^{0.875}(\cos\Lambda_{vt})^{-1}A_v^{0.35}\left(t/c\right)_{\text{root}}^{-0.5} \end{aligned} \]

\(K_{uht}\) is 1.143 for an all-moving tail, otherwise 1; \(F_w\), the fuselage's width where the horizontal tail meets it; \(B_h\), the horizontal tail's span; \(L_t\), the tail arm (ft); \(K_y \approx 0.3L_t\) and \(K_z \approx L_t\), the radii of gyration in pitch and yaw; \(S_e\), the elevator's area; \(H_t/H_v\), the height of the horizontal tail on the fin as a share of the fin's height: 0 on the fuselage, 1 for a T-tail. Source: [1], Section 15.3.

A longer tail arm \(L_t\) lightens both tails twice over: the equations fall with it, and a longer arm needs less tail area for the same volume coefficient (Module 4). A T-tail puts the horizontal tail's loads on top of the fin, whose structure must carry them down: \(2^{0.225} = 1.169\), 17% more fin.

Example 3.2 — The business jet's tails

The business jet's horizontal tail (Module 6): \(S_{ht} = 5.42\) m² (58.4 ft²), \(A_h = 4\), \(\Lambda_{ht} = 30^\circ\), span 4.66 m, on the tail cone where the fuselage is 0.48 m wide, elevator 30% of its area. The fin: \(S_{vt} = 3.45\) m² (37.2 ft²), \(A_v = 1.5\), \(\Lambda_{vt} = 35^\circ\), \(t/c = 0.10\). Both arms are 7.285 m (23.9 ft). Find their weights.

Show solution

With \(K_y = 0.3(23.9) = 7.17\) ft and \(K_z = 23.9\) ft:

\[ \begin{aligned} W_{\text{ht}} &= 0.0379(0.976)(472.2)(1.172)(21.12) \\ &\quad\times\frac{4.002}{23.90}(1.155)(1.259)(1.027) = 108.0\ \text{lb} \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} W_{\text{vt}} &= 0.0026(212.2)(2.339)(0.2046)(6.095) \\ &\quad\times(16.07)(1.221)(1.152)(3.162) = 115.1\ \text{lb} \end{aligned} \]

That is 49.0 and 52.2 kg: together 1.5% of \(\Wo\). The fin weighs more than the larger horizontal tail, mostly through its long yaw radius of gyration and its thin section.

Key takeaways

  • Statistical weight equations are power laws in pounds and feet; each exponent is the percentage change in weight per percent change in its variable.
  • Wing weight grows with the ultimate load (\(0.557\)), the area (\(0.649\)), the span (\(A^{0.5}\)) and the sweep, and falls with thickness (\(-0.4\)).
  • The business jet's wing weighs 559 kg (8.1% of \(\Wo\)); its tails 49.0 and 52.2 kg.
  • Longer tail arms lighten the tails twice over; a T-tail makes the fin 17% heavier.
