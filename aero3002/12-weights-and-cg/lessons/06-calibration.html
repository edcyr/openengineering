Checking and Calibrating the Estimate

The equations gave the business jet an empty weight of 3504 kg, 12% below Module 3's 3972 kg. Before believing either, check them against aircraft that exist. This lesson finds the equations light for a modern business jet, calibrates them, and closes the sizing loop with them.

Learning objectives

  • Compare a statistical weight estimate with real aircraft of the same class.
  • Calibrate statistical equations to a known empty weight, and explain what calibration keeps and what it changes.
  • Apply the reductions for composite structure.
  • Close the sizing loop with component weights, and find how sensitive the takeoff weight is to the empty weight.

Module 3's fraction agrees with the real aircraft; the group weights do not. Their equations were fitted to aircraft of the 1950s and 1960s [4], with spartan cabins and simple systems. A business jet's cabin has cabinetry, sound insulation and lighting; its avionics and systems do far more; and modern certification adds structure for fatigue, damage tolerance and crashworthiness. Module 6's illustrative airliner shows the same gap more strongly: the furnishings equation gives its 150-seat cabin 462 kg, and its empty weight comes out at 40.5% of \(\Wo\) against the 58.9% its fuel fraction and payload leave.

Calibrating the equations

A power law's exponents carry the physics of how a part's weight scales with its size and load; its constant carries the technology and practice of the aircraft it was fitted to. Calibration keeps the exponents and replaces the constant, with a factor \(k\) that makes the equations reproduce a known aircraft of the class:

Calibration factor

\[ k = \frac{W_{e,\text{target}} - W_{\text{catalog}}}{W_{\text{statistical}}} \]

\(W_{\text{catalog}}\): the weights known from the manufacturer, such as the dry engines, which are not scaled; \(W_{\text{statistical}}\): the sum of every group from the equations. Each statistical group is multiplied by \(k\).

Example 6.1 — Calibrating to Module 3

Module 3's empty-weight fraction agrees with the light jets. Calibrate the business jet's equations to its 3972 kg, keeping the engines' 401.6 kg.

Show solution
\[ W_{\text{statistical}} = 3503.8 - 401.6 = 3102.2\ \text{kg}, \qquad k = \frac{3972.4 - 401.6}{3102.2} = 1.151 \]

Every statistical group is 15.1% heavier: the wing 644 kg, the fuselage 1163 kg. The calibrated statement keeps each group's share from the equations, and with it each group's position, which Lesson 7 needs.

Calibrated against the empty weights their own fuel fractions and payloads leave, the four aircraft of this module need factors from 1.15 (business jet) and 1.17 (commuter) to 1.24 (turboprop) and 1.54 (airliner). The business jet's statement uses Module 11's gust load factor; the three illustrative aircraft, whose gust loads Module 11 did not find, keep the maneuvering factor of 25.337 [3]. A calibration is only as good as the aircraft it comes from: find one as close as possible to the design in size, role and technology.

Closing the sizing loop

Module 3 closed the sizing equation with an empty-weight fraction. With group weights, the empty weight is a function of the design itself:

\[ \Wo = W_{\text{crew}} + W_{\text{payload}} + \frac{W_f}{\Wo}\Wo + \We(\Wo) \]

To evaluate \(\We(\Wo)\) at a new takeoff weight, the design must be resized consistently: the wing area in proportion to \(\Wo\) at the same wing loading, the tails at the same volume coefficients, the engines at the same thrust-to-weight ratio (Module 7's rubber engine), the fuel at the same fraction. The fuselage stays, since the payload does. Guess \(\Wo\), compute the right side, and repeat until the two agree.

Example 6.3 — One step of the loop

At a guess of 6000 kg the uncalibrated equations give the resized business jet \(\We = 3200\) kg. Find the next estimate of \(\Wo\).

Show solution
\[ \Wo = 980 + 0.2867(6000) + 3200 = 5900\ \text{kg} \]

Lower than the guess, so the loop moves down. It settles at 5743 kg; with the calibration it stays at 6943 kg, as it must.

The loop amplifies every kilogram. With the calibration, an all-composite structure saves not 202 kg but more than 530: the business jet closes at 6409 kg. A calibration factor 0.1 higher, 1.25 instead of 1.15, closes it near 7900 kg. That is why the calibration must come from real aircraft, and why weight estimates carry margins.

This loop holds the fuel fraction at Module 3's 0.2867, so it follows only the weights. The full design loop follows the drag as well: a heavier aircraft needs a larger wing at the same wing loading, with more wetted area, and any change to the layout changes the zero-lift drag, \((L/D)_{\max}\) and with them the fuel fraction. Module 10 found the business jet cruising at an \(L/D\) of 10.8 at 12 km, not the 14.6 that Module 3's fuel fraction assumed; Lesson 8 follows one turn of that larger loop.

Key takeaways

  • Check any estimate against real aircraft of the class: the equations give the business jet 3504 kg, an operating weight of 53% of \(\Wo\) against 60 to 65% for real light jets.
  • Calibration keeps the equations' exponents and replaces their constants: \(k = (W_{e,\text{target}} - W_{\text{catalog}})/W_{\text{statistical}}\), 1.151 for the business jet.
  • Composites: factors of 0.85 to 0.95 on the structure; 202 kg on the business jet.
  • The sizing loop multiplies every kilogram by about 2.6; uncalibrated, the business jet would close at 5743 kg.
  • The loop here holds Module 3's fuel fraction; a full design loop also feeds every change of layout through the drag, \((L/D)_{\max}\) and the fuel fraction (Lesson 8).
  • Lesson 7 finds the centers of gravity from the calibrated statement, 3972 kg.
