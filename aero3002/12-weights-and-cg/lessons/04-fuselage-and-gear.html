Fuselage and Landing Gear Weights

The fuselage is the business jet's heaviest group: a pressurized tube with cutouts for doors and windows that carries the tails' loads forward to the wing. The landing gear is small by comparison, but its weight is set by the hardest landing the aircraft may make. This lesson estimates both.

Learning objectives

  • Estimate a transport fuselage's weight, with the factors for cargo doors, fuselage-mounted gear and the wing's sweep.
  • Estimate the main and nose landing gear weights from the landing weight and load factor.
  • Find the weight each extra row of seats adds to the fuselage.

The fuselage

Fuselage weight, transport aircraft (lb)

\[ W_{\text{fus}} = 0.3280\,K_{\text{door}}K_{lg}(W_{dg}N_z)^{0.5}L^{0.25}S_f^{0.302}(1 + K_{ws})^{0.04}(L/D)^{0.10} \] \[ K_{ws} = 0.75\left[\frac{1 + 2\lambda}{1 + \lambda}\right]\frac{B_w\tan\Lambda}{L} \]

\(L\) and \(D\), the fuselage's structural length and depth (ft); \(S_f\), its wetted area (ft²); \(B_w\), the wing span (ft). \(K_{\text{door}}\) is 1.0 with no cargo door, 1.06 with one side cargo door, 1.12 with two or with an aft clamshell door, and 1.25 with two side doors and a clamshell; \(K_{lg}\) is 1.12 if the main gear is mounted on the fuselage, otherwise 1. Source: [1], Section 15.3.

The square root of the ultimate load says the fuselage is less load-driven than the wing. Its size matters more, through the wetted area and the length. \(K_{ws}\) grows with the sweep and span of the wing: a swept wing's root carries its bending moment into the fuselage over a longer stretch. The fineness \(L/D\) adds a little for long, slender tubes, which bend more under the tail loads.

The equation has no pressurization term: the aircraft it was fitted to were pressurized, so the pressure loads are in its constant.

Example 4.1 — The business jet's fuselage

The business jet's fuselage (Module 6): \(L = 15.5\) m (50.85 ft), \(D = 1.597\) m, \(S_f = 67.38\) m² (725.3 ft²), no cargo door, main gear on the wing. The wing: \(B_w = 14.97\) m, \(\lambda = 0.35\), \(\Lambda = 25^\circ\). Find the fuselage's weight.

Show solution
\[ K_{ws} = 0.75\left(\frac{1.70}{1.35}\right)\frac{14.97\tan 25^\circ}{15.5} = 0.425, \qquad \frac LD = \frac{15.5}{1.597} = 9.71 \] \[ (W_{dg}N_z)^{0.5} = (74\,731)^{0.5} = 273.4, \qquad L^{0.25} = 2.670, \qquad S_f^{0.302} = 7.310 \] \[ W_{\text{fus}} = 0.3280(273.4)(2.670)(7.310)(1.014)(1.255) = 2228\ \text{lb} \]

That is 1011 kg, 14.6% of \(\Wo\) and 15.0 kg per square meter of wetted area: the heaviest group, as for most transports.

The landing gear

Main and nose landing gear weights, transport aircraft (lb)

\[ W_{\text{main}} = 0.0106\,K_{mp}W_l^{0.888}N_l^{0.25}L_m^{0.4}N_{mw}^{0.321}N_{mss}^{-0.5}V_{\text{stall}}^{0.1} \] \[ W_{\text{nose}} = 0.032\,K_{np}W_l^{0.646}N_l^{0.2}L_n^{0.5}N_{nw}^{0.45} \]

\(W_l\), the landing design gross weight (lb); \(N_l = 1.5\,N_{\text{gear}}\), the ultimate landing load factor; \(L_m\) and \(L_n\), the extended lengths of the main and nose gear (in); \(N_{mw}\) and \(N_{nw}\), the numbers of main and nose wheels; \(N_{mss}\), the number of main shock struts; \(V_{\text{stall}}\), the landing stall speed (kt). \(K_{mp} = 1.126\) and \(K_{np} = 1.15\) for kneeling gear, otherwise 1. Source: [1], Section 15.3.

The main gear carries nearly all the landing load, so its weight follows the landing weight almost in proportion (\(0.888\)); the nose gear, sized as much by braking and steering as by landing, less so. Long legs add weight, as Module 8 found for gear that must clear a high tail or a large propeller.

Example 4.2 — The business jet's landing gear

The business jet lands at \(W_l = 0.9\Wo = 6249\) kg (13 776 lb) with \(N_{\text{gear}} = 3\) (Module 8). One wheel on each of its two main legs, 0.615 m (24.2 in) long from the wing to the axle with the strut extended; one nose wheel on a leg 0.686 m (27.0 in) long. It stalls at 41.25 m/s (80.2 kt) in the landing configuration. Find the gear weights.

Show solution
\[ W_l^{0.888} = 4738, \qquad N_l^{0.25} = 4.5^{0.25} = 1.457, \qquad L_m^{0.4} = 3.578 \] \[ 2^{0.321} = 1.249, \qquad 80.2^{0.1} = 1.550 \] \[ W_{\text{main}} = 0.0106(4738)(1.457)(3.578)(1.249)(0.7071)(1.550) = 358.4\ \text{lb} \] \[ W_{\text{nose}} = 0.032(472.2)(1.351)(27.0)^{0.5}(1)^{0.45} = 106.1\ \text{lb} \]

That is 162.5 and 48.1 kg: 210.6 kg, 3.0% of \(\Wo\). Lesson 1's approximate weights gave 4.3% (transports) and 5.7% (general aviation).

Key takeaways

  • Fuselage weight grows with the square root of the ultimate load and with the wetted area (\(0.302\)) and length (\(0.25\)); doors and fuselage-mounted gear add 6 to 25%.
  • The business jet's fuselage weighs 1011 kg, 14.6% of \(\Wo\): its heaviest group.
  • Main gear weight follows the landing weight almost in proportion (\(0.888\)); long legs, more wheels and kneeling gear add to it.
  • The business jet's gear: 162.5 kg main, 48.1 kg nose, 3.0% of \(\Wo\).
