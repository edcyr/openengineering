The Weight Statement

Module 3 found the business jet's takeoff weight with one statistical number for its empty weight: 57.2% of the whole. Now that the aircraft has a wing, a fuselage, engines and landing gear, its empty weight can be built up piece by piece. This lesson sets out the weights a design is built around, checks them against three real business jets, and makes a first estimate of the pieces.

Learning objectives

  • Define the empty, operating empty, zero-fuel, landing and takeoff weights, and say what each one limits.
  • Read a manufacturer's weight data and compare a design with it as fractions of the takeoff weight.
  • List the groups of a weight statement: structure, propulsion and equipment.
  • Estimate group weights from weights per unit area and fractions, and explain why that method does not scale.

The weights a design is built around

An aircraft's weight changes through every flight, so a design is built around a handful of defined weights, each with a limit the certification basis sets for its own operating condition (14 CFR 25.25 [3]):

  • The empty weight \(\We\) is the aircraft as built: structure, engines and all fixed equipment, with its unusable fuel and full operating fluids, such as oil and hydraulic fluid (14 CFR 25.29 [3]). It is what the scales show at delivery.
  • The operating empty weight adds the crew and the items an operator needs to fly. Manufacturers often quote a basic operating weight for a typical aircraft ready to fly.
  • The zero-fuel weight adds the payload. Its maximum limits the bending at the wing root: fuel in the wing hangs where the lift is and relieves the bending, so the worst case is a full cabin and empty tanks.
  • The takeoff weight \(\Wo\), the design gross weight of the structural equations in this module, adds the fuel. The ramp weight adds the fuel burned while taxiing.
  • The landing weight limits the landing gear and the structure in a hard landing (Module 8). Module 5 took the business jet's as 90% of \(\Wo\).

Example 1.1 — Reading the CJ3's weights

From the table, find the CJ3's operating weight as a fraction of its takeoff weight, and the fuel it can carry with its maximum payload aboard.

Show solution
\[ \frac{3874}{6291} = 0.616, \qquad W_{\text{fuel}} = 6291 - 4842 = 1449\ \text{kg} \]

With a full cabin the CJ3 takes off with 68% of its usable fuel. The business jet, sized for its full payload over 4600 km, carries all its fuel with all its payload.

The weight groups

A weight statement lists the empty weight by groups, in a fixed order, so that one design can be compared with another and each group's owner knows their budget. The lessons use three groups:

  • Structure: the wing, the horizontal and vertical tails, the fuselage and the main and nose landing gear.
  • Propulsion: the engines, their nacelles and mounts, the engine controls, the starters and the fuel system.
  • Equipment: the flight controls, the auxiliary power unit, instruments, hydraulic and electrical systems, avionics, furnishings with the seats and lavatories, air conditioning, anti-icing and handling gear.

Each group's weight also has a position along the aircraft. Lessons 2 to 6 estimate the weights and Lessons 7 and 8 place them, to find the center of gravity and how it moves as the aircraft is loaded.

A first estimate: approximate group weights

The quickest estimate gives each structural group a weight per unit area, and the rest as fractions: the wing and tails per square meter of exposed planform, the fuselage per square meter of wetted area, the landing gear and everything else as shares of \(\Wo\), and the installed engines as a multiple of their dry weight.

Example 1.2 — The business jet by approximate group weights

The business jet's exposed wing is 23.73 m², its tails 5.42 and 3.45 m², its fuselage's wetted area 67.38 m² (Module 6), and its two engines weigh 401.6 kg dry (Module 7). Estimate its empty weight with the transport values.

Show solution
\[ W_{\text{wing}} = 48.8(23.73) = 1158\ \text{kg}, \qquad W_{\text{fus}} = 24.4(67.38) = 1645\ \text{kg} \] \[ W_{\text{tails}} = 26.9(5.42 + 3.45) = 238\ \text{kg}, \qquad W_{\text{gear}} = 0.043(6943) = 299\ \text{kg} \] \[ W_{\text{engines}} = 1.3(401.6) = 522\ \text{kg}, \qquad W_{\text{else}} = 0.17(6943) = 1180\ \text{kg} \]

The total is 5043 kg, 72.6% of \(\Wo\): far above Module 3's 57.2%. The general-aviation values give 2489 kg, 35.9%, far below.

Neither class fits, because weight per unit area is not a constant. A wing's bending moment grows with the weight it carries times its span, so a larger aircraft needs a heavier structure for every square meter: the transport values come from aircraft many times the business jet's size, and the general-aviation values from aircraft a quarter of it. Approximate group weights work only for an aircraft close in size to the ones they came from. The statistical group weight equations of Lessons 3 to 5 carry the size, the load and the shape of each part explicitly.

Key takeaways

  • The empty weight is the aircraft as built, with unusable fuel and operating fluids; the operating weight adds the crew; the zero-fuel weight adds the payload; the takeoff weight adds the fuel.
  • The maximum zero-fuel weight limits wing bending; the maximum landing weight limits the gear and the structure in a hard landing.
  • Light business jets carry 60 to 65% of their takeoff weight as operating weight; the course's design, at 59.8%, is at the light end.
  • A weight statement lists structure, propulsion and equipment by group.
  • Weights per unit area grow with size, so approximate group weights fit only aircraft of the size they came from: for the business jet they give 36% to 73% of \(\Wo\).
