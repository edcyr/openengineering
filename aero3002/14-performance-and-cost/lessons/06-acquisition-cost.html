Lesson 6 · 30 min

Development and Production Cost

An aircraft that meets every requirement but costs too much will not be built. At the conceptual stage its cost is estimated the way its weight was: from statistical equations fitted to past programs, driven by a few numbers the design already has. This lesson estimates what it would cost to develop the business jet and build 500 of them.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish the non-recurring costs of developing an aircraft from the recurring costs of building each one.
  • Estimate the labor hours and costs of a program with the DAPCA IV model, and bring them to present-day dollars.
  • Explain how the production quantity and the learning effect drive the unit cost.

The DAPCA IV model

A program's cost falls into non-recurring costs, paid once (engineering and design, tooling, development support, flight test), and recurring costs, paid for each aircraft (manufacturing labor, quality control, materials, and the engines, avionics and interior bought in). The RAND Corporation's DAPCA model (Development and Procurement Costs of Aircraft) fits each to the empty weight, the maximum speed and the number built. Its fourth version, DAPCA IV, is the one the course text gives [1]; in metric form:

DAPCA IV labor hours (metric)

\[ H_E = 5.18\,W_e^{0.777}\,V^{0.894}\,Q^{0.163}, \qquad H_T = 7.22\,W_e^{0.777}\,V^{0.696}\,Q^{0.263} \] \[ H_M = 10.5\,W_e^{0.82}\,V^{0.484}\,Q^{0.641}, \qquad H_Q = 0.133\,H_M \]

Engineering, tooling, manufacturing and quality-control hours for the whole program; \(W_e\) in kg, \(V\) the maximum speed in km/h, \(Q\) the number built (\(H_Q = 0.076\,H_M\) for a cargo aircraft). Each is multiplied by a wrap rate: in 2012 dollars per hour, 115 for engineering, 118 for tooling, 98 for manufacturing and 108 for quality control.

DAPCA IV costs (metric, 2012 dollars)

\[ C_D = 67.4\,W_e^{0.630}\,V^{1.3}, \qquad C_F = 1947\,W_e^{0.325}\,V^{0.822}\,N_{FT}^{1.21} \] \[ C_M = 31.2\,W_e^{0.921}\,V^{0.621}\,Q^{0.799} \]

Development support, flight test with \(N_{FT}\) test aircraft, and manufacturing materials. Engines, avionics and the interior are added at their own prices. Source: the DAPCA IV model of [1], as implemented in AeroSandbox, an open-source Python library for aircraft design [5]. To bring 2012 dollars to 2024, multiply by 1.327, the ratio of consumer price indices that [5] uses.

The model was fitted to military aircraft, built to military standards and documentation, and it is known to overpredict the cost of civil and general-aviation aircraft. Its value at the conceptual stage is in comparing designs, and in showing which numbers drive the cost: the empty weight above all, then the speed and the quantity.

Example 6.1 — The baseline's engineering hours

The baseline business jet: \(W_e = 4932\) kg and a maximum level speed of 978 km/h (Mach 0.86 at 6 km, Lesson 2). For a run of 500 aircraft, estimate the engineering hours and their cost in 2024 dollars.

Show solution
\[ H_E = 5.18(4932)^{0.777}(978)^{0.894}(500)^{0.163} \] \[ H_E = 5.18(740.4)(471.4)(2.754) = 4.98 \times 10^6\ \text{h} \]

About 2400 engineer-years. At $115 per hour in 2012 dollars, times 1.327:

\[ C_E = 4.98 \times 10^6(115)(1.327) = 760\ \text{million dollars} \]

$1.52 million per aircraft over the run. Manufacturing hours, 16.9 million, cost the most: $2192 million.

Quantity and learning

The non-recurring costs are shared by every aircraft built, so they fall per aircraft as \(1/Q\). The recurring costs fall too, more slowly, because the workforce learns: the exponent 0.641 on \(Q\) in the manufacturing hours means the average hours per aircraft fall by \(1 - 2^{0.641 - 1} = 22\%\) each time the run doubles, a 78% learning curve.

Example 6.2 — 100 aircraft or 500?

The baseline's program, with the engines at $1.22 million each, $0.8 million of avionics and the interior, costs $2653 million for 100 aircraft and $6715 million for 500 (2024 dollars). What is the cost per aircraft in each case, and how much of the difference is the non-recurring cost, $1132 million and $1486 million?

Show solution

Per aircraft: \(2653/100 = 26.5\) and \(6715/500 = 13.4\) million dollars. The non-recurring share falls from \(1132/100 = 11.3\) to \(1486/500 = 3.0\) million; the recurring cost, from 15.2 to 10.5 million, with learning. A program that sells only 100 must charge twice as much per aircraft to break even: the market forecast is as much a design input as the range.

The manufacturer's price adds a profit: take 15%. At 500 aircraft the baseline costs $13.4 million each to make and sells for $15.4 million. Real light business jets are priced well below that, and the model's military pedigree is the main reason; but the comparison between designs, which is what a conceptual estimate is for, is sound: every kilogram of empty weight adds about $1700 to the cost of each aircraft at this size, so the weight savings of earlier modules pay twice, in fuel and in price.

The business jet's cost inputs

Take 500 aircraft; 4 flight-test aircraft; aluminum structure; engines at $1.2 million for 15.4 kN, scaled with thrust; $0.8 million of avionics; $3500 per seat of interior in 2012 dollars (the jet-transport value of [1], as given in [5]; a business jet's interior costs far more); a 15% margin. A real program replaces them with quotes and a market study; the Design Closure tool takes your own.

Key takeaways

  • Non-recurring costs (engineering, tooling, development, flight test) are shared over the run; recurring costs (manufacturing, materials, bought-in equipment) are paid per aircraft.
  • DAPCA IV estimates each from the empty weight, the maximum speed and the quantity; wrap rates turn hours into dollars, a price index brings them to the present.
  • The baseline: about $6.7 billion for 500 aircraft, $13.4 million each to make; $26.5 million each if only 100 are built.
  • DAPCA IV overpredicts civil aircraft; use it to compare designs.
