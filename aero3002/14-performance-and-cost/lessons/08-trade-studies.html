Lesson 8 · 40 min

Trade Studies and the Final Design

The closed baseline is consistent but unacceptable: it lands long, its fuel does not quite fit, and it cruises too low. A trade study resizes the design over its main parameters, checks every requirement at every point, and finds the best design that meets them all. This lesson runs it for the business jet, and arrives at the course's final design.

Learning objectives

  • Build a sizing matrix: close the design loop over a grid of wing loadings and thrust-to-weight ratios, and check each point against the requirements.
  • Read a carpet plot, and find the lightest design that meets every requirement.
  • Trade a further parameter, the cruise altitude, by operating cost, and state the final design and what it leaves for the next iteration.

The sizing matrix

Module 5 chose the wing loading and the thrust-to-weight ratio from statistical constraints, before the aircraft existed. Now each choice can be tested properly: for each pair of \(W/S\) and \(T/W\), close the weight loop of Lesson 1, then check the requirements with that design's own polar, weights and field lengths. Each point is a complete aircraft. Raymer [1] calls the grid a sizing matrix; drawn with the takeoff weight against one parameter for several values of the other, and the requirements as boundaries, it becomes a carpet plot.

The requirements every point is checked against

Cruise at Mach 0.80 and the cruise altitude, with 300 ft/min to spare there (the cruise ceiling); 2.4% climb gradient with one engine failed (§25.121 of [3]); takeoff field length at most 1200 m; landing distance from 50 ft at most 840 m at \(0.9\,W_0\); the mission's fuel in the wing's tanks; 800 kg over 4600 km (built into the sizing).

Two changes open the design space. Double-slotted flaps raise the landing \(C_{L\max}\) from 2.1 to about 2.4 (Module 10's comparison of flap types), which raises the landing limit on wing loading from 202 to 231 kg/m², at a cost in flap weight and complexity that the group weights do not include. And the cruise altitude, held at 12 km since Module 2, can rise: Lesson 3 showed what that saves.

Example 8.1 — One point of the matrix

At 13 km, with double-slotted flaps, \(W/S = 230\) kg/m² and \(T/W = 0.39\), the loop closes at \(W_0 = 9781\) kg with \(S = 42.53\) m². The design's own requirements: \(T/W\) of 0.357 to cruise, 0.386 for the ceiling, 0.215 with an engine failed, 0.302 for takeoff; field length 991 m, landing 836 m; fuel 4.22 m³ in 5.13 m³ of tanks. Does it meet them all, and which requirement is closest to binding?

Show solution

Yes: 0.39 exceeds every \(T/W\) required, 991 m is under 1200, 836 m is under 840, and the fuel fits. Two requirements are nearly binding: the ceiling (0.386 against 0.39) and the landing (836 against 840). The best design lies where both are just met: the corner of the feasible region, where the black line meets the red one in the figure. Shrinking the engines to exactly 0.3859 there saves 48 kg: \(W_0 = 9733\) kg. Pushing \(W/S\) to the landing limit itself, 231.5 kg/m², would save another 80 kg; the final design keeps 230 and a few metres of landing margin.

The carpet's shape explains the design. Along the black line, a higher wing loading means a smaller, lighter wing that cruises nearer its best lift coefficient, so the takeoff weight falls steadily as \(W/S\) rises; the landing limit stops it. Above the black line, extra thrust only adds weight. Below it, the aircraft cannot reach its cruise ceiling. With the old flaps and 12 km, the landing limit falls at 202 kg/m², and meeting it costs nearly 4000 kg.

The cruise altitude

Higher cruise saves fuel (Lesson 3), but the 300 ft/min requirement moves up with it, and needs larger engines (Lesson 4). Each altitude gives a different sizing, and here weight and cost disagree: the takeoff weight keeps falling with altitude, while the price, after a shallow dip, rises with the engines.

Example 8.2 — Choosing the altitude

From the table: which cruise altitude would you choose by takeoff weight, by price, and by operating cost? Which would you pick, and why?

Show solution

By weight, 14 km (9456 kg); by price, 12.5 km ($16.91 million, with 12 and 13 km only $0.03 million dearer); by operating cost, 13 km ($5396 an hour), where the fuel saved by flying higher just balances the larger engines' price. The operating cost is what the customer pays, so 13 km. But the minimum is shallow: 12.5 to 13.5 km are within 0.4%, well inside the uncertainty of the cost model. A real program would also weigh what the cost model does not price: the engine's availability (13 km needs 18.4 kN, 14% more than an FJ44-4A [4]), the cabin pressure at higher altitude (Module 8), and the competition.

The final design

The business jet as the course leaves it: \(W/S = 230\) kg/m², just inside the landing limit with double-slotted flaps, \(T/W = 0.386\) for a 300 ft/min cruise ceiling at 13 km, and the loop closed.

It meets every requirement of the brief. It is 40% heavier than Module 3's first estimate, mostly because the real drag polar gives a cruise \(L/D\) of 11, not the 14.6 that estimate assumed; Module 11's gust loads, larger than the maneuvering loads at this wing loading, add about 430 kg to it. That is the most important lesson of the course: a conceptual design is a chain of estimates, and the weakest one, here a lift-to-drag ratio estimated from statistics in Module 3, propagates through everything until the loop is closed.

What the next iteration would change

The final design changes inputs that earlier modules held. Its wing is 51% larger than Module 6's and its engines 74% more powerful, so Module 12's group positions, loading diagram and wing placement would move; Module 13's scissor plot would be redrawn for the new wing (the loop held its volume coefficients, which already grow the tails to 12.5 and 6.8 m²); Module 8's gear would carry 9.7 t instead of 6.9, with larger tires; Module 7's engine would be a new rubber size. Module 11's design loads, by contrast, are already in this loop: the gust load factor is recomputed for each design's own wing, and at the final design's 230 kg/m² the gust gives \(n = 3.38\), \(N_z = 5.07\), against 4.29 from the maneuvers of §25.337 [3] alone. The others would each change the weights and drag a little, and the loop would turn again. Each turn changes less than the one before; a real program stops when the changes fall inside the estimates' own uncertainty, then moves on to preliminary design with wind tunnels, finite elements and suppliers' quotes.

What the course has built

From a mission in Module 2 to a closed design here: a first weight (3), a wing (4), a design point (5), a layout (6), engines (7), landing gear and systems (8), a drag polar (10), the design loads and structure (11), a weight statement and balance (12), tails sized for stability and control (13), and the performance and cost that close the loop (14). Each module's tool works for the other aircraft too; the Design Closure tool runs the whole loop for any choice of wing loading, thrust, flaps and cruise altitude.

Key takeaways

  • A sizing matrix closes the design at every combination of \(W/S\) and \(T/W\); a carpet plot shows the takeoff weight with the requirements as boundaries.
  • The lightest acceptable design lies at a corner of the feasible region: for the business jet, the landing limit and the cruise ceiling.
  • Better flaps (\(C_{L\max,L}\) 2.4) and a 13 km cruise make every requirement attainable; the altitude is chosen by operating cost.
  • The final design: 9733 kg, 42.3 m² of wing, two 18.4 kN engines, cruise \(L/D\) 11.1, $16.9 million, $5400 an hour.
  • The loop never quite ends: the next turn would revisit the CG, the tails, the gear and the engines for this aircraft.
