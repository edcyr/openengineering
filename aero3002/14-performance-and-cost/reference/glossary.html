A

Back to top
Absolute ceiling
The altitude at which the best rate of climb falls to zero: the low- and high-speed limits of the flight envelope meet, and level flight is possible at one speed only. The baseline's is 14.25 km at the start of cruise.
See: Lesson 2, Lesson 4Related: Flight envelope, Service ceiling, Cruise ceiling
Airborne distance takeoff
The distance from liftoff to the 35 ft screen: a flare, \(V_{LOF}^2/(g\sqrt2)\), then a climb at \(\gamma = T/W - 1/(L/D)\) to the screen (Torenbeek's method [2]). 291 m for the baseline.
See: Lesson 5Related: Ground roll, Screen height, Liftoff speed \(V_{LOF}\)
Approach speed \(V_A\)
The speed at which the aircraft crosses the 50 ft threshold, \(1.3\,V_S\) in the landing configuration. 53.8 m/s for the baseline at its landing weight.
See: Lesson 5Related: Touchdown speed \(V_{TD}\), Landing distance

B

Back to top
Balanced field length
The runway needed when the decision speed is chosen so that stopping after an engine failure and continuing on the remaining engines need the same distance. Torenbeek's estimate [2] uses the one-engine-out gradient, the braking and an inertia time. 1160 m for the baseline, 998 m for the final design.
See: Lesson 5Related: Decision speed \(V_1\), Takeoff field length, Inertia time \(t_i\)
Best climb speed
The speed for the greatest rate of climb. For a jet whose thrust does not change with speed it is faster than the minimum-drag speed (Lesson 4 gives the formula): 166 m/s for the baseline at sea level.
See: Lesson 4Related: Rate of climb, Minimum-drag speed \(V_{md}\), Climb angle \(\gamma\)
Best-range lift coefficient jet
A jet's range is best at the largest \(\sqrt{C_L}/C_D\), at \(C_L = \sqrt{C_{D0}/(3K)}\), a speed 1.32 times the minimum-drag speed. 0.363 for the baseline, reached at Mach 0.80 near 13.9 km.
See: Lesson 3Related: Specific air range, Breguet range equation, Minimum-drag speed \(V_{md}\)
Braking deceleration \(\bar a\)
The mean deceleration on the ground with the brakes on, as a fraction of g: 0.37 on dry concrete in Torenbeek's method [2], more with carbon brakes or thrust reversers.
See: Lesson 5Related: Landing distance, Balanced field length
Breguet range equation
\(R = (V/C)(L/D)\ln(W_1/W_2)\) for a jet: exact when \(V\), \(C\) and \(L/D\) are constant, as in a cruise-climb. At constant altitude it is applied in steps.
See: Lesson 3Related: Cruise-climb, Cruise in steps, Specific air range

C

Back to top
Carpet plot
A sizing matrix drawn as the takeoff weight against one parameter for several values of another (here \(W/S\) and \(T/W\)), with the requirements as boundaries. The lightest acceptable design lies at a corner of the region that meets them all.
See: Lesson 8Related: Sizing matrix, Trade study
Climb angle \(\gamma\)
\(\sin\gamma = (T - D)/W\). A jet climbs steepest at the minimum-drag speed: \(\sin\gamma_{\max} = T/W - 1/(L/D)_{\max}\), \(17.7^\circ\) for the baseline at sea level.
See: Lesson 4Related: Rate of climb, Minimum-drag speed \(V_{md}\)
Closed baseline
The business jet as Modules 3 to 13 defined it (\(W/S = 250\), \(C_{L\max,L} = 2.1\), cruise at 12 km), with the weight and thrust loops closed: 9240 kg, \(T/W = 0.346\). Consistent but not acceptable: its fuel does not quite fit and it lands long.
See: Lesson 1Related: Design loop, Final design
Crew cost
The crew's annual cost spread over the year's flights: \(n_{\text{crew}}c_{\text{crew}}/N_f\). Two pilots at $150 000 a year cost the baseline $4275 per design flight: more than its fuel.
See: Lesson 7Related: Direct operating cost, Utilization
Cruise ceiling
The altitude at which 300 ft/min (1.524 m/s) of climb remains. Module 5 asked for it at 12 km; with the real polar it sized the baseline's engines, \(T/W = 0.346\), and the final design's at 13 km, 0.386.
See: Lesson 4, Lesson 1Related: Service ceiling, Absolute ceiling, Thrust loop
Cruise in steps
A cruise at constant altitude and Mach divided into steps, each flown with Breguet's equation at the \(L/D\) of its middle weight, because \(C_L = W/(qS)\), and so \(L/D\), changes as fuel burns.
See: Lesson 1Related: Breguet range equation, Cruise-climb, Fuel fraction \(W_f/W_0\)
Cruise-climb
A cruise at constant \(C_L\) and Mach number, climbing slowly as fuel burns so that \(q\) falls with the weight: \(L/D\) stays constant and Breguet's equation is exact. It would take the baseline 12% farther than holding 12 km. Airliners approximate it with step climbs.
See: Lesson 3Related: Breguet range equation, Cruise in steps

D

Back to top
DAPCA IV
The RAND Corporation's model of the Development and Procurement Costs of Aircraft [1]: engineering, tooling, manufacturing and quality-control hours, development support, flight test and materials, from the empty weight, the maximum speed and the number built. Fitted to military aircraft; it overpredicts civil ones.
See: Lesson 6Related: Wrap rate, Recurring cost, Non-recurring cost, Learning curve
Decision speed \(V_1\)
The speed up to which the pilot stops after an engine failure and beyond which the takeoff continues. The balanced field length is the runway at the \(V_1\) that makes both need the same distance.
See: Lesson 5Related: Balanced field length
Depreciation
The loss of the aircraft's value over its life, per flight: \(P(1 - r_{\text{resale}})/(N_fY)\). $6603 per design flight for the baseline over 20 years with 40% resale.
See: Lesson 7Related: Interest, Direct operating cost, Utilization
Design loop
The cycle in which every estimate feeds the others: size, lay out, estimate the polar, fly the mission, weigh, check the requirements, repeat until the takeoff weight stops changing. Closed, it took the business jet from 6943 kg to 9240 kg; each further turn changes less.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 8Related: Weight loop, Thrust loop, Closed baseline
Direct operating cost
The costs that depend on the aircraft: ownership (depreciation, interest, insurance), crew, fuel, maintenance and fees. The baseline's design mission costs $32 300, $5032 a flight hour, with indirect costs of 20% added.
See: Lesson 7Related: Indirect costs, Depreciation, Utilization
Double-slotted flaps
Flaps with two slots, which raise the landing \(C_{L\max}\) from about 2.1 to 2.4 (Module 10) at a cost in weight and complexity that the group weights do not include. They raise the business jet's landing limit from 202 to 231.5 kg/m².
See: Lesson 8Related: Landing distance, Final design

E

Back to top
Effective friction coefficient \(\mu'\)
Torenbeek's lumping [2] of the rolling friction and the aerodynamic drag on the takeoff run: \(\mu' = \mu + 0.72\,C_{D0}/C_{L\max}\), with \(\mu = 0.02\) on dry concrete.
See: Lesson 5Related: Ground roll
Endurance
Time aloft on the fuel: for a jet \(E = (L/D)\ln(W_1/W_2)/C\), best at \((L/D)_{\max}\), the minimum-drag speed. The 45-minute loiter costs the baseline 2.5% of its weight.
See: Lesson 3Related: Breguet range equation, Minimum-drag speed \(V_{md}\)
Excess thrust
The thrust available less the drag: it climbs or accelerates the aircraft. At Mach 0.80 and 12 km the baseline has 550 N, exactly the 300 ft/min its engines were sized for.
See: Lesson 2, Lesson 4Related: Rate of climb, Thrust lapse \(\sigma\), Specific excess power \(P_s\)

F

Back to top
Final design
The business jet as the course leaves it: \(W/S = 230\) with double-slotted flaps, cruise at 13 km, \(T/W = 0.386\); 9733 kg, 42.3 m² of wing, two 18.4 kN engines, cruise \(L/D = 11.1\), $16.9 million, $5396 an hour. It meets every requirement.
See: Lesson 8Related: Closed baseline, Carpet plot, Double-slotted flaps
Flight envelope
The altitudes and speeds at which level flight is possible: the stall (or, high up, the thrust) on the low-speed side, thrust and wave drag on the high-speed side, meeting at the absolute ceiling. Structure and cabin pressure narrow a real one.
See: Lesson 2Related: Absolute ceiling, Maximum level speed
Flight-test cost
DAPCA IV's \(C_F = 1947\,W_e^{0.325}V^{0.822}N_{FT}^{1.21}\) (2012 dollars) for \(N_{FT}\) test aircraft: $62 million for the baseline's four.
See: Lesson 6Related: DAPCA IV, Non-recurring cost
Fuel fraction \(W_f/W_0\)
The fuel as a share of the takeoff weight: \(1.06(1 - W_{\text{end}}/W_0)\), with 6% for reserves and trapped fuel. With the real polar the business jet needs 0.360, not Module 3's 0.287.
See: Lesson 1Related: Cruise in steps, Weight loop
Fuel volume
The fuel the wing's tanks can hold. The baseline's 4.16 m³ (3328 kg) is just less than the 3329 kg its mission needs, so its full-payload range is 4598 km, not 4600; the final design's larger wing holds 5.10 m³.
See: Lesson 3Related: Payload-range diagram, Final design

G

Back to top
Glide
Flight with the engines at idle, flattest at \((L/D)_{\max}\), where \(\tan\gamma = 1/(L/D)\). From 12 km the baseline glides about 190 km.
See: Lesson 4Related: Endurance, Climb angle \(\gamma\)
Ground roll takeoff
The takeoff run to liftoff: \(s_G = V_{LOF}^2/[2g(T/W - \mu')]\), with the drag folded into the effective friction coefficient. 626 m for the baseline.
See: Lesson 5Related: Effective friction coefficient \(\mu'\), Liftoff speed \(V_{LOF}\), Airborne distance

I

Back to top
Indirect costs
The operator's costs not tied to the aircraft (administration, sales, ground facilities), taken here as 20% of the direct costs.
See: Lesson 7Related: Direct operating cost
Inertia time \(t_i\)
The time to recognize a failure and act, or to get the brakes and spoilers working after touchdown, during which the aircraft rolls at speed: 4.5 s in Torenbeek's method [2], as implemented in AeroSandbox [5].
See: Lesson 5, Lesson 5Related: Balanced field length, Landing distance
Insurance
In AeroSandbox's model [5], 2.5% of the price a year, scaled with the seats and the hours flown: \(0.025P\sqrt{(n_{\text{pax}} + n_{\text{crew}} + 1)/11}\sqrt{U/1200}\). $3314 per design flight for the baseline.
See: Lesson 7Related: Direct operating cost, Depreciation
Interest
The cost of the money tied up in the aircraft: for a loan repaid in equal payments over the life, \(P[iY/(1 - (1 + i)^{-Y}) - 1]\) in all, spread over every flight. $5190 per design flight for the baseline at 4%.
See: Lesson 7Related: Depreciation, Direct operating cost

L

Back to top
Landing distance
From 50 ft above the threshold to a stop: the air distance on a 3° approach and flare, \([(V_A^2 - V_{TD}^2)/(2g) + h_L]/(2\tan 3^\circ)\), plus the ground roll. 979 m for the baseline against 840 m required; it scales with \((W/S)/C_{L\max,L}\).
See: Lesson 5Related: Approach speed \(V_A\), Touchdown speed \(V_{TD}\), Braking deceleration \(\bar a\), Double-slotted flaps
Learning curve
The fall in hours per aircraft as a workforce gains experience. DAPCA IV's \(Q^{0.641}\) makes the average manufacturing hours fall by 22% each time the run doubles: a 78% curve.
See: Lesson 6Related: DAPCA IV, Recurring cost
Liftoff speed \(V_{LOF}\)
The speed at which the wheels leave the ground: \(V_2\max[(1 + \gamma\sqrt2)^{-1/2}, 1.2/1.3]\) in Torenbeek's method. 58.2 m/s for the baseline.
See: Lesson 5Related: Takeoff safety speed \(V_2\), Ground roll, Airborne distance

M

Back to top
Maintenance cost
Airframe and engine maintenance per flight, scaled with the price in AeroSandbox's model [5]: \((P/3\times10^6)[(65t + 65) + (58t + 50)]\). $4577 per design flight for the baseline.
See: Lesson 7Related: Direct operating cost
Maximum level speed
The speed at which the thrust available equals the drag, on the right of the drag curve. The baseline reaches Mach 0.83 at 12 km, held there by wave drag.
See: Lesson 2Related: Thrust required, Thrust lapse \(\sigma\), Wave drag
Minimum-drag speed \(V_{md}\)
The speed at \(C_L^* = \sqrt{C_{D0}/K}\), where the drag is least and \(L/D\) greatest: \(V_{md} = \sqrt{2W/(\rho SC_L^*)}\). 153 m/s (Mach 0.52) for the baseline at 12 km.
See: Lesson 2Related: Thrust required, Best-range lift coefficient, Endurance

N

Back to top
Non-recurring cost
Costs paid once for the whole program: engineering, tooling, development support and flight test. Spread over the run, they fall as \(1/Q\): $11.1 million an aircraft for 100 of the baseline, $2.9 million for 500.
See: Lesson 6, Lesson 6Related: Recurring cost, DAPCA IV

P

Back to top
Payload-range diagram
Payload against range: the maximum payload at the maximum takeoff weight, then payload traded for fuel until the tanks are full, then the ferry range with no payload. The baseline's tanks fill before its takeoff weight is reached.
See: Lesson 3Related: Fuel volume, Breguet range equation
Price
The manufacturer's cost per aircraft plus a margin, 15% here. The baseline's: $15.44 million at 500 aircraft; it drives the owning costs.
See: Lesson 6Related: DAPCA IV, Depreciation
Price index
The ratio of consumer price indices that brings a cost from one year's dollars to another's. DAPCA IV's 2012 dollars are multiplied by 1.327 for 2024, the value AeroSandbox uses [5].
See: Lesson 6Related: DAPCA IV, Wrap rate

R

Back to top
Rate of climb
\(\text{RoC} = V(T - D)/W\). The baseline's best is 7600 ft/min at sea level with the thrust held at its static value (about 5000 with thrust falling with speed), 924 at 12 km.
See: Lesson 4Related: Best climb speed, Climb angle \(\gamma\), Specific excess power \(P_s\)
Recurring cost
Costs paid for each aircraft: manufacturing labor, quality control, materials, and the engines, avionics and interior bought in. They fall slowly with quantity through learning.
See: Lesson 6Related: Non-recurring cost, Learning curve

S

Back to top
Screen height
The obstacle height the field lengths are measured to or from: 35 ft (10.7 m) for a transport's takeoff, 50 ft (15.2 m) for its landing.
See: Lesson 5, Lesson 5Related: Airborne distance, Landing distance
Service ceiling
The altitude at which 100 ft/min (0.508 m/s) of climb remains: 13.95 km for the baseline at the start of cruise, 14.7 km for the final design.
See: Lesson 4Related: Absolute ceiling, Cruise ceiling
Sizing matrix
A grid of complete designs: the loop closed at each combination of two parameters, here \(W/S\) and \(T/W\), each checked against every requirement.
See: Lesson 8Related: Carpet plot, Trade study
Specific air range
The distance per kilogram of fuel at the present weight: \(V(L/D)/(CW)\). The baseline's, halfway through its cruise at Mach 0.80: 1.84 km/kg at 12 km, 2.19 at 14 km.
See: Lesson 3Related: Best-range lift coefficient, Breguet range equation
Specific excess power \(P_s\)
\(P_s = V(T - D)/W\): the rate at which the aircraft can raise its energy height \(h + V^2/(2g)\), by climbing or accelerating.
See: Lesson 4Related: Rate of climb, Excess thrust

T

Back to top
Takeoff field length
The greater of the balanced field length and 115% of the all-engines distance to 35 ft ([3], §25.113). 1160 m for the baseline, against 1200 m required.
See: Lesson 5Related: Balanced field length, Airborne distance, Ground roll
Takeoff safety speed \(V_2\)
The speed to be reached by the 35 ft screen, taken as \(1.3\,V_S\) in the takeoff configuration in Torenbeek's method [2].
See: Lesson 5Related: Liftoff speed \(V_{LOF}\), Screen height
Thrust lapse \(\sigma\)
The model of a jet's thrust at altitude used since Module 5: \(T = T_0\sigma\), \(\sigma = \rho/\rho_0\). Simple and slightly optimistic at high Mach; it keeps the static thrust at any speed.
See: Lesson 2Related: Maximum level speed, Cruise ceiling
Thrust loop
Resizing the engines to the requirements checked with the closed design's own polar, then closing the weight loop again. For the baseline, the 300 ft/min ceiling raised \(T/W\) from 0.31 to 0.346 and \(W_0\) from 8898 to 9240 kg.
See: Lesson 1Related: Weight loop, Cruise ceiling, Design loop
Thrust required
The drag in level flight, \(qSC_{D0} + KW^2/(qS) + qSC_{D,\text{wave}}\): parasite drag growing with \(V^2\), induced drag falling with \(1/V^2\), and wave drag near the drag-divergence Mach number.
See: Lesson 2Related: Minimum-drag speed \(V_{md}\), Maximum level speed, Wave drag
Time to climb
\(t = \int dh/\text{RoC}\); with the rate falling linearly to the absolute ceiling \(H\), \(t = (H/R_0)\ln[1/(1 - h/H)]\). The baseline takes 13.8 min and 219 kg to reach 12 km.
See: Lesson 4Related: Rate of climb, Absolute ceiling
Touchdown speed \(V_{TD}\)
The speed at which the wheels meet the runway, taken as \(1.15\,V_S\): 47.6 m/s for the baseline.
See: Lesson 5Related: Approach speed \(V_A\), Landing distance
Trade study
A systematic comparison of designs that differ in one or more parameters, each closed and checked, to find the best by a chosen measure: here the takeoff weight for \(W/S\) and \(T/W\), and the operating cost for the cruise altitude.
See: Lesson 8, Lesson 8Related: Sizing matrix, Carpet plot, Final design

U

Back to top
Utilization
The hours an aircraft flies a year. Fixed costs (ownership, insurance, crew) per hour fall as it rises: the baseline costs $5032 an hour at 450 h a year, $3344 at 900.
See: Lesson 7Related: Direct operating cost, Depreciation, Crew cost

W

Back to top
Wave drag
The drag of shock waves near and beyond the drag-divergence Mach number (Module 10), which sets the business jet's maximum level speed near Mach 0.83.
See: Lesson 2Related: Maximum level speed, Thrust required
Weight loop
Repeating the sizing equation with the design's own fuel fraction and group weights until the takeoff weight stops changing. At \(T/W = 0.31\) the business jet's converged from 6943 kg to 8898 kg, each step moving 60% of the way.
See: Lesson 1Related: Design loop, Thrust loop, Fuel fraction \(W_f/W_0\)
Wrap rate
The cost of an hour of labor with overheads and benefits included: in DAPCA IV, $115 for engineering, $118 for tooling, $98 for manufacturing and $108 for quality control, in 2012 dollars [1], [5].
See: Lesson 6Related: DAPCA IV, Price index
