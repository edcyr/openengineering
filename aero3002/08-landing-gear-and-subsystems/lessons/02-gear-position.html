Placing the Gear

Where the wheels touch the ground decides whether the aircraft can rotate for takeoff, whether it sits on its tail when the passengers board at the back, whether the nose wheel can steer and whether a fast turn tips it over. This lesson turns those questions into four angles and a load share, and places the business jet's nose and main gear.

Learning objectives

  • Find the tail-down angle and the tip-back angle from a side view, and state the guidelines for each.
  • Place the main gear and the nose gear so the nose wheel carries a suitable share of the weight over the whole CG range.
  • Compute the lateral tip-over angle and the smallest track that meets the guideline.
  • Check the ground clearance of the wing tips, nacelles and propellers.

What the gear is placed against

The gear is placed against the aircraft's CG range: the CG moves as fuel burns and as passengers and baggage are loaded, and the gear must work at both ends. The CG range comes from the weight and balance of Module 12, but the gear cannot wait for it, so a first layout assumes one. Take the business jet's CG between 15% and 35% of the mean aerodynamic chord (MAC) and 0.10 m below the fuselage axis, a typical range for its class. With the MAC's leading edge at station 6.471 m and a MAC of 2.015 m (Module 6), the forward and aft limits are

\[ x_{cg,f} = 6.471 + 0.15(2.015) = 6.773\ \text{m}, \qquad x_{cg,a} = 6.471 + 0.35(2.015) = 7.177\ \text{m} \]

Stations \(x\) are measured aft from the nose, as in Module 6. The other inputs are the fuselage's underside, which sets the room to rotate, and the height of the CG above the ground, which grows with the length of the legs.

Rotation, tipping back and the nose gear's share

Three guidelines, after Chapter 11 of [1], fix the main gear's station and the wheelbase \(B = x_m - x_n\):

  • The tail-down angle \(\theta_{TD}\) is the angle the aircraft can rotate about its main wheels before the fuselage touches the runway. It must exceed the attitude at liftoff and at touchdown; typical values are 10 to 15°.
  • The tip-back angle \(\theta_{tb}\), measured from the vertical through the main wheels to the aft CG, must be at least 15° [7], and larger than the tail-down angle, so that an aircraft rotated onto its tail falls back onto its nose wheel.
  • The nose gear's share of the weight should stay between about 8 and 15% over the CG range. Below about 5% the nose wheel cannot steer; above about 20% the aircraft is hard to rotate and the nose gear grows heavy [7].

The side-view guidelines

\[ \tan\theta_{TD} = \frac{h}{x - x_m}, \qquad \tan\theta_{tb} = \frac{x_m - x_{cg,a}}{h_{cg}}, \qquad \frac{W_n}{W} = \frac{x_m - x_{cg}}{B} \]

Here \(h\) is the height above the ground of the critical point on the fuselage at station \(x\) (for a straight underside, the start of the tail upsweep), \(h_{cg}\) the height of the CG, and \(W_n\) the static load on the nose gear. The tail-down angle is the smallest such angle over the fuselage and nacelles behind the main wheels.

The guidelines pull against each other. The main wheels must be far enough behind the aft CG to give the tip-back angle and the minimum nose share, but every centimeter further aft shortens the distance to the tail upsweep, which lowers the tail-down angle unless the legs grow, and raises the nose share at the forward CG. The CG range itself sets the spread of the nose share: \(\Delta(W_n/W) = (x_{cg,a} - x_{cg,f})/B\), so a long CG range needs a long wheelbase.

Example 2.1 — The business jet's wheelbase and main gear

Place the business jet's main and nose gear so the nose gear carries 8% of the weight at the aft CG and 15% at the forward CG. Then find the height of the fuselage's underside above the ground that gives a tail-down angle of 12°, given that the underside is straight back to the tail upsweep at station 9.911 m. Check the tip-back angle.

Show solution

The spread of the nose share fixes the wheelbase, and the aft-CG share then fixes the main gear:

\[ B = \frac{x_{cg,a} - x_{cg,f}}{0.15 - 0.08} = \frac{0.404}{0.07} = 5.76\ \text{m}, \qquad x_m = 7.177 + 0.08(5.76) = 7.64\ \text{m} \]

Round to \(x_m = 7.65\ \text{m}\) and \(x_n = 1.80\ \text{m}\) (\(B = 5.85\ \text{m}\)); the shares are then 8.1% and 15.0%. For a tail-down angle of 12° the underside must be at least

\[ h_b = (9.911 - 7.65)\tan 12^\circ = 0.481\ \text{m} \]

above the ground; take 0.50 m, which gives 12.5°. The fuselage's axis is then 0.50 + 0.80 = 1.30 m high and the CG, 0.10 m below it, 1.20 m high:

\[ \tan\theta_{tb} = \frac{7.65 - 7.177}{1.20} = 0.395, \qquad \theta_{tb} = 21.6^\circ \]

which is more than 15° and more than the tail-down angle.

Tipping over sideways

In a fast turn on the ground, or with a gust under one wing, the aircraft can roll over the line joining the nose wheel to a main wheel. The lateral tip-over angle \(\psi\) measures how far the CG sits inside that line compared with its height: seen along the line, it is the angle at the ground between the vertical and the CG. It should be at most about 63° (54° for carrier aircraft) [1], [7].

The lateral tip-over angle

\[ \tan\delta = \frac{y_m}{B}, \qquad d = (x_{cg} - x_n)\sin\delta, \qquad \tan\psi = \frac{h_{cg}}{d} \]

\(y_m\) is half the track (the distance from the centerline to the main wheels), \(\delta\) the angle of the tipping line to the centerline, and \(d\) the distance in the ground plane from the CG to that line. The forward CG is the critical one: it is closest to the nose wheel, where the line passes nearest the centerline.

Example 2.2 — The business jet's track

The business jet's main legs hang from the wing 1.60 m either side of the centerline (track 3.20 m). Find the tip-over angle at the forward CG, and the smallest track that would meet 63°.

Show solution
\[ \delta = \arctan\frac{1.60}{5.85} = 15.3^\circ, \qquad d = (6.773 - 1.80)\sin 15.3^\circ = 1.312\ \text{m} \] \[ \psi = \arctan\frac{1.20}{1.312} = 42.4^\circ \]

For 63° the distance must be at least \(d = 1.20/\tan 63^\circ = 0.611\ \text{m}\), so \(\sin\delta = 0.611/4.973\), \(\delta = 7.05^\circ\) and

\[ y_m = 5.85\tan 7.05^\circ = 0.724\ \text{m}, \qquad \text{track} = 1.45\ \text{m} \]

The tip-over angle does not set the business jet's track; the wing structure and the place the gear retracts into do (Lesson 6).

Ground clearance

The gear's height must also keep the rest of the aircraft off the ground:

  • Wing tips and nacelles in a bank. The aircraft must be able to land with some bank, at least 5° for certification [2] and more for crosswind landings, without touching a wing tip or an engine. The angle is measured about the main wheels.
  • Engines under the wing. A nacelle low over the runway swallows stones and water; a clearance of about a quarter of the fan diameter is typical [2]. Large modern fans are one reason airliner legs have grown.
  • Propellers. CS/FAR 25.925 [3], [4] asks for at least 7 in (0.18 m) between the propeller and the ground with the gear statically deflected (9 in for a tail-wheel aircraft), and some clearance with a flat tire and a bottomed strut.

Example 2.3 — The business jet's wing tips

The wing's mid-plane at the tip is 0.207 m below the fuselage axis (low wing, 3° dihedral, semispan 7.48 m), and the tip section is 12% thick on a 0.97 m chord. How far can the business jet bank about its main wheels before a wing tip touches?

Show solution

The underside of the tip is half its thickness, \(0.5(0.12)(0.97) = 0.058\ \text{m}\), below the mid-plane. The axis is 1.30 m above the ground, so the underside of the tip is \(1.30 - 0.207 - 0.058 = 1.03\ \text{m}\) up, 7.48 − 1.60 = 5.88 m outboard of the main wheels:

\[ \varphi = \arctan\frac{1.03}{5.88} = 10.0^\circ \]

which is comfortably more than 5°. The rear engines sit high on the tail cone and never come near the ground.

Key takeaways

  • The gear is placed against the CG range; for the business jet, 15 to 35% MAC, stations 6.773 to 7.177 m, with the CG 1.20 m above the ground.
  • Tail-down angle 10 to 15°; tip-back angle at least 15° and more than the tail-down angle; nose share about 8 to 15%, never below 5% or above 20%.
  • The CG range sets the wheelbase: \(B \ge (x_{cg,a} - x_{cg,f})/\Delta(W_n/W)\); the business jet's gear is at stations 1.80 and 7.65 m, \(B = 5.85\ \text{m}\).
  • The lateral tip-over angle should be at most about 63°; the business jet's is 42°, with a 3.2 m track.
  • The legs must also clear the wing tips in a bank, the nacelles and the propellers.
