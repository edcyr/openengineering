Tire Selection

Aircraft tires come in standard sizes, each rated for a load at an inflation pressure and a speed. The designer estimates the size from statistics, then picks a real tire from a manufacturer's catalog that carries the loads of Lesson 3 with the pressure the runways allow. This lesson reads the catalog, applies the certification rules, and chooses the business jet's tires.

Learning objectives

  • Read an aircraft tire's size designation and its catalog data.
  • Estimate a tire's diameter and width from the load on the wheel.
  • Choose main and nose tires that meet the rated-load, braking and speed rules.
  • Relate the tire's load, inflation pressure and footprint, and find its deflection.

Reading the catalog

A tire's name gives its size in inches. A three-part name such as H22x8.25-10 gives the outside diameter (22 in), the section width (8.25 in) and the rim diameter (10 in); older names give the width and rim only (Type III, such as 6.00-6, the low-pressure tires of light aircraft) or the diameter and width only (Type VII, such as 18x4.4, high-pressure tires for jets). An R marks a radial tire. The ply rating is an index of the casing's strength, not a count of plies: the same size comes in several ply ratings, each with its own rated load, pressure and rated speed.

The rated load is the largest static load at the rated inflation; the catalog also gives the largest braking load (1.5 times the rated load) and the static loaded radius, the distance from the axle to the ground under the rated load. The difference between half the outside diameter and the static loaded radius is the tire's deflection.

A first estimate from statistics

Before the loads are known well enough to open a catalog, a statistical fit gives the size of the wheel needed for the load \(W_w\) on each wheel.

Statistical tire size

\[ D \ \text{or}\ w = A\,W_w^{\,B} \qquad (D, w \text{ in inches; } W_w \text{ in pounds}) \]

\(D\) is the outside diameter, \(w\) the section width; \(A\) and \(B\) come from Table 4.2. The fits describe the main tires; nose tires are smaller, typically 60 to 100% of the main tires' size.

Example 4.1 — A first size for the business jet's tires

Each business jet main wheel carries 7036 lb and the nose wheel 2294 lb (Lesson 3). Estimate the tire sizes with the business-twin fit.

Show solution
\[ D_m = 2.69(7036)^{0.251} = 24.9\ \text{in}, \qquad w_m = 1.170(7036)^{0.216} = 7.9\ \text{in} \] \[ D_n = 2.69(2294)^{0.251} = 18.8\ \text{in}, \qquad w_n = 1.170(2294)^{0.216} = 6.2\ \text{in} \]

About a 25 by 8 in main tire and a 19 by 6 in nose tire; the catalog now finds real ones.

The rules a tire must meet

CS/FAR 25.733 [3], [4] sets the loads a transport's tires must carry; other categories have similar rules:

  • Main tires: the load from the most critical combination of weight and CG may not exceed the rated load. On an axle with more than one wheel, the rating may be multiplied by 1.07.
  • Nose tires: the static load at the most critical CG may not exceed the rated load, and in braking (0.31 g at the maximum landing weight, 0.20 g at the maximum ramp weight) the load may not exceed 1.5 times the rated load.
  • Speed: the rated speed must exceed the highest ground speed at liftoff or touchdown, including tail winds and hot, high airports.

Two other considerations choose among the tires that pass. Aircraft grow heavier over their lives, so it pays to pick a size that a higher ply rating can later carry heavier. And the wheel must be big enough to hold the brake, whose heat sink must absorb a rejected takeoff, so a tiny high-pressure tire on a small rim is rarely the answer for a main wheel.

Example 4.2 — The business jet's tires

Choose the business jet's main and nose tires from the catalog. The main tires carry 7036 lb each; the nose tire 2294 lb at rest and 3267 lb braking at 0.31 g.

Show solution

The Cessna Citation CJ3 uses the H22x8.25-10 main tire in its 12-ply version, rated for 6900 lb [5]: too little for 7036 lb. The 14-ply version of the same size, rated for 8300 lb at 156 psi, carries it at 85% of its rating; it is the tire of the heavier CJ4 [5]. For the nose, the 18x4.4 in its 6-ply version (2100 lb) is too weak, but the 10-ply version, the CJ3's own nose tire, carries

\[ 2294 \le 3550\ \text{lb}, \qquad 3267 \le 1.5(3550) = 5325\ \text{lb} \]

Both are rated for 190 mph or more, well above the business jet's speeds on the runway. The main tires are close to the statistical estimate; the nose tire is 18 by 4.4 in, narrower than the estimate's 6.2 in.

Inflation and the footprint

A loaded tire flattens until its footprint, pressed at the inflation pressure, carries the load. The footprint's area is therefore about the load divided by the pressure, and a tire carrying less than its rated load is inflated less in proportion, which keeps its deflection and footprint area about the same.

Load, pressure and footprint

\[ W_w \approx p\,A_p, \qquad p = p_{\text{rated}}\,\frac{W_w}{W_{\text{rated}}}, \qquad S_T = \frac{D}{2} - R_{\text{SLR}} \]

\(A_p\) is the footprint area, \(p\) the inflation pressure, \(S_T\) the deflection under the load and \(R_{\text{SLR}}\) the static loaded radius.

The pressure is what a runway feels. A concrete runway at a major airport typically takes 200 psi or more; grass, gravel and soft strips need much lower pressures, which means bigger tires for the same load. This is why bush aircraft have balloon tires, and why the runways an aircraft must use enter the tire choice.

Example 4.3 — Inflating the business jet's main tires

At what pressure should the business jet's main tires be inflated for 7036 lb, and what are their footprint area and deflection? The H22x8.25-10 (14 ply) has a static loaded radius of 9.14 in.

Show solution
\[ p = 156\,\frac{7036}{8300} = 132\ \text{psi}\ (0.91\ \text{MPa}), \qquad A_p = \frac{8300}{156} = 53.2\ \text{in}^2\ (343\ \text{cm}^2) \] \[ S_T = \frac{22}{2} - 9.14 = 1.86\ \text{in} = 47\ \text{mm} \]

The deflection is used in Lesson 5: the tire absorbs part of the landing's energy before the shock absorber does.

Key takeaways

  • Tire names give the size in inches; the ply rating indexes the strength, and each size and ply has a rated load, inflation and speed.
  • Statistics give a first size: \(D\) or \(w = A W_w^B\), with \(W_w\) in pounds and \(D\), \(w\) in inches.
  • Main tires: load ≤ rated load (× 1.07 on multiple-wheel axles). Nose tires: static load ≤ rated, braking load ≤ 1.5 × rated. Rated speed above the highest ground speed.
  • The footprint area is about the load over the pressure; inflation falls in proportion to the load; the deflection is \(D/2 - R_{\text{SLR}}\).
  • The business jet: H22x8.25-10 main tires (14 ply, 132 psi, 47 mm deflection) and an 18x4.4 nose tire (10 ply).
