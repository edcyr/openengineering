Retraction and Stowage

A retractable gear needs somewhere to go: a bay in the wing, the fuselage or a nacelle, with structure around the cutout, doors to close it and a mechanism to fold the leg in and lock it down again. This lesson surveys where gears go and how they fold, checks that the business jet's main wheels fit in its wing roots, and draws the aircraft on its gear.

Learning objectives

  • Describe where main and nose gears stow on low-wing, high-wing and propeller aircraft.
  • Describe the mechanisms that fold a gear and lock it down.
  • State the requirements for emergency extension and warnings, and why the direction of retraction matters.
  • Check that a retracted wheel fits in the space available.

Where the gear goes

  • In the wing and wing root. On low-wing aircraft the main legs hang from the wing, usually behind the rear spar, and fold inward so the wheels lie in the wing root or in a fairing under the fuselage: the A320, the 737 and most business jets. Airliners often add a kink in the inboard trailing edge to make room behind the rear spar for the gear's pivot.
  • In the fuselage. A high wing is too far from the ground for its legs, so the gear folds into the fuselage or into pods (sponsons) on its sides: the ATR 72, the C-130, the A400M. The track is then limited by the fuselage's width, which makes tip-over the critical check.
  • In a nacelle. Twin turboprops often fold the main gear into the engine nacelles, where the legs are short and the structure is already there: the Dash 8, the King Air.
  • The nose gear folds forward or aft into the fuselage under the cockpit floor.

The bay must hold the wheel with room to spare: tires grow in service as their casings stretch, and the manufacturers publish growth allowances and minimum clearances to the structure. Hot brakes and the risk of a tire bursting in the bay also keep wiring, hydraulic lines and fuel away from it.

Folding and locking

The simplest retraction swings the leg about a single pivot; a skewed pivot axis can turn the wheel as it swings, so it ends flat or edgewise as the bay requires. Many gears add a four-bar linkage, which moves the wheel along a curve no single pivot can, or shortens the leg as it retracts. A side brace or drag brace carries the loads on the ground and folds in the middle as the gear retracts; an over-center down-lock holds it straight. An actuator, usually hydraulic, drives the motion.

CS/FAR 25.729 asks for an emergency means of extending the gear after any reasonably probable failure of the retraction system, including the loss of any single hydraulic or electric power source, and for a position indicator and an aural warning if a landing is attempted with the gear not locked down. Most gears meet the first by free fall: the uplocks are released and the gear falls under its own weight. A gear that retracts forward, against the airflow, falls back with the help of the air loads; one that retracts with the airflow must overcome them, so heavier springs or assist actuators are needed.

Example 6.1 — Does the business jet's main wheel fit?

The business jet's main leg hangs from a pivot 0.68 m above the ground at 1.60 m from the centerline, and its H22x8.25-10 wheel's axle is 0.232 m (the static loaded radius) above the ground. The leg folds inward through 90°. Where does the wheel end up, and is the wing root deep enough to take it lying flat? The wing is 12% thick on its root chord of 2.77 m.

Show solution
\[ \ell = 0.68 - 0.232 = 0.45\ \text{m}, \qquad y_{\text{axle}} = 1.60 - 0.45 = 1.15\ \text{m} \]

The 22-in (0.559 m) wheel then spans 1.15 ± 0.28 m, from 0.87 to 1.43 m, just outside the fuselage's radius of 0.80 m: it lies in the wing root and the fairing under it. Lying flat it needs its width plus clearances, \(0.21 + 2(0.025) = 0.26\ \text{m}\), against a root depth of

\[ 0.12(2.77) = 0.33\ \text{m} \]

so it fits behind the rear spar, where the wing is a little thinner, with a small fairing to spare. The CJ series stows its main wheels the same way.

The business jet on its gear

The business jet's gear is now laid out: a nose gear at station 1.80 m folding forward into the nose, and main gears at 7.65 m on a 3.20 m track folding inward into the wing roots, each on one wheel. Figure 6.2 adds it to the three-view of Modules 6 and 7.

What the gear changes in the rest of the design

The gear's height fixed the fuselage's height above the runway (0.50 m under the belly), which sets the door sill and the stairs. Its bays cut into the wing root behind the rear spar and into the nose, where the structure must carry loads around them. And its weight, typically a few percent of the takeoff weight, is estimated with the other components in Module 11, where the real CG range will replace the 15 to 35% assumed here; if the CG moves, the gear moves with it.

Key takeaways

  • Low-wing aircraft fold the main gear inward into the wing root; high-wing aircraft into the fuselage or sponsons; twin turboprops often into the nacelles; the nose gear into the fuselage.
  • Pivots, skewed axes and four-bar linkages fold the leg; braces and over-center locks hold it down.
  • CS/FAR 25.729 requires an emergency extension and an aural warning; a gear retracting forward falls into place with the air loads.
  • The bay must take the wheel with clearance for tire growth: the business jet's main wheel lies flat in a root 0.33 m deep.
