Shock Absorbers

An aircraft touches down still sinking at a meter or more per second. The gear must stop that descent over a short distance without breaking the airframe or bouncing it back into the air. This lesson writes the energy balance that sizes the stroke, compares shock absorbers by their efficiency, and sizes the business jet's oleo struts.

Learning objectives

  • State the descent velocities the certification rules require the gear to absorb.
  • Define a shock absorber's efficiency and compare springs and oleo-pneumatic struts.
  • Compute the stroke from the energy balance, including the tire's share.
  • Estimate an oleo strut's diameter and length.

The energy of the touchdown

At touchdown the aircraft has a vertical velocity \(V_v\). The wing still carries about the aircraft's weight, so the gear need not hold the aircraft up while it compresses; it must absorb the kinetic energy of the descent, \(\tfrac{1}{2}(W/g)V_v^2\). The rules set \(V_v\):

  • CS/FAR 25.473 [3], [4]: a limit descent velocity of 10 ft/s (3.05 m/s) at the design landing weight, and 6 ft/s at the design takeoff weight, with the lift taken as no more than the weight.
  • CS/FAR 25.723 [3], [4]: in a reserve-energy test at 12 ft/s (3.66 m/s) at the design landing weight, the gear may not fail.

Military aircraft use larger values; carrier aircraft, which fly onto the deck without a flare, design for 20 ft/s or more.

Shock absorbers and their efficiency

A shock absorber stops the descent with a force on the airframe over its stroke. The energy it absorbs is the area under its force–stroke curve; its efficiency compares that area with the rectangle of the peak force times the stroke:

Shock-absorber efficiency

\[ \eta = \frac{\int_0^S F\,\mathrm{d}s}{F_{\max}\,S} \]

For the same energy and stroke, a more efficient absorber needs a lower peak force, and so a lighter airframe.

  • Leaf springs of steel or composite, as on many light aircraft: no maintenance, but the force grows linearly with the deflection (\(\eta \approx 0.5\)) and the stored energy throws the aircraft back up.
  • Rubber or bungee blocks: simple, with some damping.
  • Oleo-pneumatic struts, on almost every larger aircraft: a piston forces oil through an orifice, which dissipates the energy, while a column of nitrogen acts as the spring that carries the aircraft on the ground. Their force stays nearly constant over the stroke, so \(\eta\) is typically 0.75 to 0.9.
  • A trailing link puts the wheel on an arm hinged to the leg, with the strut between them. It absorbs bumps from the front as well as from below, and it is shorter: business jets often use it.

The stroke

The tire deflects too, by \(S_T\), with an efficiency \(\eta_T\) of about 0.47. If the gear's vertical force is held to \(N_g\) times the weight, the energy balance (Chapter 11 of [1]) is

\[ \tfrac{1}{2}\frac{W}{g}V_v^2 = N_g W\left(\eta S + \eta_T S_T\right) \]

The shock absorber's stroke

\[ S = \frac{V_v^2}{2g\,\eta\,N_g} - \frac{\eta_T}{\eta}\,S_T \]

\(N_g\) is the gear load factor, the largest vertical gear force over the weight; about 2 to 3 for transports and business aircraft, higher for military aircraft. Add a margin of about 25 mm (1 in) to the result. The weight cancels: the stroke depends on the descent velocity, the efficiency and the load factor chosen.

Example 5.1 — The business jet's stroke

Size the business jet's main gear stroke for 10 ft/s, with an oleo efficiency of 0.80, a gear load factor of 3, and the main tire's deflection of 47 mm (Lesson 4).

Show solution
\[ \frac{V_v^2}{2g} = \frac{3.048^2}{2(9.81)} = 0.4735\ \text{m} \] \[ S = \frac{0.4735}{0.80(3)} - \frac{0.47}{0.80}(0.0472) = 0.1973 - 0.0277 = 0.170\ \text{m} \]

With the 25 mm margin the stroke is 0.195 m, about 7.7 in.

Example 5.2 — The reserve-energy test

The business jet's struts have the 0.195 m stroke of Example 5.1. In the 12 ft/s test, the whole stroke is available. What gear load factor does the test reach?

Show solution

Solve the energy balance for \(N_g\):

\[ N_g = \frac{V_v^2/(2g)}{\eta S + \eta_T S_T} = \frac{3.658^2/(2 \times 9.81)}{0.80(0.195) + 0.47(0.0472)} = \frac{0.682}{0.178} = 3.83 \]

The energy grows with the square of the descent velocity, so the 20% faster descent needs 44% more energy, which the same stroke absorbs only at a 28% higher load. The gear and its attachments must survive about 3.8 times the weight.

Sizing the oleo strut

On the ground the nitrogen carries the static load, so the piston's area is the load over the gas pressure. A static pressure of about 1800 psi (12.4 MPa) is typical; the strut's outside diameter is about 1.3 times the piston's, and its length about 2.5 times the stroke, to leave room for the piston's bearings and the gas ([1], Chapter 11; [7]).

A first oleo strut

\[ d_p = \sqrt{\frac{4 L}{\pi P}}, \qquad D_{\text{oleo}} \approx 1.3\,d_p, \qquad L_{\text{oleo}} \approx 2.5\,S \]

\(L\) is the strut's load (the maximum static load for a main strut; the braking load for the nose), \(P\) the gas pressure.

Example 5.3 — The business jet's struts

Size the business jet's main strut for its 31 300 N, and the nose strut for its braking load of 14 530 N, at 12.4 MPa, with the 0.195 m stroke.

Show solution
\[ d_{p,m} = \sqrt{\frac{4(31\,300)}{\pi(12.4\times 10^6)}} = 0.057\ \text{m}, \qquad D_m = 1.3(0.057) = 0.074\ \text{m} \] \[ d_{p,n} = \sqrt{\frac{4(14\,530)}{\pi(12.4\times 10^6)}} = 0.039\ \text{m}, \qquad D_n = 0.050\ \text{m} \]

Both struts are about 2.5(0.195) = 0.49 m long. On a trailing-link main gear the strut lies along the link and the leg is shorter.

Large transports need longer strokes than the business jet: lower gear load factors (about 2) spare their heavy structure, and their struts travel about half a meter [2], the oil damping all the way and the gas spring stiffening over the last part.

Key takeaways

  • The gear absorbs the descent's kinetic energy, \(\tfrac{1}{2}(W/g)V_v^2\), with lift about equal to weight: 10 ft/s for the limit case, 12 ft/s without failure.
  • Efficiency \(\eta = \int F\,\mathrm{d}s/(F_{\max}S)\): about 0.5 for springs, 0.75 to 0.9 for oleo-pneumatic struts.
  • Stroke: \(S = V_v^2/(2g\eta N_g) - (\eta_T/\eta)S_T\), plus about 25 mm; the business jet's is 0.195 m.
  • Oleo: piston \(d_p = \sqrt{4L/(\pi P)}\) at about 12.4 MPa, outside diameter 1.3 \(d_p\), length 2.5 \(S\); the business jet's main strut is 74 mm across and about 0.49 m long.
