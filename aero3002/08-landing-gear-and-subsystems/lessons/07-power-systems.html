Hydraulic, Electrical and Pneumatic Power

Besides thrust, the engines supply the aircraft's secondary power: hydraulic pressure that moves the gear, flaps and control surfaces; electricity for the avionics, lights and galleys; and compressed air for the cabin and the ice protection. A conceptual design must find room and weight for these systems and decide how much power each takes. This lesson surveys them and sizes a few of their parts.

Learning objectives

  • Trace secondary power from its sources (engines, APU, batteries, ram-air turbine) to its users.
  • Size a hydraulic actuator from its force and the system pressure, and find a pump's power from its flow.
  • Explain why higher voltages and pressures save weight.
  • Describe bleed air, the APU, flight-control actuation and the more-electric aircraft.

Sources and users

Each engine drives an accessory gearbox carrying hydraulic pumps and electrical generators, and gives up some of its compressed air as bleed air. On the ground, or if the engines fail, an auxiliary power unit (APU), a small gas turbine usually in the tail cone, supplies air and electricity; batteries start it and carry the essential loads for a while; and many transports carry a ram-air turbine that drops into the airstream to drive a pump or a generator if everything else is lost. Every critical user is fed by more than one source, so that no single failure removes it.

Hydraulic power

Hydraulics move heavy loads quickly with light actuators: the gear, the flaps and slats, the spoilers, the brakes, the nose-wheel steering and, on most transports, the primary flight controls. Engine-driven pumps keep the fluid at a set pressure, traditionally 3000 psi (20.7 MPa); some recent large aircraft use 5000 psi (34.5 MPa) to make their actuators and pipes smaller. Transports have two or three independent systems, with the flight controls spread across them.

Actuators and pumps

\[ A = \frac{F}{p}, \qquad d = \sqrt{\frac{4A}{\pi}}, \qquad P = p\,Q \]

An actuator of piston area \(A\) at system pressure \(p\) gives a force \(F\); the pump delivering a flow \(Q\) at pressure \(p\) supplies the hydraulic power \(P\) (before its own losses).

Example 7.1 — An actuator at two pressures

A flap actuator must push with 20 kN. Find its bore at 3000 psi and at 5000 psi.

Show solution
\[ A_{3000} = \frac{20\,000}{20.7\times 10^6} = 9.67\ \text{cm}^2, \qquad d = 35.1\ \text{mm} \] \[ A_{5000} = \frac{20\,000}{34.5\times 10^6} = 5.80\ \text{cm}^2, \qquad d = 27.2\ \text{mm} \]

The higher pressure shrinks the piston's area by 40% and moves 40% less fluid for the same stroke, so the pipes, the fluid and the reservoir shrink too.

Example 7.2 — The business jet's gear actuators

Raising the business jet's main gear takes two actuators of 25 mm bore and 0.15 m stroke, and the nose gear one of the same size; the gear must be up in 6 s. What average flow and hydraulic power does that take at 3000 psi?

Show solution
\[ V = 3\,\frac{\pi(0.025)^2}{4}(0.15) = 2.21\times 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3 = 0.221\ \text{L} \] \[ Q = \frac{0.221}{6} = 0.0368\ \text{L/s}, \qquad P = (20.7\times10^6)(3.68\times10^{-5}) = 0.76\ \text{kW} \]

A small pump does it, and an accumulator can store the fluid to deliver the burst faster.

Electrical power

Light aircraft and business jets run on 28 V direct current from engine-driven generators, often starter-generators that also spin the engines up to start them, with batteries for starting and emergencies. Transports generate 115 V alternating current at 400 Hz, with transformer-rectifiers for the 28 V DC loads. The higher voltage matters because a cable's size is set by the current it carries, \(I = P/V\): the same power at a higher voltage needs less current and less copper. Electrical loads grow with every generation of avionics, cabin entertainment and galley equipment.

Example 7.3 — Voltage and cable weight

A 5 kW load is fed at 28 V DC or at 115 V. Compare the currents. For the same current density, how do the cables' cross-sections compare?

Show solution
\[ I_{28} = \frac{5000}{28} = 179\ \text{A}, \qquad I_{115} = \frac{5000}{115} = 43\ \text{A} \]

At the same current density the cross-section, and so the copper's weight per meter, is in proportion to the current: the 115 V cable needs 28/115 = 24% of the copper.

Bleed air and the APU

Air bled from the engines' compressors is hot and at high pressure: it pressurizes and air-conditions the cabin (Lesson 8), heats the wing and engine inlets against ice, and on many airliners starts the engines through air turbine starters. It is convenient but not free: air that the engine has compressed and then does not pass through its turbine costs fuel, more than the energy the cabin actually needs, because the bleed pressure is set by the worst flight condition and the excess is throttled away.

The APU supplies bleed air and electricity at the gate, so the main engines need not run, and starts them. Airliners carry one as a matter of course; many business jets do without, saving its weight and cost and relying on ground power carts.

Flight controls and the more-electric aircraft

Small aircraft move their control surfaces through cables and push rods. As speed and size grow, the pilot can no longer move the surfaces unaided, so hydraulic actuators take over, first boosting the pilot's effort and then, in fly-by-wire aircraft, moving the surfaces on electrical commands from flight computers. Fly-by-wire saves the weight of the cables and rods and lets the computers protect the aircraft's envelope; it needs multiple redundant computers, power supplies and actuators.

The more-electric aircraft replaces some hydraulic and pneumatic systems with electrical ones. The Boeing 787 takes no bleed air from its engines: electric compressors pressurize the cabin, the wing is heated electrically and some actuators are electric. It needs much larger generators, but its engines run more efficiently without bleed, and electrical wiring is easier to route, monitor and maintain than ducts.

The business jet's systems

The business jet takes the systems of its class: a starter-generator on each engine feeding a 28 V DC system, with batteries; one hydraulic system for the gear, flaps, spoilers and brakes, with an emergency means of lowering the gear (Lesson 6); manual flight controls through cables and rods, which suit its size and speed; and bleed air for the cabin and the inlets' ice protection. It has no APU. Module 12 adds the weights of these systems to the empty weight.

Key takeaways

  • Engines supply secondary power through gearbox-driven pumps and generators and bleed air; the APU, batteries and a ram-air turbine back them up.
  • Hydraulics at 3000 psi (some new aircraft 5000 psi): \(A = F/p\), pump power \(P = pQ\).
  • 28 V DC on small aircraft, 115 V 400 Hz AC on transports; a higher voltage carries the same power with less current and copper.
  • Bleed air pressurizes, air-conditions and de-ices, at a fuel cost; the more-electric 787 does without it.
