Landing Gear Arrangements

The landing gear is dead weight in flight, and in a few seconds at each end of the flight it carries the whole aircraft: it rolls, steers and brakes, absorbs the touchdown, and holds the aircraft up while it is loaded and fuelled. This lesson surveys the arrangements in use, explains why almost every modern aircraft rolls on a tricycle gear, and chooses the gear for the business jet.

Learning objectives

  • List what the landing gear must do on the ground and at touchdown.
  • Describe the main gear arrangements and the aircraft that use them.
  • Explain, with the forces on a swerving aircraft, why a tricycle gear is stable on the ground and a taildragger is not.
  • Weigh a fixed gear against a retractable one.

What the gear must do

  • Carry the weight on the ground at every weight and center-of-gravity (CG) position, without tipping over backward or sideways.
  • Absorb the touchdown. A transport must land at a vertical speed of 10 ft/s (3.05 m/s) at its design landing weight without damage [3], [4], which takes a shock absorber with a stroke of tens of centimeters (Lesson 5).
  • Roll, steer and brake. The tires must carry the loads at takeoff speed; the nose wheel steers; the brakes stop the aircraft on a wet runway and after a rejected takeoff.
  • Let the aircraft rotate at takeoff and flare at landing without striking its tail, and clear the ground with its engines, propellers and wing tips.
  • Get out of the way. On most aircraft the gear retracts, so it needs a place to go, a mechanism and a way to come down again if the power fails (Lesson 6).

A conceptual layout settles the arrangement, the position of each leg, the number and size of the wheels, the length of the legs and where they retract. Those decisions fix the fuselage's height above the runway and the wing structure around the gear, so they come early, before the weights of Module 12.

The arrangements

Table 1.1 Landing gear arrangements
ArrangementLayoutWhere it is used
Single mainOne main wheel under the CG, skids or small wheels at the nose and tail, the wing tips held off the groundSailplanes
Taildragger (conventional)Two main wheels ahead of the CG, a small tail wheel or skidEarly aircraft, the DC-3, many light, aerobatic and bush aircraft
TricycleTwo main wheels or legs just behind the CG, a steerable nose wheelNearly all modern aircraft, from light trainers to the narrow-bodies
BicycleTwo main legs on the centerline, ahead of and behind the CG, outriggers near the wing tipsThe Harrier, the U-2, the B-47: thin wings with no room for the gear
QuadricycleFour main legs at the corners of a rectangle under the fuselageThe B-52
Multi-bogeyA tricycle whose main legs carry bogies of four or six wheels, often with extra legs under the fuselageWide-bodies: the 747, the 777, the A380

Why the tricycle won

The deciding difference is what happens when the aircraft is not rolling quite straight, after a gust, an uneven brake or a crosswind touchdown. The tires resist sliding sideways, so the ground pushes sideways on the main wheels, while the aircraft's inertia acts at its CG and keeps it moving the way it was going. Those two forces form a couple:

  • With the main wheels behind the CG (tricycle), the side force at the wheels swings the tail back behind the CG, like the feathers on an arrow. The swerve dies out.
  • With the main wheels ahead of the CG (taildragger), the same side force swings the tail further out. The swerve grows, and if the pilot does not catch it with rudder and brake the aircraft spins round on the ground: a ground loop.

The tricycle also keeps the floor level for loading and the pilot's view clear over the nose, lets the pilot brake hard without tipping onto the nose, and starts the takeoff run at a low angle of attack and low drag. The taildragger's tail wheel is smaller and lighter than a nose gear, and its nose-high stance clears a large propeller and suits rough strips, which is why it survives on bush and aerobatic aircraft.

Example 1.1 — The moment that straightens a tricycle

A tricycle-gear aircraft's main wheels are 0.47 m behind its CG. During a swerve the ground pushes on the main wheels with a side force of 4.0 kN. What yawing moment does this give about the CG? If the same aircraft had a tail wheel and its main wheels 0.47 m ahead of the CG, what would change?

Show solution
\[ N = F_y\,(x_m - x_{cg}) = 4.0(0.47) = 1.88\ \text{kN m} \]

The moment turns the nose toward the direction of travel and cancels the swerve. With the main wheels ahead of the CG the moment has the same size and the opposite sense: it turns the nose further away and makes the swerve grow, the start of a ground loop.

Fixed or retractable

A fixed gear is light, simple and cheap, and it needs no bay, mechanism or power. Its drag, though, grows with the square of the speed: a fixed gear can be a large share of a light aircraft's parasite drag even with streamlined fairings over the wheels. A retractable gear removes that drag at the cost of the retraction mechanism, the doors, the bays in the wing or fuselage, the structure around the cutouts and an emergency means of extension. Below a cruise speed of roughly 150 to 200 knots the saving is small and fixed gears are common; faster aircraft almost always retract.

The business jet's gear

The business jet cruises at Mach 0.80, so its gear retracts. It takes the arrangement of its class: a retractable tricycle with one wheel on each leg, the nose gear in the nose and the main gear on the wing, folding inward toward the wing root. Its engines are on the tail cone, so the gear need not clear them, and its low wing keeps the main legs short. The next lessons place the legs (Lesson 2), find their loads (Lesson 3), choose the tires (Lesson 4), size the shock absorbers (Lesson 5) and find where the gear folds (Lesson 6).

Key takeaways

  • The gear carries the aircraft on the ground, absorbs the touchdown, rolls, steers and brakes, lets the aircraft rotate, and must retract and come down again.
  • The arrangements: single main, taildragger, tricycle, bicycle, quadricycle and multi-bogey; nearly every modern aircraft uses a tricycle, the largest with bogies.
  • With the main wheels behind the CG, the ground's side force straightens a swerve; with them ahead, it makes the swerve grow into a ground loop.
  • A retractable gear trades weight, cost and complexity for the drag of a fixed one; fast aircraft retract.
