Lesson 8 · 30 min

Pressurization, Air and Ice Protection

At 12 km the air outside is at a fifth of sea-level pressure and −56.5 °C. The cabin systems keep the people inside alive and comfortable: they hold the cabin at a moderate altitude, which loads the fuselage like a pressure vessel, they supply fresh air, and they keep ice off the surfaces that need to stay clean. This lesson sizes the business jet's pressurization and air supply and closes the module.

Learning objectives

  • State the cabin-altitude rules and compute the pressure differential across the fuselage.
  • Find the loads the differential puts on a door and the hoop stress in the skin.
  • Compute the fresh-air flow for the occupants and the power to compress it.
  • Describe oxygen systems and the methods of ice protection.

Cabin altitude and the pressure differential

CS/FAR 25.841 [3], [4] requires a pressurized cabin to stay at a cabin altitude of no more than 8000 ft (2438 m) in normal operation. After a probable failure the occupants may not be exposed to more than 15 000 ft. The FAA's rule adds that after a decompression from a failure that is not extremely improbable, the cabin may not exceed 25 000 ft for more than 2 minutes, nor 40 000 ft at all [3]. The fuselage then carries the pressure differential between the cabin and the outside air:

The pressure differential

\[ \Delta p = p_{\text{cabin}} - p(h), \qquad \sigma_{\text{hoop}} = \frac{\Delta p\,r}{t} \]

Both pressures come from the standard atmosphere. A fuselage of radius \(r\) and skin thickness \(t\) carries the differential with a circumferential (hoop) stress \(\sigma_{\text{hoop}}\), twice the longitudinal stress; every door and window carries \(\Delta p\) times its area.

Example 8.1 — The business jet's cabin

The business jet cruises at 12 km with an 8000 ft cabin. Find the pressure differential, the load on a 0.6 m by 1.3 m door, and the skin thickness that carries the hoop stress at 100 MPa, a low allowable that leaves room for fatigue and the other loads. The fuselage's radius is 0.80 m.

Show solution
\[ p_{\text{cabin}} = 101\,325(1 - 2.256\times10^{-5}(2438))^{5.256} = 75\,260\ \text{Pa} \] \[ p(12\ \text{km}) = 19\,330\ \text{Pa} \] \[ \Delta p = 55\,930\ \text{Pa}\ (8.11\ \text{psi}) \]

The door carries \(55\,930(0.6)(1.3) = 43.6\ \text{kN}\), the weight of about 4.4 tonnes, which is why doors are plugs that the pressure pushes against their frames. The skin needs

\[ t = \frac{\Delta p\,r}{\sigma} = \frac{55\,930(0.80)}{100\times10^6} = 0.45\ \text{mm} \]

Real skins are thicker, about 1 to 2 mm, for fatigue, damage tolerance and the bending loads; but every cycle of pressurization is a fatigue cycle, which is why fuselages are tested for many times their lives' flights.

Fresh air and the air-conditioning packs

CS/FAR 25.831 [3], [4] requires at least 0.55 lb (0.25 kg) of fresh air per minute for each occupant in normal operation, and limits carbon dioxide to 0.5% by volume and carbon monoxide to 1 part in 20 000. On most jets the air comes from the engines' compressors as bleed air, too hot and at too high a pressure; air-conditioning packs cool it, using air-cycle machines that expand it through a turbine, and mix it with filtered, recirculated cabin air. On the 787 electric compressors draw it from outside instead.

Air for the cabin, and the power to compress it

\[ \dot m = n\,(0.55\ \text{lb/min}) = n\,(0.00416\ \text{kg/s}) \] \[ P = \frac{\dot m\,c_p T_1}{\eta_c}\left[\left(\frac{p_{\text{cabin}}}{p_1}\right)^{(\gamma - 1)/\gamma} - 1\right] \]

\(n\) is the number of occupants; \(c_p = 1005\ \text{J/(kg K)}\), \(\gamma = 1.4\), \(T_1\) and \(p_1\) the temperature and pressure of the air entering the compressor and \(\eta_c\) the compressor's efficiency. In flight, the ram rise in the inlet, \(p_1 = p(1 + 0.2M^2)^{3.5}\), does part of the work.

Example 8.2 — Fresh air for the business jet

The business jet carries 10 occupants (8 passengers, 2 crew). Find the fresh-air flow and the ideal power to compress it from the outside air at 12 km (216.65 K) to the cabin's 75 260 Pa, without and with the ram rise at Mach 0.80.

Show solution
\[ \dot m = 10(0.55)(0.4536)/60 = 0.0416\ \text{kg/s} \] \[ \frac{p_{\text{cabin}}}{p} = \frac{75\,260}{19\,330} = 3.89 \] \[ P = 0.0416(1005)(216.65)(3.89^{0.286} - 1) = 4.30\ \text{kW} \]

The ram rise raises the inlet pressure by \((1 + 0.2(0.8)^2)^{3.5} = 1.524\), and its temperature to 244 K, leaving a pressure ratio of 2.55 for the compressor: the power falls by about a quarter, to 3.14 kW. These are small powers; the air conditioning's real cost lies in the bleed air's wasted pressure, in cooling the air, and in the extra air for heating, cooling and recirculation.

Oxygen and ice protection

If the cabin loses pressure at altitude, the occupants have only seconds to a minute or two of useful consciousness. Pressurized aircraft therefore carry oxygen: masks that drop automatically for the passengers, from chemical generators or stored gas, and quick-donning masks for the crew, enough to last the emergency descent to a safe altitude.

Ice on a wing's leading edge spoils its shape: it lowers the maximum lift coefficient, raises the drag and the stall speed, and on a tail can lead to a loss of control. Aircraft certified to fly in icing protect the wing and tail leading edges, the engine inlets, the windshields, the propellers and the air-data probes:

  • Hot bleed air piped along the leading edges, on most jets.
  • Pneumatic boots that inflate to crack the ice off, on turboprops and piston twins without much bleed air.
  • Electrical heating, on propellers, windshields and probes, and on the 787's wing.
  • Fluid weeping from porous leading edges, on some light aircraft and business jets.

Key takeaways

  • Cabin altitude at most 8000 ft in normal operation (CS/FAR 25.841 [3], [4]); the business jet's differential at 12 km is 55.9 kPa (8.11 psi).
  • The differential loads doors and windows with \(\Delta p\) times their area and the skin with \(\sigma = \Delta p\,r/t\); every flight is a fatigue cycle.
  • Fresh air: 0.55 lb/min per occupant (CS/FAR 25.831 [3], [4]); compressing it takes a few kilowatts for a business jet, less with the ram rise.
  • Oxygen covers a decompression; ice protection uses bleed air, boots, electrical heating or fluid.
