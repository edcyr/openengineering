A

Back to top
Actuator hydraulic
A piston in a cylinder that turns hydraulic pressure into force: \(F = pA\). A 20 kN actuator needs a 35.1 mm bore at 3000 psi and 27.2 mm at 5000 psi.
See: Lesson 7Related: Hydraulic system, Hydraulic power
Air-conditioning pack
The unit that cools hot bleed air, often by expanding it through the turbine of an air-cycle machine, before it is mixed with recirculated air and sent to the cabin.
See: Lesson 8Related: Bleed air, Fresh air
Aircraft classification number (ACN)
A number grading how hard an aircraft's wheels load a pavement; it must not exceed the runway's pavement classification number (PCN) without the airport's permission. Roughly twice the load on a single wheel in tonnes, more for wheels close together.
See: Lesson 3Related: Number of wheels, Bogie
Auxiliary power unit (APU)
A small gas turbine, usually in the tail cone, that supplies air and electricity on the ground and starts the engines, and backs up the engines' generators in flight. Many business jets, including this module's, do without one.
See: Lesson 7Related: Bleed air, Ram-air turbine

B

Back to top
Bank clearance
The bank angle, about the main wheels, at which a wing tip, nacelle or propeller first touches the ground. It must allow landing with at least 5° of bank; the business jet's is 10.0°.
See: Lesson 2Related: Ground clearance, Track
Bicycle gear
Two main legs on the centerline, ahead of and behind the CG, with small outriggers near the wing tips; used where a thin wing has no room for the gear.
See: Lesson 1Related: Gear arrangement, Quadricycle gear
Bleed air
Hot, compressed air taken from the engines' compressors to pressurize and air-condition the cabin, heat leading edges against ice and start engines. It costs fuel, more than the energy the cabin needs.
See: Lesson 7Related: Air-conditioning pack, Auxiliary power unit (APU), More-electric aircraft, Ice protection
Bogie
A beam on a main leg carrying four or six wheels, which spreads a heavy aircraft's weight over the pavement.
See: Lesson 1Related: Gear arrangement, Aircraft classification number (ACN), Number of wheels
Braking nose load
The nose gear's load while braking at \(a\): \(W_{n,\text{brake}} = W[(x_m - x_{cg,f}) + (a/g)h_{cg}]/B\). Checked at 0.31 g; the business jet's is 14 530 N (3267 lb), 21% of its weight.
See: Lesson 3Related: Nose gear share, Static gear loads, Tire rules (CS/FAR 25.733)

C

Back to top
Cabin altitude
The altitude in the standard atmosphere whose pressure matches the cabin's. CS/FAR 25.841 sets at most 8000 ft (2438 m) in normal operation, 15 000 ft after a probable failure.
See: Lesson 8Related: Pressure differential \(\Delta p\), Hoop stress
CG range
The span of center-of-gravity positions between the forward and aft limits, as fuel burns and loads change. This module assumes the business jet's is 15 to 35% of the MAC, stations 6.773 to 7.177 m, until Module 11.
See: Lesson 2Related: Nose gear share, Tip-back angle \(\theta_{tb}\), Wheelbase \(B\)

D

Back to top
Down-lock
A lock, often an over-center link, that holds the gear's brace straight when the gear is down.
See: Lesson 6Related: Side brace, Free fall

E

Back to top
Electrical system
Generators on the engines feed 28 V DC on small aircraft and 115 V 400 Hz AC on transports, with batteries for starting and emergencies. A higher voltage carries the same power with less current and copper.
See: Lesson 7Related: Starter-generator, More-electric aircraft

F

Back to top
Fly-by-wire
Flight controls in which the pilot's inputs go as electrical signals to computers that command the actuators, replacing cables and rods and protecting the flight envelope.
See: Lesson 7Related: Actuator, Redundancy
Footprint tire
The patch of a loaded tire pressed against the ground; its area is about the load over the inflation pressure, \(A_p \approx W_w/p\): 53.2 in² (343 cm²) for the business jet's main tire.
See: Lesson 4Related: Inflation pressure, Tire deflection \(S_T\)
Four-bar linkage
A mechanism of four pinned links that moves the wheel along a path no single pivot can, or shortens the leg as it retracts.
See: Lesson 6Related: Retraction
Free fall
Lowering the gear by releasing its uplocks and letting it fall under its own weight, the usual emergency means of extension. A gear that retracts forward falls into place with the help of the air loads.
See: Lesson 6Related: Retraction, Down-lock
Fresh air
Outside air supplied to the cabin: at least 0.55 lb (0.25 kg) per minute per occupant (CS/FAR 25.831), 0.0416 kg/s for the business jet's ten occupants.
See: Lesson 8Related: Air-conditioning pack, Bleed air

G

Back to top
Gear arrangement
How the wheels are laid out under the aircraft: single main, taildragger, tricycle, bicycle, quadricycle or multi-bogey. Nearly every modern aircraft uses a tricycle.
See: Lesson 1Related: Tricycle gear, Taildragger, Bicycle gear, Bogie
Gear load factor \(N_g\)
The largest vertical force on the gear during a landing divided by the weight; about 2 to 3 for transports and business aircraft. A lower factor needs a longer stroke.
See: Lesson 5Related: Stroke \(S\), Limit descent velocity
Ground clearance
The room between the ground and the parts of the aircraft nearest it: a nacelle about a quarter of its fan diameter, a propeller at least 7 in (0.18 m) by CS/FAR 25.925.
See: Lesson 2Related: Bank clearance, Tail-down angle \(\theta_{TD}\)
Ground loop
A swerve that grows until the aircraft spins round on the ground. It happens when the main wheels are ahead of the CG, so the ground's side force on them turns the aircraft further.
See: Lesson 1Related: Taildragger, Tricycle gear

H

Back to top
Hoop stress
The circumferential stress in a pressurized cylinder, \(\sigma = \Delta p\,r/t\), twice the longitudinal stress. Every flight is a fatigue cycle for the fuselage.
See: Lesson 8Related: Pressure differential \(\Delta p\)
Hydraulic power
A pump delivering a flow \(Q\) at pressure \(p\) supplies \(P = pQ\). Raising the business jet's gear in 6 s takes about 0.76 kW.
See: Lesson 7Related: Hydraulic system, Actuator
Hydraulic system
Engine-driven pumps keep a fluid at a set pressure, traditionally 3000 psi (20.7 MPa), some recent aircraft 5000 psi (34.5 MPa), to drive the gear, flaps, spoilers, brakes and flight controls.
See: Lesson 7Related: Actuator, Hydraulic power, Redundancy

I

Back to top
Ice protection
Keeping ice off the leading edges, inlets, windshields, propellers and probes: hot bleed air on most jets, pneumatic boots on turboprops, electrical heating, or fluid weeping from porous leading edges.
See: Lesson 8Related: Bleed air, More-electric aircraft
Inflation pressure
A tire's internal pressure. A tire carrying less than its rated load is inflated less in proportion, \(p = p_{\text{rated}}W_w/W_{\text{rated}}\): 132 psi for the business jet's main tires.
See: Lesson 4Related: Footprint, Rated load

L

Back to top
Lateral tip-over angle \(\psi\)
The angle measuring how far the CG sits inside the line from the nose wheel to a main wheel, compared with its height: \(\tan\psi = h_{cg}/d\). At most about 63°; the business jet's is 42.4° at the forward CG.
See: Lesson 2Related: Track, Tip-back angle \(\theta_{tb}\)
Limit descent velocity
The vertical speed at touchdown the gear must absorb: 10 ft/s (3.05 m/s) at the design landing weight (CS/FAR 25.473), and 12 ft/s without failure in the reserve-energy test (25.723).
See: Lesson 5Related: Stroke \(S\), Gear load factor \(N_g\)

M

Back to top
More-electric aircraft
An aircraft that replaces hydraulic and pneumatic systems with electrical ones; the Boeing 787 takes no bleed air, pressurizing its cabin with electric compressors and heating its wing electrically.
See: Lesson 7Related: Bleed air, Electrical system

N

Back to top
Nose gear share
The fraction of the weight on the nose gear, \((x_m - x_{cg})/B\). About 8 to 15% over the CG range; below 5% the nose wheel cannot steer, above 20% the aircraft is hard to rotate.
See: Lesson 2, Lesson 3Related: Wheelbase \(B\), CG range, Braking nose load
Number of wheels
Chosen from the load per wheel and the pavement: one wheel per leg on light aircraft and business jets, about 20 t per wheel on two-wheel legs on narrow-bodies, about 30 t per wheel on wide-body bogies.
See: Lesson 3Related: Aircraft classification number (ACN), Bogie

O

Back to top
Oleo-pneumatic strut
A shock absorber in which a piston forces oil through an orifice to dissipate energy while nitrogen acts as the spring. Its piston is sized by \(d_p = \sqrt{4L/(\pi P)}\) at about 12.4 MPa; outside diameter about 1.3 \(d_p\), length about 2.5 times the stroke.
See: Lesson 5, Lesson 5Related: Shock-absorber efficiency \(\eta\), Stroke \(S\), Trailing link
Oxygen system
Masks that drop for the passengers and quick-donning masks for the crew, supplied from chemical generators or stored gas, to cover a decompression until the aircraft descends.
See: Lesson 8Related: Cabin altitude

P

Back to top
Ply rating
An index of a tire casing's strength, not a count of its plies. One size comes in several ply ratings: the H22x8.25-10 is rated 6900 lb in 12 ply and 8300 lb in 14 ply.
See: Lesson 4Related: Rated load, Tire size designation
Pressure differential \(\Delta p\)
The cabin's pressure less the outside air's, \(\Delta p = p_{\text{cabin}} - p(h)\): 55 930 Pa (8.11 psi) for the business jet at 12 km with an 8000 ft cabin.
See: Lesson 8Related: Cabin altitude, Hoop stress

Q

Back to top
Quadricycle gear
Four main legs at the corners of a rectangle under the fuselage, as on the B-52.
See: Lesson 1Related: Gear arrangement, Bicycle gear

R

Back to top
Ram-air turbine
A small turbine that drops into the airstream to drive a pump or generator when all other power is lost.
See: Lesson 7Related: Auxiliary power unit (APU), Redundancy
Rated load tire
The largest static load a tire may carry at its rated inflation. The catalog also gives the largest braking load, 1.5 times the rated load.
See: Lesson 4, Lesson 4Related: Tire rules (CS/FAR 25.733), Ply rating, Inflation pressure
Redundancy
Feeding every critical user from more than one independent source, so that no single failure removes it: two or three hydraulic systems, several generators, batteries and a ram-air turbine.
See: Lesson 7Related: Hydraulic system, Ram-air turbine, Fly-by-wire
Retraction
Folding the gear into a bay in the wing, fuselage or a nacelle. The business jet's main legs fold inward 90° about a pivot on the wing, putting the wheels in the wing roots; its nose gear folds forward.
See: Lesson 6, Lesson 6Related: Free fall, Four-bar linkage, Stowage

S

Back to top
Shock-absorber efficiency \(\eta\)
The energy a shock absorber takes in, divided by its peak force times its stroke: \(\eta = \int F\,\mathrm{d}s/(F_{\max}S)\). About 0.5 for a spring, 0.75 to 0.9 for an oleo-pneumatic strut.
See: Lesson 5Related: Oleo-pneumatic strut, Spring gear, Stroke \(S\)
Side brace
A strut that carries the gear's side or fore-and-aft loads on the ground and folds in the middle as the gear retracts.
See: Lesson 6Related: Down-lock, Retraction
Spring gear
A leg of spring steel or composite, as on many light aircraft: no maintenance, but its force grows linearly with deflection (\(\eta \approx 0.5\)) and it throws the aircraft back up.
See: Lesson 5Related: Shock-absorber efficiency \(\eta\), Oleo-pneumatic strut
Starter-generator
An electrical machine on the engine's gearbox that spins the engine up to start it and then works as its generator; common on business jets.
See: Lesson 7Related: Electrical system
Static gear loads
The loads on the gear at rest: the main gear's largest at the aft CG, \(W(x_{cg,a} - x_n)/B\); the nose gear's at the forward CG, \(W(x_m - x_{cg,f})/B\). The business jet's main legs carry 31 300 N (7036 lb) each.
See: Lesson 3Related: Braking nose load, Nose gear share
Static loaded radius
The distance from the axle to the ground under the rated load: 9.14 in for the H22x8.25-10.
See: Lesson 4Related: Tire deflection \(S_T\), Rated load
Statistical tire size
A first estimate, \(D\) or \(w = A W_w^B\) (inches, pounds), with coefficients for each class of aircraft; for business twins \(D = 2.69W_w^{0.251}\).
See: Lesson 4Related: Tire size designation
Stowage
The room the retracted gear takes, with clearance for tire growth in service. The business jet's main wheel, lying flat, needs 0.26 m of depth in a wing root 0.33 m deep.
See: Lesson 6Related: Retraction
Stroke \(S\)
The shock absorber's travel: \(S = V_v^2/(2g\eta N_g) - (\eta_T/\eta)S_T\), plus about 25 mm. The business jet's is 0.195 m.
See: Lesson 5Related: Shock-absorber efficiency \(\eta\), Gear load factor \(N_g\), Tire deflection \(S_T\), Limit descent velocity

T

Back to top
Tail-down angle \(\theta_{TD}\)
The angle the aircraft can rotate about its main wheels before the fuselage touches the ground; typically 10 to 15°. The business jet's is 12.5°.
See: Lesson 2Related: Tip-back angle \(\theta_{tb}\), Ground clearance
Taildragger
The conventional gear: two main wheels ahead of the CG and a small tail wheel. Light and good for large propellers and rough strips, but prone to ground loops.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 1Related: Ground loop, Tricycle gear
Tip-back angle \(\theta_{tb}\)
The angle between the vertical through the main wheels and the line to the aft CG. At least 15° and more than the tail-down angle; the business jet's is 21.6°.
See: Lesson 2Related: Tail-down angle \(\theta_{TD}\), CG range
Tire deflection \(S_T\)
How far a tire squashes under its load: \(S_T = D/2 - R_{\text{SLR}}\). It absorbs part of the touchdown energy; the business jet's main tire deflects 47 mm.
See: Lesson 4, Lesson 5Related: Static loaded radius, Stroke \(S\), Footprint
Tire rules (CS/FAR 25.733)
A main tire's load may not exceed its rated load (times 1.07 on a multiple-wheel axle); a nose tire's static load may not exceed its rating, nor its braking load 1.5 times it.
See: Lesson 4Related: Rated load, Braking nose load
Tire size designation
A tire's name in inches: H22x8.25-10 is 22 in in outside diameter, 8.25 in wide on a 10 in rim; 6.00-6 (Type III) gives width and rim, 18x4.4 (Type VII) diameter and width; R marks a radial.
See: Lesson 4Related: Ply rating, Statistical tire size
Track
The lateral distance between the main wheels, twice the half track \(y_m\). The business jet's is 3.20 m; 1.45 m would meet the 63° tip-over guideline.
See: Lesson 2Related: Lateral tip-over angle \(\psi\), Wheelbase \(B\)
Trailing link
A gear whose wheel is on an arm hinged to the leg, with the shock absorber between them; it takes bumps from the front as well as from below and is short, so business jets often use it.
See: Lesson 5Related: Oleo-pneumatic strut
Tricycle gear
Two main wheels or legs just behind the CG and a steerable nose wheel: stable on the ground, level on the ground, and able to brake hard. Nearly every modern aircraft uses it.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 1Related: Taildragger, Ground loop

W

Back to top
Wheelbase \(B\)
The distance between the nose and main gear, \(B = x_m - x_n\). The CG range sets its least value: \(B \ge (x_{cg,a} - x_{cg,f})/\Delta(W_n/W)\). The business jet's is 5.85 m.
See: Lesson 2Related: Nose gear share, CG range
