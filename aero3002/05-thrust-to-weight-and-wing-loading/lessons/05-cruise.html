Lesson 5 · 30 min

Cruise and Loiter

Lesson 2 matched the thrust to the drag at the best lift-to-drag ratio for range. Whether the aircraft can fly at that ratio depends on its wing loading: in a given cruise, the wing loading fixes the lift coefficient, and the lift coefficient fixes the drag. Written in terms of \(\WS\), the cruise becomes a curve on the chart, with a best wing loading for range and another for endurance.

Learning objectives

  • Write the thrust-to-weight ratio for level cruise in terms of the wing loading, the dynamic pressure and the drag polar.
  • Explain the shape of the cruise curve: why both a large and a small wing need more thrust.
  • Find the wing loadings for best range and longest loiter, for jets and for propeller aircraft.
  • Judge how far a design's cruise is from its best, and what that costs.

Thrust for level cruise

In level flight the lift equals the weight, so the lift coefficient is \(C_L = W/(qS)\), with the dynamic pressure \(q = \tfrac12\rho V^2 = 0.7\,p\,M^2\). With the drag polar of Module 4, \(C_D = \CDz + K C_L^2\) and \(K = 1/(\pi A e)\), the drag-to-weight ratio is

\[ \frac{T}{W} = \frac{D}{W} = \frac{q\,\CDz}{W/S} + \frac{K}{q}\,\frac{W}{S} \]

with \(W/S\) in N/m² at the cruise weight. The first term is the zero-lift drag, which falls as the wing loading rises because a smaller wing has less area to drag through the air. The second is the induced drag, which rises with the wing loading because a smaller wing must work at a higher lift coefficient. Corrected to takeoff conditions as in Lesson 2:

Cruise constraint

\[ \TWo = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}\left[\frac{q\,\CDz}{\beta g\,\WSo} + \frac{K\beta g\,\WSo}{q}\right] \]

\(\WSo\) in kg/m²; \(\beta g\,\WSo\) is the cruise wing loading in N/m².

Example 5.1 — The business jet at its landing limit

The business jet cruises at Mach 0.80 and \(12\ \text{km}\), where \(p = 19\,330\ \text{Pa}\) and \(\sigma = 0.2537\), with \(\beta = 0.9555\), \(\CDz = 0.0165\), \(A = 8\) and \(e = 0.75\). Find its lift coefficient, lift-to-drag ratio and cruise \(\TW\) at \(\WS = 250\ \text{kg/m}^2\).

Show solution
\[ q = 0.7(19\,330)(0.80)^2 = 8660\ \text{Pa}, \qquad K = \frac{1}{\pi(8)(0.75)} = 0.05305 \] \[ C_L = \frac{0.9555(9.81)(250)}{8660} = 0.271, \qquad \frac{L}{D} = \frac{0.271}{0.0165 + 0.05305(0.271)^2} = 13.3 \] \[ \TWo = \frac{0.9555}{0.2537}\,\frac{1}{13.3} = 0.284 \]

Lesson 2 assumed the best-range lift-to-drag ratio, 14.6, and found 0.257. At its landing-limited wing loading, the business jet's wing is larger than the cruise wants: it flies at a lift coefficient of 0.27 instead of 0.32, with more friction drag, and needs 10% more thrust.

The cruise curve

Plotted against the wing loading, the cruise thrust is a U-shaped curve. Its minimum, the least thrust, is where the two drag terms are equal: the aircraft flies at \(C_L^{*} = \sqrt{\CDz/K}\) and \(\LDmax\). A jet's best range is at a lower lift coefficient, \(C_L^{*}/\sqrt3\), where \(L/D = 0.866\,\LDmax\) (Module 3), so its best-range wing loading sits on the left of the minimum.

The curve is flat near its bottom: a design can sit some way from the best wing loading at little cost in thrust. It is steep on the left, where a large wing drags much more surface through the air at a low lift coefficient. A high cruise altitude, or a low Mach number, lowers \(q\) and moves the best wing loading to the left; a high aspect ratio lowers \(K\) and moves it to the right.

Best wing loadings for range and loiter

Each best lift coefficient of Module 3 gives a best wing loading in a cruise or loiter at a dynamic pressure \(q\):

Table 5.1 Wing loading at the start of the segment, \(\WS = q\,C_L/g\) in kg/m²
SegmentJetPropeller aircraft
Best range\(\dfrac{q}{g}\sqrt{\dfrac{\CDz}{3K}}\)\(\dfrac{q}{g}\sqrt{\dfrac{\CDz}{K}}\)
Longest loiter\(\dfrac{q}{g}\sqrt{\dfrac{\CDz}{K}}\)\(\dfrac{q}{g}\sqrt{\dfrac{3\CDz}{K}}\)

A jet flies for range at the lift coefficient where \(\sqrt{C_L}/C_D\) is largest, and loiters at \(\LDmax\). A propeller aircraft, whose fuel burn follows the power rather than the thrust, flies for range at \(\LDmax\) and loiters at the lift coefficient where \(C_L^{3/2}/C_D\) is largest. These are the wing loadings at the weight at the start of the segment; divide by \(\beta\) for the takeoff wing loading. As fuel burns the wing loading falls, so the best \(q\) falls too, which is why jets climb gradually during a long cruise.

Example 5.2 — The business jet's best-range wing loading

Find the takeoff wing loading that would let the business jet start its cruise at the best-range lift coefficient.

Show solution
\[ C_L = \sqrt{\frac{0.0165}{3(0.05305)}} = 0.322, \qquad \WSo = \frac{8660(0.322)}{9.81(0.9555)} = 297\ \text{kg/m}^2 \]

The cruise wants a 19% smaller wing than the landing allows. The landing wins: a wing that is too large for the cruise costs some thrust and fuel, but a wing that is too small cannot land on the runway. Lesson 7 asks how much a better flap would buy.

Key takeaways

  • Level cruise: \(T/W = q\CDz/(W/S) + K(W/S)/q\), corrected by \(\beta/\alpha\) to takeoff conditions.
  • The cruise curve is U-shaped; its minimum is at \(\LDmax\), and a jet's best range is to its left, at \(C_L^{*}/\sqrt3\).
  • Best wing loadings: jet range \((q/g)\sqrt{\CDz/(3K)}\), jet loiter and propeller range \((q/g)\sqrt{\CDz/K}\), propeller loiter \((q/g)\sqrt{3\CDz/K}\).
  • The business jet's cruise wants 297 kg/m², but the landing allows 250: it cruises at \(L/D = 13.3\) and needs \(\TW = 0.284\).
