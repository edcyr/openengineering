Two Numbers That Size the Aircraft

Module 3 found how heavy the business jet will be, and Module 4 shaped its wing. Two numbers now decide how big that wing must be and how much thrust the engines must give: the thrust-to-weight ratio and the wing loading. Nearly every performance requirement, from the runway to the ceiling, can be written in terms of these two, and the lessons that follow choose them for the business jet.

Learning objectives

  • Define the thrust-to-weight ratio, the power loading and the wing loading, and convert between their units.
  • Explain which requirements each one sets, and what raising or lowering it costs.
  • Describe how both have changed over a century of aircraft, and quote typical values by class.
  • State the business jet's design brief, the requirements that Lessons 3 to 8 turn into a wing area and an engine size.

Two ratios that size the aircraft

The thrust-to-weight ratio \(\TW\) compares the engines' thrust with the aircraft's weight. A propeller aircraft is described instead by its power loading, the power per unit of mass \(\PW\), because its engine delivers power and its thrust depends on speed. The wing loading \(\WS\) compares the weight with the reference wing area of Module 4.

Thrust-to-weight ratio, power loading and wing loading

\[ \frac{T}{W} = \frac{T_0}{\Wo\,g}, \qquad \frac{P}{W} = \frac{P_0}{\Wo}\ \ (\text{kW/kg}), \qquad \frac{W}{S} = \frac{\Wo}{S}\ \ (\text{kg/m}^2) \]

\(T_0\) and \(P_0\) are the total sea-level static thrust and power of all the engines, \(\Wo\) is the takeoff mass in kg. As in Modules 2 to 4, wing loading is a mass per area: multiply by \(g\) for N/m², or divide by 4.882 for lb/ft².

Both ratios change in flight. Fuel burns off, so \(W\) falls; the engines lose thrust with altitude and speed, so \(T\) falls faster. To compare designs, and to draw a single chart of the requirements, every requirement is converted back to the takeoff weight and the sea-level static thrust. That conversion is the theme of Lesson 2.

Example 1.1 — The business jet's loadings

The business jet weighs \(\Wo = 6943\ \text{kg}\) (Module 3) and has a reference area of \(28\ \text{m}^2\) (Module 4). Find its wing loading in kg/m², N/m² and lb/ft², and the thrust each of its two engines must give for \(\TW = 0.30\).

Show solution
\[ \frac{W}{S} = \frac{6943}{28} = 248.0\ \text{kg/m}^2 = 248.0(9.81) = 2433\ \text{N/m}^2 = \frac{248.0}{4.882} = 50.8\ \text{lb/ft}^2 \] \[ T_0 = 0.30(6943)(9.81) = 20\,430\ \text{N}, \qquad \frac{T_0}{2} = 10.2\ \text{kN} \]

About 2300 lbf per engine, the size of the engines on light business jets. Whether 0.30 is enough is what Lessons 2 to 8 decide.

What each ratio sets

Almost every performance requirement pulls one ratio up or the other down:

Table 1.1 What raising each ratio does
RaiseIt buysIt costs
\(\TW\)Shorter takeoff, steeper climb, higher ceiling, sustained turns, higher top speed, safer climb with an engine failedHeavier, costlier engines; in a jet, more thrust than cruise needs
\(\WS\)A smaller, lighter wing with less skin friction; cruise nearer the best lift coefficient at high altitude; a smoother ride in gustsHigher stall and approach speeds, so longer runways; wider turns; more induced drag at altitude, so a lower ceiling

The two are not independent. A small wing (high \(\WS\)) needs more thrust to take off in the same distance, and an aircraft with plenty of thrust can carry a smaller wing. The design problem is to find the pair that meets every requirement at the least cost, usually the lowest \(\TW\) and the highest \(\WS\) that still work. Lesson 7 finds it graphically.

A century of loadings

Figure 1.1 plots the loadings of the aircraft in Module 1's data against their year of first flight. The wing loading has grown more than tenfold, from about 40 kg/m² in the First World War to over 500 kg/m² for jet transports, as flaps raised the maximum lift coefficient, runways grew longer and cruise speeds rose. The thrust-to-weight ratio of jet transports has stayed near 0.25 since the first jets, while fighters range from 0.3 to above 1.

These are a starting point, not an answer. A wing loading or thrust-to-weight ratio is chosen to meet requirements, and two aircraft of the same class with different runways or ceilings end up with different values. The business jet's 248 kg/m² sits with the jet trainers, at less than half the wing loading of a jet transport: a business jet uses shorter runways and simpler flaps.

Key takeaways

  • \(\TW = T_0/(\Wo g)\) with sea-level static thrust; propeller aircraft use \(\PW\) in kW/kg; \(\WS = \Wo/S\) in kg/m² (× 9.81 for N/m², ÷ 4.882 for lb/ft²).
  • Higher \(\TW\) buys takeoff, climb, ceiling and maneuver; higher \(\WS\) buys a smaller, lighter wing and a smoother ride, at the cost of field length and ceiling.
  • Wing loadings have grown tenfold in a century; jet transports have kept \(\TW \approx 0.25\).
  • The business jet: \(\WS = 248\ \text{kg/m}^2\) so far, and a brief of landing, takeoff, cruise, ceiling and engine-out requirements to meet.
