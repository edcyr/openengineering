The Constraint Diagram

Lessons 3 to 6 turned each requirement into a limit on the wing loading or a minimum thrust-to-weight ratio. Drawn on one chart, they leave a region of designs that meet every requirement. The best of them, the design point, needs the least thrust with the smallest wing, and the chart shows which requirements put it there and what relaxing them would buy.

Learning objectives

  • Draw a constraint diagram from a set of requirements, and identify the feasible region.
  • Choose the design point, and name the requirements that set it.
  • Explain why the best design point is not always at the largest allowed wing loading.
  • Use the diagram to judge what changing a requirement or a technology is worth.

One chart for every requirement

Each requirement of the business jet's brief has become a line on a chart of \(\TW\) against \(\WS\):

A design must sit above every \(\TW\) curve and to the left of every wing loading limit. Together the curves form an envelope, the highest of them at each wing loading, and the region above the envelope and left of the limits is the feasible region.

Choosing the design point

Every point in the feasible region meets the requirements, but they are not equally good. Engines are heavy and expensive, and a large wing is heavy and has a large wetted area. So the designer looks for the lowest thrust-to-weight ratio in the region and, among designs with that thrust, the highest wing loading. The point usually sits on a corner of the envelope, where two requirements meet: these are the active constraints, the ones that actually size the aircraft. The others are met with room to spare.

Example 7.1 — The business jet's design point

Read the business jet's design point from its constraints at the landing limit.

Show solution

The landing allows at most 249.9 kg/m². Every \(\TW\) constraint that applies there is below the ceiling's 0.308; to the left of the landing line the ceiling curve rises, so no smaller wing needs less thrust. The design point is

\[ \WSo = 250\ \text{kg/m}^2, \qquad \TWo = 0.308 \to 0.31 \]

The landing and the ceiling are the active constraints; the takeoff (0.287), cruise (0.284) and engine-out climb (0.248) are met with margin. Rounding the thrust up to 0.31 keeps a small allowance for growth.

The design point is not always at the largest allowed wing loading. A takeoff line rises to the right, so if the wing loading limit is generous, a smaller wing may need more thrust than a slightly larger one. The best point is then where the rising takeoff line crosses a falling curve such as the ceiling, to the left of the limit.

What-if studies

The diagram's real value is in the questions it answers quickly. Which requirement is expensive? What would a technology buy? Each change moves one line, and the design point follows.

Example 7.2 — Better flaps

Double-slotted flaps would raise the business jet's landing \(C_{L_{\max},L}\) from 2.1 to 2.4, with the takeoff value unchanged. Where does the design point move?

Show solution
\[ \WSo \le \frac{(840 - 305)(2.4)}{4.996(0.9)} = 285.6\ \text{kg/m}^2 \]

At that wing loading the takeoff needs \(2.34(285.6)/(1200 \times 1.7) = 0.328\), while the ceiling needs only 0.287. The takeoff line and the ceiling curve cross at

\[ \WSo = 261.7\ \text{kg/m}^2, \qquad \TWo = 0.300 \]

The new design point sits at the crossing, left of the new landing limit: the wing shrinks by 4.5%, to 26.5 m², and the thrust falls by about 3%. The extra flap capability beyond about 2.2 is not worth using unless the takeoff flaps improve too.

Example 7.3 — A lower ceiling

The ceiling sets the business jet's thrust. What if the customer accepted a cruise ceiling of \(11\ \text{km}\) instead of 12 km (\(\sigma = 0.2971\)), with the same Mach number and weight fraction?

Show solution

At 11 km the ceiling needs \(\TW = 0.290\) at 250 kg/m², close to the takeoff's 0.287. The design point becomes

\[ \WSo = 250\ \text{kg/m}^2, \qquad \TWo = 0.290 \]

6% less thrust, with the landing, the ceiling and the takeoff now all nearly active. The designer can show the customer exactly what the extra kilometer of ceiling costs.

Every constraint rests on assumptions: the thrust lapse \(\alpha = \sigma\), the drag polar, the takeoff correlation, the weight fractions. The diagram is only as good as these, so a design point is always rechecked as the engine, the drag and the weights are refined.

Key takeaways

  • The feasible region lies above the envelope of the \(\TW\) constraints and to the left of the wing loading limits.
  • The design point is the lowest \(\TW\) in the region, at the highest \(\WS\) that gives it; the requirements that meet there are active.
  • The business jet's design point is \(\WS = 250\ \text{kg/m}^2\) and \(\TW = 0.31\), set by the landing and the ceiling.
  • Better flaps move it to the crossing of the takeoff and ceiling lines, (262, 0.300); a lower ceiling lowers the thrust to 0.290.
