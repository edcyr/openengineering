Statistics and Thrust Matching

Before any requirement is analyzed, two quick estimates give a first thrust-to-weight ratio: statistics from aircraft of the same class, and thrust matching, which asks how much thrust the cruise needs. Both have to deal with the same complication. The engines are rated at sea level and standing still, but the aircraft cruises high and fast, lighter than at takeoff.

Learning objectives

  • Estimate \(\TW\) of a jet from its maximum Mach number, and \(\PW\) of a propeller aircraft from its maximum speed, using statistical fits.
  • Explain the weight fraction \(\beta\) and the thrust lapse \(\alpha\), and convert a thrust-to-weight ratio in flight to takeoff conditions.
  • Match the thrust to the drag in cruise to find the takeoff \(\TW\) a cruise needs.
  • Convert a required thrust to a power for a propeller aircraft, with a lapse of power with altitude.

Statistical estimates

Aircraft built for the same job end up with similar thrust-to-weight ratios. For jets, \(\TW\) grows with the maximum Mach number; a fit of the form \(a\,M_{\max}^{\,C}\) captures the trend for each class. Propeller aircraft are fitted the same way, with the power loading against the maximum speed:

Statistical thrust-to-weight ratio and power loading

\[ \TWo = a\,M_{\max}^{\,C}, \qquad \left(\frac{P}{W}\right)_0 = a\,V_{\max}^{\,C}\ \ (\text{kW/kg},\ V_{\max}\ \text{in km/h}) \]

Example 2.1 — Two jets from statistics

Estimate the takeoff thrust-to-weight ratio of a jet trainer with a maximum Mach number of 0.8, and of a jet transport with a maximum Mach number of 0.85.

Show solution
\[ \text{Trainer:}\quad \TWo = 0.488(0.8)^{0.728} = 0.415 \] \[ \text{Transport:}\quad \TWo = 0.267(0.85)^{0.363} = 0.252 \]

Both agree with the typical values of Lesson 1, 0.4 and 0.25 to 0.4. The fit cannot know about the runway, the ceiling or the number of engines; it only says what similar aircraft carry.

From takeoff to cruise: weight fraction and thrust lapse

A thrust-to-weight ratio always refers to the takeoff weight and the sea-level static thrust. In flight, the weight and the available thrust are both smaller:

Weight fraction and thrust lapse

\[ \beta = \frac{W}{\Wo}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{T}{T_0}, \qquad \TWo = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}\left(\frac{T}{W}\right) \]

\(\beta\) comes from the mission segment weight fractions of Module 3. \(\alpha\) depends on the engine, the altitude, the speed and the throttle setting.

A turbojet or turbofan's thrust falls roughly with the air density. The lessons use the simplest model, \(\alpha = \sigma = \rho/\rho_0\), the density ratio of the standard atmosphere. Real engines differ: a high-bypass turbofan also loses thrust as its speed rises, and at a cruise Mach number of 0.8 it may give noticeably less than \(\sigma\) predicts. Engine data replace this model as soon as an engine is chosen, and designs sized with \(\alpha = \sigma\) should expect to need some extra thrust.

Thrust matching in cruise

In steady, level cruise the thrust equals the drag and the lift equals the weight, so the thrust-to-weight ratio in cruise is the inverse of the lift-to-drag ratio. Corrected to takeoff conditions:

Thrust matching in cruise

\[ \left(\frac{T}{W}\right)_{\text{cruise}} = \frac{1}{(L/D)_{\text{cruise}}}, \qquad \TWo = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}\,\frac{1}{(L/D)_{\text{cruise}}} \]

A jet cruises for best range at \(0.866\,\LDmax\), a propeller aircraft at \(\LDmax\) (Module 3).

Example 2.2 — The business jet's cruise thrust

The business jet has \(\LDmax = 16.9\) and starts its cruise at Mach 0.80 and \(12\ \text{km}\) with \(\beta = 0.9555\). Find the takeoff thrust-to-weight ratio its cruise needs, with \(\alpha = \sigma\).

Show solution

At 12 km the density ratio is \(\sigma = 0.2537\), and the best-range lift-to-drag ratio is \(0.866(16.9) = 14.64\):

\[ \TWo = \frac{0.9555}{0.2537}\,\frac{1}{14.64} = 0.257 \]

The cruise needs only 6.8% of the weight in thrust, but the engines lose three-quarters of their thrust at 12 km, so the takeoff thrust must be almost four times the cruise thrust. This assumes the business jet can cruise at its best-range lift coefficient; Lesson 5 checks whether its wing loading allows it.

Propeller aircraft: from thrust to power

A propeller converts shaft power into thrust power with the propeller efficiency \(\eta_p\), about 0.8 to 0.85 in cruise: \(P = TV/\eta_p\). Dividing by the weight and correcting to takeoff conditions gives the power loading a cruise needs:

Power loading for a cruise

\[ \left(\frac{P}{W}\right)_0 = \frac{gV}{1000\,\eta_p}\,\frac{\beta}{\alpha_P}\,\frac{1}{(L/D)_{\text{cruise}}}\ \ (\text{kW/kg}) \]

\(V\) in m/s. The power lapse \(\alpha_P = P/P_0\) is about \(\sigma\) for a turboprop; for a piston engine without a supercharger, \(\alpha_P = \sigma - (1-\sigma)/7.55\).

Example 2.3 — A turboprop's cruise power

A twin turboprop cruises at \(500\ \text{km/h}\) and \(7\ \text{km}\) (\(\sigma = 0.4812\)) with \(L/D = 15\), \(\eta_p = 0.85\) and \(\beta = 0.97\). What takeoff power loading does the cruise need?

Show solution
\[ V = \frac{500}{3.6} = 138.9\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \left(\frac{P}{W}\right)_0 = \frac{9.81(138.9)}{1000(0.85)}\,\frac{0.97}{0.4812}\,\frac{1}{15} = 0.215\ \text{kW/kg} \]

An 18-tonne turboprop would need about 3900 kW, two engines of about 1950 kW. In Module 1's data [11] the C-130 carries 0.21 kW/kg and the King Air 200 0.22 kW/kg. The twin-turboprop fit of Figure 2.1 suggests more, 0.36 kW/kg at a maximum speed of 500 km/h, because statistics include the takeoff and climb requirements that the cruise alone does not see.

Which estimate to trust

The two estimates answer different questions. Statistics say what similar aircraft carry, which includes every requirement those aircraft had to meet. Thrust matching says what one requirement, the cruise, needs. A business jet's cruise thrust of 0.257 is a lower limit: the takeoff, the climb to the ceiling and the climb with one engine failed may need more. Lessons 3 to 6 derive each of those limits, and Lesson 7 finds the one that decides.

Key takeaways

  • Statistics: \(\TWo = a\,M_{\max}^{\,C}\) for jets and \((P/W)_0 = a\,V_{\max}^{\,C}\) for propeller aircraft give a first estimate.
  • Every \(\TW\) refers to the takeoff weight and sea-level static thrust; \(\TWo = (\beta/\alpha)(T/W)\), with \(\beta = W/\Wo\) and \(\alpha = T/T_0\).
  • The lessons take \(\alpha = \sigma\) for jets; real turbofans lose more thrust with speed.
  • In cruise \(T/W = 1/(L/D)\); the business jet needs \(\TWo = 0.257\) for its cruise at 12 km.
  • For a propeller aircraft, \(P = TV/\eta_p\) converts the thrust to a power.
