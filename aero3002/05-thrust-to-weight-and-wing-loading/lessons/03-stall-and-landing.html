Stall Speed and Landing

The slowest an aircraft can fly is set by its wing loading and its maximum lift coefficient. A stall speed, an approach speed or a landing distance therefore puts an upper limit on the wing loading, whatever the engines. For most transports and business jets, the landing is the requirement that sizes the wing.

Learning objectives

  • Find the largest wing loading that meets a stall speed or approach speed requirement.
  • Describe how the maximum lift coefficient has grown, and what it depends on.
  • Estimate the landing distance from the wing loading, the landing lift coefficient and the obstacle allowance, and the field length from it.
  • Turn a landing distance requirement into a wing loading limit, and find the business jet's.

Stall speed and approach speed

At the stall the wing gives its maximum lift coefficient, and the lift still equals the weight. Solved for the wing loading:

Wing loading for a stall speed

\[ \frac{W}{S} = \frac{\rho\,V_s^2\,\CLmax}{2g}\ \ (\text{kg/m}^2) \]

Any wing loading below this value stalls slower than \(V_s\). The density is that of the airport, usually sea level on a standard day.

A stall speed limit appears in some certification rules. Small single-engine aircraft have long been held to a stall speed of 61 kt, \(113\ \text{km/h}\), in the landing configuration. Transports have no stall speed limit, but their approach speed is tied to the stall: the reference landing speed is \(V_{\text{REF}} = 1.23\,V_{SR}\) (Module 2), and a customer or an airport may set a maximum approach speed. Both use the landing weight \(W_L\), not the takeoff weight. The limit on the takeoff wing loading is then larger by \(\Wo/W_L\).

Example 3.1 — A light aircraft's stall

A single-engine light aircraft must stall at no more than \(113\ \text{km/h}\) in the landing configuration, where its \(\CLmax\) is 1.6. What is the largest wing loading it can have at sea level?

Show solution
\[ V_s = \frac{113}{3.6} = 31.39\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \frac{W}{S} = \frac{1.225(31.39)^2(1.6)}{2(9.81)} = 98.4\ \text{kg/m}^2 \]

About 20 lb/ft². The statistics of Lesson 1 give 83 kg/m² for a single-engine aircraft: most sit comfortably below the limit.

The maximum lift coefficient

The maximum lift coefficient comes from Module 4: the airfoil, the sweep and the aspect ratio set the clean value, and the flaps and slats add to it. Landing flaps are set for the most lift; takeoff flaps for less, about 80% of the landing value, because they also add drag that the climb after takeoff cannot afford. Figure 3.1 shows what the high-lift devices have bought: the maximum lift coefficient implied by each aircraft's stall speed has doubled in a century.

Early aircraft had thin airfoils and no flaps, and landed on grass fields into the wind at any angle; their \(\CLmax\) near 1.2 was enough. Split and slotted flaps arrived in the 1930s, and the jet transports added slats and double- and triple-slotted flaps to land fast swept wings on runways of reasonable length. The business jet's landing \(\CLmax\) of 2.1, with simple slotted flaps and no slats, sits below the jet transports' average.

The landing distance

The landing distance runs from a height of 50 ft (15 m) above the runway threshold to a full stop. It has two parts: the approach and flare over the obstacle, which is nearly the same length for aircraft of a type, and the ground roll, which grows with the kinetic energy at touchdown, so with the wing loading. A simple estimate captures both:

Landing distance

\[ s_L = 4.996\,\frac{(W/S)_L}{\sigma\,C_{L_{\max},L}} + S_a\ \ (\text{m}) \]

\((W/S)_L\) in kg/m² at the landing weight. The obstacle allowance \(S_a\) is \(305\ \text{m}\) for airliners, \(183\ \text{m}\) for light aircraft and \(137\ \text{m}\) for short-takeoff-and-landing aircraft. Thrust reversers or reversing propellers cut the first term by about a third (multiply it by 0.66).

The constant is 80 ft per lb/ft² of wing loading in US units: \(80(0.3048)(9.81)/47.88 = 4.996\ \text{m}\) per kg/m². A transport's landing field length adds the margin of the certification rules: the aircraft must stop within 60% of the runway, so the field length is the landing distance divided by 0.6, or multiplied by 1.67.

The wing loading the landing allows

Solved for the takeoff wing loading, the landing distance requirement gives an upper limit, a vertical line on a chart of \(\TW\) against \(\WS\): the landing does not depend on the thrust.

Wing loading limit from the landing distance

\[ \WSo \le \frac{(s_L - S_a)\,\sigma\,C_{L_{\max},L}}{4.996\,(W_L/\Wo)} \]

Example 3.2 — The business jet's landing

The business jet must land in \(840\ \text{m}\) at sea level, at a landing weight of \(0.9\,\Wo\), with \(C_{L_{\max},L} = 2.1\). Find the largest takeoff wing loading it can have, and its stall and reference speeds at that wing loading.

Show solution
\[ \left(\frac{W}{S}\right)_L = \frac{(840 - 305)(1)(2.1)}{4.996} = 224.9\ \text{kg/m}^2 \] \[ \WSo = \frac{224.9}{0.9} = 249.9 \approx 250\ \text{kg/m}^2 \]

At the landing weight, the stall and reference speeds are

\[ V_{SR} = \sqrt{\frac{2(224.9)(9.81)}{1.225(2.1)}} = 41.4\ \text{m/s}, \qquad V_{\text{REF}} = 1.23(41.4) = 50.9\ \text{m/s} \]

\(V_{\text{REF}}\) is 99 kt. The 28 m² wing of Module 4, at 248 kg/m², just meets the landing requirement; the landing alone would allow \(6943/250 = 27.8\ \text{m}^2\). The clean wing, with \(\CLmax = 1.3\), would stall at 55.5 m/s (108 kt) at the takeoff weight.

The landing limit is the reason business jets have lower wing loadings than airliners: they serve shorter runways, and their simpler flaps give a lower \(C_{L_{\max},L}\). A higher landing lift coefficient moves the line to the right, which allows a smaller wing; Lesson 7 shows what that is worth.

Key takeaways

  • A stall speed limits the wing loading: \(\WS = \rho V_s^2\CLmax/(2g)\), at the landing weight for an approach speed.
  • The maximum lift coefficient has doubled in a century, from about 1.2 to over 2.4, with flaps and slats.
  • Landing distance: \(s_L = 4.996(W/S)_L/(\sigma C_{L_{\max},L}) + S_a\); a transport's field length is \(s_L/0.6\).
  • The landing gives a vertical line on the constraint diagram; for the business jet, \(\WSo \le 250\ \text{kg/m}^2\).
