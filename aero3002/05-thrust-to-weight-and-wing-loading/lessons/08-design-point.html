From the Design Point to the Aircraft

A design point is a pair of ratios. Multiplied by the takeoff weight, it becomes a wing area and an engine thrust: the first real dimensions of the aircraft's two most important parts. This lesson makes that step for the business jet, checks the result against every requirement, and compares it with the aircraft of the last century.

Learning objectives

  • Find the wing area and the installed thrust or power from a design point and a takeoff weight.
  • Explain the rubber engine, and how the design changes when a real engine or a rounded wing area is chosen.
  • Rescale the wing and the engines when the takeoff weight estimate changes.
  • Check a design against its requirements, and place it among real aircraft.

From ratios to sizes

Wing area and installed thrust or power

\[ S = \frac{\Wo}{\WSo}, \qquad T_0 = \TWo\,\Wo\,g, \qquad P_0 = \left(\frac{P}{W}\right)_0\Wo \]

\(\Wo\) in kg, \(S\) in m², \(T_0\) in N and \(P_0\) in kW, all divided among the \(N\) engines.

Example 8.1 — The business jet's wing and engines

Size the business jet's wing and engines for its design point, \(\WS = 250\ \text{kg/m}^2\) and \(\TW = 0.31\), at \(\Wo = 6943\ \text{kg}\).

Show solution
\[ S = \frac{6943}{250} = 27.8\ \text{m}^2, \qquad b = \sqrt{AS} = \sqrt{8(27.8)} = 14.9\ \text{m} \] \[ T_0 = 0.31(6943)(9.81) = 21\,110\ \text{N}, \qquad \frac{T_0}{2} = 10.6\ \text{kN} \]

Each engine must give about 10.6 kN (2370 lbf) at sea level, standing still. Module 4's wing of 28 m² is within 1% of the landing's 27.8 m², so it stays: at 248 kg/m² it lands slightly shorter than required.

The rubber engine

Conceptual design treats the engine as rubber: a known engine type scaled up or down to give exactly the thrust the design point asks for, with its weight, size and fuel consumption scaled with it. That keeps the design free to find its best point. Later the designer must choose a real engine, or commit to developing a new one. A real engine comes in one size, so the thrust-to-weight ratio becomes whatever that engine gives:

  • An engine with more thrust than needed moves the design point up. The aircraft meets every requirement with margin, but carries extra engine weight and may burn more fuel in cruise at a low throttle setting.
  • An engine with less thrust moves the design point down, below some \(\TW\) constraint. The designer must relax that requirement, or win it back elsewhere: a lower wing loading, better flaps, less drag.

The wing area is rounded in the same way, and a wing that is slightly too large, like the business jet's, only moves the design point to the left, away from the landing limit. Module 7 chooses the business jet's engines.

When the weight changes

Every constraint of this module is written in ratios, so none depends on the takeoff weight itself. When a later module refines the weight, the design point stays where it is, and the wing and the engines are simply rescaled: \(S\) and \(T_0\) grow in proportion to \(\Wo\). The weight estimate in turn depends on the wing and engine sizes, through the empty weight, so design proceeds in loops, each one refining the weight, the sizes and the design point.

Example 8.2 — A heavier business jet

Suppose the detailed weight estimate of a later module raises the business jet's takeoff weight by 5%, to \(7290\ \text{kg}\). Rescale its wing and engines.

Show solution
\[ S = \frac{7290}{250} = 29.2\ \text{m}^2, \qquad T_0 = 0.31(7290)(9.81) = 22\,170\ \text{N} \]

Each engine now needs 11.1 kN. Both sizes grow 5%; the ratios, and so the performance, are unchanged. In practice the bigger wing and engines weigh more again, which is why the weight estimate is repeated until it converges.

The design point among real aircraft

Figure 8.1 places the business jet's design point among the aircraft of Module 1's data. Jet transports sit at high wing loadings and modest thrust: long runways, powerful flaps and a cruise near their best lift coefficient. Fighters spread upward, with thrust for climbing and turning. The business jet has about the wing loading of the first jet transport, the Comet, and the thrust of the most powerful ones: the price of short runways and a high ceiling.

Key takeaways

  • \(S = \Wo/\WSo\) and \(T_0 = \TWo\Wo g\); divide the thrust among the engines.
  • A rubber engine is scaled to the design point; a real engine fixes \(\TW\) and moves the design point.
  • The constraints do not depend on \(\Wo\): when the weight changes, rescale \(S\) and \(T_0\) and keep the ratios.
  • The business jet: a 28 m² wing and two engines of 10.6 kN, meeting every requirement, with the ceiling the tightest.
