Climb, Ceiling and Maneuver

Cruise asks the engines only to balance the drag. Climbing, reaching a ceiling and turning ask for more: thrust beyond the drag to gain height, or extra lift that brings extra induced drag. One equation, built on the aircraft's excess power, covers all of them, and a simple one covers the climb with an engine failed, which often sets the thrust of a twin.

Learning objectives

  • Derive the master equation of constraint analysis from the specific excess power.
  • Find the \(\TW\) for a climb gradient with one engine failed, using the certification minimums.
  • Find the \(\TW\) for a ceiling with a residual rate of climb, and for a sustained turn.
  • Find the wing loading limit an instantaneous turn sets.

The master equation

An aircraft whose thrust exceeds its drag can use the difference to climb or to accelerate. Its specific excess power, the excess power per unit weight, is the rate at which it gains energy height:

\[ P_s = \frac{V(T - D)}{W} = \frac{\dd h}{\dd t} + \frac{V}{g}\,\frac{\dd V}{\dd t} \]

Solved for \(T/W\), with the drag polar and a load factor \(n = L/W\) for turns, and corrected to takeoff conditions as in Lesson 2:

The master equation (steady speed)

\[ \TWo = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}\left[\frac{q\,\CDz}{\beta g\,\WSo} + \frac{n^2 K\beta g\,\WSo}{q} + \frac{1}{V}\,\frac{\dd h}{\dd t}\right] \]

\(\WSo\) in kg/m². With \(n = 1\) and no climb it is the cruise constraint of Lesson 5. An acceleration adds \((1/g)\,\dd V/\dd t\) inside the brackets.

The climb term is the climb gradient, \(G = (\dd h/\dd t)/V\), the sine of the climb angle. The turn multiplies the induced drag by \(n^2\), because the wing must carry \(n\) times the weight. Every flight condition the customer asks for, at its own altitude, speed, weight fraction, load factor and climb rate, becomes one curve on the chart.

Climb with one engine failed

A multi-engine aircraft must keep climbing after an engine fails at takeoff. The most demanding case is usually the second segment: landing gear up, takeoff flaps, at the takeoff safety speed \(V_2\), with takeoff thrust on the remaining engines. The certification rules set minimum gradients for transports:

Minimum second-segment climb gradient with one engine failed (transport certification rules)
EnginesGradient \(G\)
Two2.4%
Three2.7%
Four3.0%

In a steady climb at a small angle, \(T = D + W\sin\gamma\), so \(T/W = 1/(L/D) + G\) with the remaining engines. With \(N\) engines, one failed, the total installed thrust must be \(N/(N-1)\) times as large:

Climb with one of \(N\) engines failed

\[ \TWo = \frac{N}{N-1}\left(\frac{1}{L/D} + G\right) \]

\(L/D\) with takeoff flaps and gear up, about 10 for a business jet. At takeoff \(\beta = 1\) and, near sea level, \(\alpha \approx 1\), so this is a horizontal line on the chart.

Example 6.1 — The business jet loses an engine

The business jet is a twin with \(L/D = 10\) in the second-segment configuration. What \(\TW\) does a 2.4% gradient need?

Show solution
\[ \TWo = \frac{2}{1}\left(\frac{1}{10} + 0.024\right) = 0.248 \]

Below the takeoff's 0.287, so for this design the engine failure does not decide the thrust. A twin has to double its one-engine requirement; a four-engine aircraft adds only a third. That is one reason twins carry more thrust than four-engine aircraft of the same size.

Ceiling

As an aircraft climbs, the engines lose thrust until the rate of climb falls to zero, at its absolute ceiling. Because the last few hundred meters take a very long time, ceilings are defined by a small remaining rate of climb: 100 ft/min (0.51 m/s) for the service ceiling, and 300 ft/min (1.52 m/s) for the cruise ceiling, the highest altitude an airline or business jet can usefully cruise at. A ceiling requirement is the master equation with that climb rate, at the cruise speed and weight.

Example 6.2 — The business jet's ceiling

The business jet must still climb at 300 ft/min at \(12\ \text{km}\) and Mach 0.80 (\(V = 236.1\ \text{m/s}\)), at the start of its cruise. Find the \(\TW\) it needs at \(\WS = 250\ \text{kg/m}^2\).

Show solution

The climb gradient is \(G = 1.524/236.1 = 0.00646\). The other two terms are the cruise of Example 5.1, \((T/W)_{\text{cruise}} = 1/13.27 = 0.0754\):

\[ \TWo = \frac{0.9555}{0.2537}\left(0.0754 + 0.00646\right) = 0.308 \]

More than the takeoff's 0.287: the ceiling is now the requirement that sets the business jet's thrust. Climbing 300 ft/min at 12 km costs 9% more thrust than cruising there.

Turns

A combat aircraft's maneuver requirements come in two kinds. A sustained turn holds a load factor without losing speed or height: the master equation with \(n > 1\) and no climb. An instantaneous turn is the tightest turn the wing can pull for a moment, trading speed for it, and is limited only by the maximum lift coefficient. With \(nW = qS\CLmax\):

Instantaneous turn

\[ \WSo \le \frac{q\,\CLmax}{n\,g\,\beta} \]

A vertical line: a wing loading limit that no amount of thrust changes.

Example 6.3 — A fighter's sustained turn

A fighter with \(\WS = 400\ \text{kg/m}^2\), \(A = 3.2\), \(e = 0.80\) and \(\CDz = 0.020\) must sustain 5 g at Mach 0.9 and \(6\ \text{km}\) (\(p = 47\,181\ \text{Pa}\), \(\sigma = 0.5385\)) at \(\beta = 0.8\). What \(\TW\) does it need?

Show solution
\[ q = 0.7(47\,181)(0.9)^2 = 26\,752\ \text{Pa}, \qquad K = \frac{1}{\pi(3.2)(0.80)} = 0.1243 \] \[ \beta g\,\WSo = 0.8(9.81)(400) = 3139\ \text{N/m}^2 \] \[ \TWo = \frac{0.8}{0.5385}\left[\frac{26\,752(0.020)}{3139} + \frac{25(0.1243)(3139)}{26\,752}\right] \] \[ \TWo = 1.486(0.1704 + 0.3648) = 0.795 \]

The induced drag of the 5 g turn is twice the zero-lift drag. A fighter's thrust-to-weight ratio comes from turns like this, not from its cruise.

Key takeaways

  • The master equation: \(\TWo = (\beta/\alpha)[q\CDz/(\beta g\WSo) + n^2K\beta g\WSo/q + (\dd h/\dd t)/V]\).
  • One engine failed: \(\TWo = [N/(N-1)](1/(L/D) + G)\), with \(G\) at least 2.4%, 2.7% or 3.0% for two, three or four engines.
  • Ceilings are defined by a residual climb rate: 100 ft/min for the service ceiling, 300 ft/min for the cruise ceiling.
  • A sustained turn multiplies the induced drag by \(n^2\); an instantaneous turn limits the wing loading to \(q\CLmax/(ng\beta)\).
  • The business jet needs 0.308 for its ceiling, 0.287 for takeoff and 0.248 with an engine failed. Next, Lesson 7 puts them all on one chart.
