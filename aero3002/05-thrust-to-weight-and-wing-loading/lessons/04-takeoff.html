Takeoff Distance

A takeoff accelerates the aircraft from rest to a safe climb speed and lifts it over a 35 ft obstacle. Thrust shortens it and a large wing shortens it; a high wing loading and thin air lengthen it. Physics says which group of variables matters, the takeoff parameter; the field lengths of real aircraft say how much runway each unit of it costs. The resulting correlation turns a runway length into a line of required thrust-to-weight ratio against wing loading.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the parts of a takeoff and the field length the certification rules define.
  • Explain why the takeoff distance grows with \(\WS\) and falls with \(\TW\), \(\sigma\) and \(C_{L_{\max},TO}\).
  • Explain how the takeoff correlation is fitted to the field lengths of similar aircraft, and when it can be trusted.
  • Use the takeoff parameter to estimate a jet's field length or a propeller aircraft's ground roll, and the \(\TW\) a field length requires at each wing loading.
  • Find the effect of a hot, high airport on the takeoff thrust.

What the takeoff distance is

A takeoff has three parts: the ground roll, from rest to the liftoff speed; the rotation, a few seconds as the pilot raises the nose; and the transition and climb over an obstacle, 35 ft for transports [8] and 50 ft for light aircraft [6]. For a multi-engine transport the certification rules add a margin for engine failure. The balanced field length is the longer of two cases: an engine fails at the decision speed and the pilot either continues the takeoff on the remaining engines or stops, both in the same distance. A takeoff with all engines must also clear the obstacle within the field length with a 15% margin [8], §25.113.

All of this is far too detailed for a first sizing. What matters is how the distance scales. The ground roll is roughly the kinetic energy at liftoff divided by the net accelerating force:

\[ s_G \approx \frac{\tfrac12 m V_{LO}^2}{T} \propto \frac{W/S}{\rho\,C_{L_{\max}}}\,\frac{W}{T} \]

because the liftoff speed is a fixed margin above the stall speed, so \(V_{LO}^2 \propto (W/S)/(\rho\,C_{L_{\max},TO})\). The takeoff distance therefore grows with one group of variables, the takeoff parameter:

Takeoff parameter (jets)

\[ \text{TOP} = \frac{W/S}{\sigma\,C_{L_{\max},TO}\,(T/W)}\ \ (\text{kg/m}^2) \]

\(W/S\) in kg/m² at takeoff, \(T/W\) at sea-level static thrust, \(\sigma\) the airport's density ratio. \(T/W\) and \(C_{L_{\max},TO}\) have no units, so TOP has the units of the wing loading.

The scaling argument says which variables matter, but not how much runway each kilogram per square metre of TOP costs. That depends on everything the argument left out: the speeds at liftoff and over the obstacle, the time to rotate, the climb to 35 ft, the engine-failure rules, the rolling friction, and the way the thrust falls as the aircraft speeds up.

A correlation fitted to real aircraft

Rather than model each of those effects, designers measure them all at once, from aircraft that have already been certified. The method is the same statistical one as the trends of Lesson 2:

  1. Collect aircraft of one class, here jet transports, whose certified field lengths are published.
  2. Work out each one's takeoff parameter from its wing loading, its thrust-to-weight ratio and an estimate of its takeoff lift coefficient.
  3. Plot field length against TOP, and fit a line through the origin. Its slope is the constant of the correlation.

Figure 4.1 does this for the 14 jet transports in Module 1's data that have a published takeoff field length [11], Table VII. Their takeoff lift coefficients are not published, so the figure assumes one value for all of them. With \(C_{L_{\max},TO} = 2.0\), the best line through the points has a slope of 2.24 m per kg/m² (35.9 ft per lb/ft²), and the aircraft scatter about it by \(\pm 11\%\). The correlation in common use, 2.34 m per kg/m² (37.5 ft per lb/ft²) [10], as given in [2] and in Chapter 5 of [5], was fitted in the same way to the transports of its day; these 14 aircraft give that slope if their takeoff lift coefficient is taken as 2.09.

Three lessons come out of the figure:

  • The constant and the lift coefficient go together. Assume a higher \(C_{L_{\max},TO}\) and every TOP shrinks, so the fitted slope grows in proportion. The correlation is only as good as the takeoff lift coefficient fed into it: estimate it the same way for every design you compare, and check the answer against similar aircraft.
  • It is a group average. The 737-200 and the C-5A, both built for shorter runways than their peers, take off 18 to 19% shorter than the line predicts, so one common \(C_{L_{\max},TO}\) undersells their flaps; the early Tu-104 takes 21% longer. A new design is predicted to within the scatter of the data, about \(\pm 10\) to 15%.
  • It holds only for the class it was fitted to. The data are jet transports with two to four engines, certified to the transport rules. A fighter, a short-takeoff aircraft or a propeller aircraft needs its own fit.

Takeoff field length of jet transports and business jets

\[ s_{TO} = 2.34\ \text{TOP}\ \ (\text{m}) \]

With TOP in kg/m². In US units, with \(W/S\) in lb/ft², \(s_{TO} = 37.5\ \text{TOP}\) ft.

The takeoff constraint

Solved for the thrust-to-weight ratio, the correlation gives the takeoff constraint:

Takeoff constraint

\[ \TWo \ge \frac{2.34}{s_{TO}\,\sigma\,C_{L_{\max},TO}}\,\WSo \]

A straight line through the origin: doubling the wing loading doubles the thrust needed for the same field length.

Example 4.1 — The business jet's takeoff

The business jet must take off in \(1200\ \text{m}\) at sea level on a standard day, with \(C_{L_{\max},TO} = 1.7\). What \(\TW\) does it need at the landing limit of Lesson 3, \(\WS = 250\ \text{kg/m}^2\)?

Show solution
\[ \TWo = \frac{2.34}{1200(1)(1.7)}\,(250) = 0.287 \]

That is more than the cruise needs (0.257, Lesson 2): so far, the takeoff sets the thrust. At the 248 kg/m² of Module 4, it is 0.284.

Takeoff lines

On a chart of \(\TW\) against \(\WS\), each field length is a straight line through the origin. Any design above the line takes off in that distance or less. Figure 4.2 draws the business jet's line and shows how the airport and the flaps move it.

Hot and high

An airport's density ratio falls with its altitude and its temperature. At a hot, high airport the takeoff needs more thrust in two ways. The air gives less lift, so the aircraft must go faster before it can fly; the \(\sigma\) in the takeoff parameter accounts for that. The engines also give less thrust, although many are flat rated: they hold their takeoff thrust up to a certain temperature. Like many first sizing methods, the takeoff constraint here keeps \(\TW\) at the sea-level static thrust and leaves that loss out; a careful analysis uses the engine's real takeoff thrust at the airport, which raises the requirement further.

Example 4.2 — A hot day at a high airport

The customer also wants to take off in \(1200\ \text{m}\) from an airport at \(1600\ \text{m}\) on a day \(20\ \text{K}\) hotter than standard. At \(1600\ \text{m}\) the standard pressure is \(83\,524\ \text{Pa}\) and the temperature \(277.75\ \text{K}\). Find the \(\TW\) needed at \(250\ \text{kg/m}^2\), and the field length the business jet would need there with \(\TW = 0.31\).

Show solution

At the same pressure and 297.75 K:

\[ \rho = \frac{83\,524}{287.05(297.75)} = 0.9772\ \text{kg/m}^3, \qquad \sigma = \frac{0.9772}{1.225} = 0.798 \] \[ \TWo = \frac{2.34}{1200(0.798)(1.7)}\,(250) = 0.360 \] \[ s_{TO} = 2.34\,\frac{250}{0.798(1.7)(0.31)} = 1392\ \text{m} \]

A 25% increase in thrust before any loss of engine thrust is counted, or a longer runway. Customers rarely require full performance on the worst day at every airport; the designer and the customer agree which airports matter.

Propeller aircraft and takeoff charts

Light propeller aircraft are certified on their ground roll and the distance to clear 50 ft, with no engine-failure balance [6], §23.2115. Their engines fix the power rather than the thrust, and a propeller's thrust falls as the speed rises. So their takeoff parameter uses the power loading of Lesson 2, and the fit to their ground rolls is a curve rather than a line:

Ground roll of light propeller aircraft

\[ \text{TOP}_P = \frac{W/S}{\sigma\,C_{L_{\max},TO}\,(P/W)} \] \[ s_G = 0.503\,\text{TOP}_P + 3.11 \times 10^{-4}\,\text{TOP}_P^{\,2}\ \ (\text{m}) \]

\(W/S\) in kg/m² and \(P/W\) in kW/kg, so \(\text{TOP}_P\) is in kg²/(m² kW). In US units, with \(W/S\) in lb/ft² and \(W/P\) in lb/hp, \(s_G = 4.9\,\text{TOP}_P + 0.009\,\text{TOP}_P^{\,2}\) ft [2], Section 3.1. The distance to clear 50 ft is longer.

Textbooks print correlations like these as charts of takeoff distance against the takeoff parameter, one curve for each class of aircraft, so that a designer can read a distance off the chart, or the TOP a runway allows. Figure 4.3 is such a chart, built from the two correlations of this lesson. Solved for the power loading, a required ground roll gives a line through the origin on a chart of \(\PW\) against \(\WS\), just as the field length does for a jet.

Example 4.3 — A light single's ground roll

A light single of \(1100\ \text{kg}\) has a \(16.2\ \text{m}^2\) wing, a \(134\ \text{kW}\) engine and \(C_{L_{\max},TO} = 1.6\). Estimate its ground roll at sea level.

Show solution
\[ \frac WS = \frac{1100}{16.2} = 67.9\ \text{kg/m}^2, \qquad \frac PW = \frac{134}{1100} = 0.1218\ \text{kW/kg} \] \[ \text{TOP}_P = \frac{67.9}{1(1.6)(0.1218)} = 348.4\ \text{kg}^2/(\text{m}^2\,\text{kW}) \] \[ s_G = 0.503(348.4) + 3.11 \times 10^{-4}(348.4)^2 = 175 + 38 = 213\ \text{m} \]

About 700 ft. The squared term adds about a fifth: at this TOP the propeller's falling thrust already matters. Like the jet correlation, this is a class average, good to the scatter of the light aircraft it was fitted to.

Key takeaways

  • The ground roll scales with the takeoff parameter \(\text{TOP} = (W/S)/(\sigma C_{L_{\max},TO}\,T/W)\).
  • The constant is statistical: a fit to the field lengths of similar aircraft. For jet transports and business jets, \(s_{TO} \approx 2.34\ \text{TOP}\) m with \(W/S\) in kg/m², and real aircraft scatter about it by 10 to 15%.
  • Light propeller aircraft use \(\text{TOP}_P = (W/S)/(\sigma C_{L_{\max},TO}\,P/W)\) and a curve fitted to their ground rolls.
  • The takeoff constraint is a straight line through the origin: \(\TWo \ge 2.34\,\WSo/(s_{TO}\,\sigma\,C_{L_{\max},TO})\).
  • The business jet needs \(\TW = 0.287\) at 250 kg/m², more than its cruise needs; a hot, high airport raises it to 0.36.
