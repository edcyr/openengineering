A

Back to top
Actuator disk
Momentum theory's model of a propeller: a thin disk of area \(A = \pi D^2/4\) that adds a pressure jump to the air passing through it. It gives \(T = 2\rho Av_i(V + v_i)\) and \(P = T(V + v_i)\).
See: Lesson 6Related: Induced velocity \(v_i\), Ideal efficiency \(\eta_i\), Disk loading
Afterburner
Extra fuel burned in the jet pipe behind the turbine for a short burst of thrust, at a very high fuel consumption. Supersonic combat aircraft use low-bypass turbofans with afterburners.
See: Lesson 1Related: Turbojet, Bypass ratio
Avgas
Aviation gasoline for piston engines, such as 100LL: about 720 kg/m³ and 43.7 MJ/kg.
See: Lesson 7Related: Jet A and Jet A-1, Heating value \(h\)

B

Back to top
Boundary layer
The air next to a surface, slowed by friction. For a turbulent layer, \(\delta \approx 0.37x/Re_x^{0.2}\): about 0.11 m at the business jet's inlets, 10 m from the nose, well inside the 0.29 m gap to its nacelles.
See: Lesson 5Related: Diverter, Inlet
Bypass ratio
The air mass flow that passes around a turbofan's core, pushed by the fan alone, divided by the mass flow through the core. A higher ratio gives a larger, slower jet and a better propulsive efficiency, but a bigger, heavier fan and nacelle: typically about 2 to 5 on business jets, 5 to 12 on airliners.
See: Lesson 1Related: Turbofan, Propulsive efficiency \(\eta_p\)

C

Back to top
Capture area \(A_0\)
The area, far ahead of the inlet, of the streamtube of air that enters the engine: \(A_0 = \dot m/(\rho V)\). Smaller than the lip in cruise, much larger at low speed. The business jet's is 0.267 m² in cruise.
See: Lesson 5Related: Highlight area \(A_{hl}\), Spillage, Inlet
Center tank
A tank in the wing box where it crosses the fuselage; on a low wing it lies under the cabin floor. The business jet's adds 0.60 m³ and makes its fuel fit.
See: Lesson 7Related: Wing box, Integral tank
Crossfeed
A valve that lets either tank feed either engine, to balance the fuel between the wings or to feed both engines from one side.
See: Lesson 7Related: Feed system, Center tank
Cruise ceiling
The highest altitude at which the aircraft can still climb at 300 ft/min at its cruise speed. With the layout's drag, the business jet's 12 km ceiling needs a takeoff \(\TW\) of 0.292 with \(\alpha = \sigma\), but 0.454 with the high-bypass lapse.
See: Lesson 8Related: Thrust lapse \(\alpha\), Engine deck

D

Back to top
Design loop
Running the sizing again with the numbers the layout and the components have replaced. With \(\LDmax = 17.52\) the business jet resizes to 6665 kg; each turn changes less, and the design settles.
See: Lesson 8Related: Growth factor
Disk loading
A propeller's thrust per unit of disk area, \(T/A\). The lower it is, the smaller the induced velocity and the higher the efficiency: large, lightly loaded propellers are efficient.
See: Lesson 6Related: Actuator disk, Ideal efficiency \(\eta_i\)
Diverter
A gap or ramp that sends the fuselage's boundary layer around an inlet built into the fuselage. Podded engines get the same effect from the pylon's standoff.
See: Lesson 5Related: Boundary layer, Pylon
Dry weight
The bare engine's weight with its accessories, but without the nacelle, inlet, exhaust or fluids, as the type-certificate data sheet lists it. The FJ44-3A's is 242.7 kg [5].
See: Lesson 3Related: Type-certificate data sheet, Rubber engine

E

Back to top
Engine deck
The engine maker's performance data, giving thrust and fuel flow at every altitude, speed and throttle setting. It replaces the lapse models once an engine is chosen.
See: Lesson 2, Lesson 8Related: Thrust lapse \(\alpha\), Cruise ceiling
Engine-out yawing moment
The turn a live engine gives when the other fails: \(N \approx T_{\text{eng}}y_e\). The rudder must balance it at the minimum control speed. The business jet's rear engines give 13.3 kN m, half that of engines on the wing.
See: Lesson 4Related: Nacelle
Exhaust mixer
A device that combines the bypass and core streams in a full-length bypass duct before the nozzle, gaining a little thrust and noise reduction for the weight of a longer nacelle. The FJ44-2A and FJ44-1AP added one [5].
See: Lesson 5Related: Nozzle

F

Back to top
Feed system
The pumps, valves, lines and vents that bring fuel from the tanks to the engines. Each engine normally draws from its own tank through boost pumps; vents keep the tanks near outside pressure, and single-point refueling fills every tank from one connector.
See: Lesson 7Related: Crossfeed, Fuel jettison
Flat rating
An engine control that holds the takeoff thrust constant up to an ambient temperature, using the turbine's margin on a cool day: 26.1 °C for the FJ44-3A [5], 30 °C for the CFM56-5B [6]. Above it the thrust falls.
See: Lesson 2Related: Installed thrust, Thrust lapse \(\alpha\)
Fuel flow \(\dot m_f\)
The mass of fuel burned per second, \(\dot m_f = c_T T\). The business jet burns about 295 kg/h in cruise.
See: Lesson 2Related: Thrust-specific fuel consumption \(c_T\)
Fuel jettison
A system to dump fuel in an emergency, so that an aircraft whose landing weight is far below its takeoff weight can return and land soon after takeoff. Light jets do not need one.
See: Lesson 7Related: Feed system

G

Back to top
Gross thrust
The momentum flux of the jet, \(\dot m V_j\). The net thrust is the gross thrust less the ram drag.
See: Lesson 1Related: Ram drag, Net thrust
Growth factor
The change in takeoff weight for each kilogram added to the fixed or empty weight, with the aircraft resized. About 5.5 for the business jet: 60 kg more of engine adds about 330 kg to \(\Wo\).
See: Lesson 8Related: Design loop

H

Back to top
Heating value \(h\)
The heat a kilogram of fuel releases when burned: about 43 MJ/kg for kerosene, 120 MJ/kg for hydrogen.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 7Related: Jet A and Jet A-1, Liquid hydrogen
Helical tip Mach number
The Mach number at which a propeller tip meets the air, \(\sqrt{(\pi Dn)^2 + V^2}/a\). Above about 0.85 to 0.9 the tips lose efficiency and grow noisy, which caps the diameter and the flight speed.
See: Lesson 6Related: Disk loading
Highlight area \(A_{hl}\)
The area enclosed by the inlet's lip, its leading edge. An inlet is sized so that the cruise capture streamtube is a little smaller than it.
See: Lesson 5Related: Capture area \(A_0\), Spillage

I

Back to top
Ideal efficiency \(\eta_i\)
The actuator disk's efficiency, \(\eta_i = V/(V + v_i)\): the upper limit for a propeller of that disk loading. Real propellers reach about 0.80 to 0.88 in cruise.
See: Lesson 6Related: Actuator disk, Induced velocity \(v_i\)
Induced velocity \(v_i\)
The speed a propeller adds to the air at the disk; far behind, the slipstream has gained \(2v_i\).
See: Lesson 6Related: Actuator disk, Ideal efficiency \(\eta_i\)
Inlet
The duct that slows the air from the flight speed to about Mach 0.4 to 0.6 at the fan, losing as little total pressure as possible and delivering it evenly. Each percent of total pressure lost costs a percent or more of thrust.
See: Lesson 5Related: Capture area \(A_0\), Pitot inlet, S-duct
Installed thrust
The thrust on the aircraft, a few percent below the data sheet's uninstalled thrust: the inlet loses pressure, the nozzle and nacelle add drag, and the systems take bleed air and shaft power.
See: Lesson 2Related: Flat rating, Type-certificate data sheet
Integral tank
A tank formed by the sealed structure itself, usually the wing box between the spars: light, close to the center of gravity, and relieving the wing's bending in flight.
See: Lesson 7Related: Wing box, Center tank

J

Back to top
Jet A and Jet A-1
Kerosene for turbine engines; Jet A-1 has a lower freezing point than Jet A. About 800 kg/m³ and 43.2 MJ/kg. Sustainable aviation fuels are blended into it.
See: Lesson 7Related: Avgas, Liquid hydrogen, Heating value \(h\)
Jet velocity \(V_j\)
The speed of the jet leaving the nozzle. For a given thrust, a lower jet velocity needs more mass flow but wastes less energy.
See: Lesson 1Related: Propulsive efficiency \(\eta_p\), Net thrust

L

Back to top
Liquid hydrogen
A fuel with nearly three times kerosene's energy per kilogram (120 MJ/kg) but a density of only 70.8 kg/m³, stored at −253 °C in thick, round, insulated tanks. For the same energy it needs about four times kerosene's volume.
See: Lesson 7Related: Jet A and Jet A-1, Heating value \(h\)

M

Back to top
Mass flow \(\dot m\)
The mass of air through the engine per second. The business jet's engines pass about 19.6 kg/s each in cruise.
See: Lesson 1Related: Net thrust, Capture area \(A_0\)

N

Back to top
Nacelle
Everything around the engine: inlet lip and diffuser, cowling and nozzle. A first nacelle is about \(D_n = 1.1H_{\text{eng}}\) by \(l_n = 1.2L_{\text{eng}}\), wetted area \(\pi D_n l_n\); the business jet's are 0.80 m by 1.77 m.
See: Lesson 4Related: Pylon, Inlet
Net thrust
The force from speeding up the air: \(T = \dot m(V_j - V)\), with the jet fully expanded and the fuel's mass neglected.
See: Lesson 1Related: Gross thrust, Ram drag, Mass flow \(\dot m\)
Nozzle
The duct that turns the gas's pressure into jet velocity: convergent on subsonic transports, convergent-divergent and variable on supersonic aircraft.
See: Lesson 5Related: Exhaust mixer, Thrust reverser

O

Back to top
Overall efficiency \(\eta_0\)
The share of the fuel's heat that becomes useful work, \(\eta_0 = TV/(\dot m_f h) = \eta_{th}\eta_p\). The business jet's engines reach about 0.30 in cruise.
See: Lesson 1Related: Thermal efficiency \(\eta_{th}\), Propulsive efficiency \(\eta_p\), Thrust-specific fuel consumption \(c_T\)

P

Back to top
Pitot inlet
A short, round inlet at the front of a podded nacelle: the lightest, shortest and most efficient kind, used on nearly all transports and business jets.
See: Lesson 5Related: Inlet, S-duct
Propulsive efficiency \(\eta_p\)
The share of the jet's added kinetic energy that does useful work: \(\eta_p = 2/(1 + V_j/V)\). It rises as the jet velocity approaches the flight speed, so efficient engines move a lot of air a little faster.
See: Lesson 1Related: Jet velocity \(V_j\), Overall efficiency \(\eta_0\), Bypass ratio
Pylon
The structure that carries an engine's weight, thrust and gyroscopic loads into the airframe, and its fuel, electrical, bleed-air and control lines.
See: Lesson 4Related: Nacelle, Rotor burst

R

Back to top
Ram drag
The momentum of the air taken in, \(\dot m V\), which the engine must overcome. It grows with flight speed, so thrust falls with speed, fastest for engines with slow jets.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 2Related: Gross thrust, Thrust lapse \(\alpha\)
Rotor burst
The failure of a fan, compressor or turbine disc, which the casing cannot contain. The engines are placed so that fragments in the narrow band around each rotor's plane miss the passengers, tanks and duplicated systems.
See: Lesson 4Related: Pylon
Rubber engine
An existing engine scaled to the thrust a design needs, keeping its cycle: \(L \propto SF^{0.4}\), \(D \propto SF^{0.5}\), \(W \propto SF^{1.1}\) [1]. The business jet's is the FJ44-3A at \(SF = 0.842\): 1.475 m long, 0.725 m high and 200.8 kg.
See: Lesson 3Related: Scale factor \(SF\), Dry weight, Type-certificate data sheet

S

Back to top
S-duct
An inlet duct that bends to bring air to an engine buried in the fuselage or tail; longer and heavier than a pitot inlet, with more loss and distortion.
See: Lesson 5Related: Pitot inlet, Inlet
Scale factor \(SF\)
The required thrust divided by the base engine's, \(SF = T_{\text{req}}/T_{\text{base}}\). The rubber-engine exponents are trusted between about 0.8 and 1.25.
See: Lesson 3Related: Rubber engine
Spillage
The air that reaches an inlet's face in cruise but does not enter it, flowing around the lip, because the capture streamtube is smaller than the highlight area.
See: Lesson 5Related: Capture area \(A_0\), Highlight area \(A_{hl}\)
Static thrust
The thrust at zero flight speed. For an ideal propeller of shaft power \(P\), \((2\rho AP^2)^{1/3}\): 49.7 kN for a 3.9 m propeller at 2051 kW.
See: Lesson 6Related: Actuator disk

T

Back to top
Thermal efficiency \(\eta_{th}\)
The share of the fuel's heat that becomes kinetic energy in the jet; it rises with the core's pressures and temperatures.
See: Lesson 1Related: Overall efficiency \(\eta_0\), Propulsive efficiency \(\eta_p\)
Thrust lapse \(\alpha\)
The installed thrust divided by the sea-level static thrust. Module 5 took \(\alpha = \sigma\); for high-bypass turbofans a correlation [2] gives \(\alpha = \delta_0(1 - 0.49\sqrt M)\): 0.163 at the business jet's cruise, against \(\sigma = 0.254\).
See: Lesson 2Related: Ram drag, Engine deck, Cruise ceiling
Thrust reverser
Doors or cascades that turn the jet forward after touchdown. Many light jets do without them to save weight and maintenance.
See: Lesson 5Related: Nozzle
Thrust-specific fuel consumption \(c_T\)
The fuel burned per second for each newton of thrust, \(c_T = \dot m_f/T = V/(\eta_0 h)\). In mg/(N s) it is \(28.33\,C\), with Module 3's \(C\) in h\(^{-1}\): 18.4 mg/(N s) for the business jet.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 2Related: Overall efficiency \(\eta_0\), Fuel flow \(\dot m_f\)
Tractor and pusher
A tractor propeller is ahead of its engine, in clean air; a pusher is behind, in the wake of the wing and fuselage, noisier and less efficient but leaving the nose free.
See: Lesson 6Related: Helical tip Mach number
Turbofan
A gas turbine whose fan pushes part of the air around the core. It suits high subsonic speeds; the business jet takes two.
See: Lesson 1Related: Bypass ratio, Turbojet, Turboprop
Turbojet
A gas turbine that sends all its air through the core: the lightest engine for its thrust and the best above Mach 2, but wasteful at subsonic speeds.
See: Lesson 1Related: Turbofan, Afterburner
Turboprop
A gas turbine driving a propeller through a reduction gearbox: light for its power and very efficient up to about 600 to 700 km/h. Rated by shaft power; the PW127M gives 2051 kW [8].
See: Lesson 1Related: Helical tip Mach number, Turbofan
Type-certificate data sheet
The certifying authority's published record of an engine model: its certified thrust or power and flat-rating temperature, dry weight, dimensions and limits.
See: Lesson 3Related: Dry weight, Flat rating

U

Back to top
Unusable fuel and expansion space
Fuel trapped at the bottom of the tanks, and room left for the fuel to expand on a hot day. The lessons allow 3% of the tank volume for both.
See: Lesson 7Related: Wing box

V

Back to top
Vent and surge tanks
Small tanks near the wing tips that let the fuel tanks breathe and catch fuel that sloshes or overflows; the main tanks stop short of them.
See: Lesson 7Related: Feed system, Wing box

W

Back to top
Wing box
The structural box between the front and rear spars, which also serves as the fuel tank. Measured between spars at 15% and 60% of the chord to 85% of the half span, the business jet's wings hold 2.05 m³.
See: Lesson 7Related: Integral tank, Center tank, Unusable fuel and expansion space
