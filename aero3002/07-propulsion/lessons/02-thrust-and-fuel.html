Thrust, Fuel Flow and Altitude

An engine's thrust on a test stand at sea level is the number on its data sheet. In flight it gives less: the air it swallows is thinner, and it arrives already moving. Module 5 corrected for this with the simplest possible rule, thrust in proportion to the air density. This lesson shows where that rule comes from, where it falls short, and what it means for the business jet's ceiling.

Learning objectives

  • Find an engine's fuel flow from its thrust-specific fuel consumption.
  • Explain how ram drag makes a turbofan's thrust fall with flight speed.
  • Estimate the thrust lapse with altitude and Mach number, and judge Module 5's \(\alpha = \sigma\).
  • Explain flat ratings and installation losses.

Fuel flow

The thrust-specific fuel consumption \(c_T\) is the fuel burned per second for each newton of thrust. Module 3 gave it for the business jet as the Breguet constant \(C\), in reciprocal hours; the two are the same quantity in different units:

Fuel flow

\[ \dot m_f = c_T\,T, \qquad c_T\ [\text{mg/(N s)}] = 28.33\,C\ [\text{h}^{-1}] \]

Example 2.1 — The business jet's cruise fuel

The business jet cruises at \(C = 0.65\ \text{h}^{-1}\) with a total thrust of \(4458\ \text{N}\) (Lesson 1). Find its fuel flow, and the fuel for 4600 km at 850 km/h.

Show solution
\[ c_T = 28.33(0.65) = 18.4\ \text{mg/(N s)} \] \[ \dot m_f = 18.4 \times 10^{-6}(4458) = 0.0821\ \text{kg/s} = 295\ \text{kg/h} \] \[ t = \frac{4600}{850} = 5.41\ \text{h}, \qquad m_f = 295(5.41) = 1599\ \text{kg} \]

About 1600 kg for the cruise alone. Module 3's 1991 kg also covers the climb, descent, loiter and reserves.

The thrust in cruise is only a fraction of the takeoff rating: here about 2.2 kN per engine against 10.6 kN on the ground. Most of that difference is the lapse with altitude and speed, which is the subject of the rest of this lesson.

Thrust and flight speed

From Lesson 1, \(T = \dot m V_j - \dot m V\). The gross thrust \(\dot m V_j\) depends on the engine; the ram drag \(\dot m V\) grows with the flight speed. If the mass flow and jet velocity stayed fixed, the thrust would fall in a straight line, to zero when the aircraft reached the jet velocity:

\[ \frac{T}{T_0} = 1 - \frac{V}{V_j} \]

A turbojet, with a fast jet, hardly notices the ram drag at takeoff speeds. A high-bypass turbofan, whose jet is slow, loses a good share of its thrust before it even leaves the runway. In a real engine the ram pressure also pushes more air in as the speed rises, which offsets part of the loss, but the trend remains: the higher the bypass ratio, the faster the thrust falls with speed.

Thrust and altitude

At a given rotor speed, an engine swallows a volume of air per second, so its mass flow, and with it the thrust, falls with the air density. That is Module 5's thrust lapse:

\[ \alpha = \frac{T}{T_0} \approx \sigma = \frac{\rho}{\rho_0} \]

It ignores the flight speed, which matters in two opposite ways: ram compression packs more air into the inlet, and ram drag takes thrust away. For high-bypass turbofans at full throttle, a widely used correlation [2] combines them through the total pressure the inlet sees:

Thrust lapse of a high-bypass turbofan

\[ \alpha = \delta_0\left(1 - 0.49\sqrt M\right), \qquad \delta_0 = \frac{p}{p_0}\left(1 + 0.2M^2\right)^{3.5} \]

\(p/p_0\) is the static-pressure ratio of the standard atmosphere. The correlation holds below the engine's flat-rating temperature, which covers the climb and cruise.

Example 2.2 — The business jet's ceiling, again

Module 5 sized the business jet for a 300 ft/min climb at Mach 0.80 and 12 km, which needed \(\TWo = 0.308\) with \(\alpha = \sigma = 0.2537\). What takeoff \(\TW\) would the same climb need with the high-bypass correlation? Compare it with the Citation CJ3 Gen2's \(\TW = 0.406\) (two FJ44-3A of 12.54 kN [5] at 6291 kg [10]).

Show solution

The thrust the climb needs at 12 km does not change: \(0.308(0.2537) = 0.0781\) of the takeoff weight. Only the lapse does:

\[ \delta_0 = \frac{19\,330}{101\,325}\left(1 + 0.2(0.8)^2\right)^{3.5} = 0.2908 \] \[ \alpha = 0.2908\left(1 - 0.49\sqrt{0.8}\right) = 0.163 \] \[ \TWo = \frac{0.0781}{0.163} = 0.478 \]

Half as much thrust again. The business jet's engines have a moderate bypass ratio, so the truth lies between the two estimates; the CJ3's 0.406 sits there too. A real design uses the engine maker's performance data, its engine deck, for every flight condition. Lesson 8 returns to what this means for the business jet.

Flat ratings and installation losses

On a hot day the air is thin and an engine would give less thrust, but certified engines are flat rated: the control holds the takeoff thrust constant up to an ambient temperature, using the margin the turbine has on a cool day. The FJ44-3A's data sheet, for example, gives its 12.54 kN of takeoff thrust up to 26.1 °C [5]; the CFM56-5B holds its rating up to 30 °C [6]. Above that temperature the thrust falls, which is one reason hot-day takeoffs are long (Module 5).

The data-sheet thrust is also uninstalled: measured on a test stand with an ideal inlet and nothing taken from the engine. On the aircraft the inlet loses some pressure, the nozzle and nacelle add drag, and the systems take bleed air and shaft power for the cabin pressure, anti-icing and electrical power. The installed thrust is a few percent lower, and the installed fuel consumption a few percent higher; conceptual design often takes both as a fixed allowance until the installation is known.

Key takeaways

  • Fuel flow \(\dot m_f = c_T T\), with \(c_T\) in mg/(N s) equal to \(28.33\,C\) in h\(^{-1}\).
  • Ram drag \(\dot m V\) makes the thrust fall with speed, fastest for high-bypass engines with slow jets.
  • \(\alpha = \sigma\) is a first estimate; for high-bypass turbofans, \(\alpha = \delta_0(1 - 0.49\sqrt M)\) gives 0.163 at the business jet's cruise, against \(\sigma = 0.254\).
  • Engines are flat rated to a temperature; installed thrust is a few percent below the data sheet's.
