Inlets and Nozzles

The engine's fan wants air arriving smoothly at about half the speed of sound, whether the aircraft is parked with the engines at full power or cruising at Mach 0.8. The inlet makes that happen, and the nozzle at the other end turns the engine's pressure into the jet. This lesson sizes the business jet's inlet streamtube, checks that its nacelles stand clear of the fuselage's boundary layer, and surveys nozzles and thrust reversers.

Learning objectives

  • State what an inlet must do at takeoff and in cruise.
  • Compute the capture streamtube of an inlet from the engine's mass flow.
  • Compare inlet types and locations, and size the gap that keeps the boundary layer out.
  • Describe nozzles, mixers and thrust reversers.

What the inlet does

The inlet slows the air from the flight speed to the speed the fan wants, about Mach 0.4 to 0.6, while losing as little total pressure as possible: each percent of total pressure lost typically costs a percent or more of thrust. It must deliver the air evenly, because a fan meeting distorted flow, faster on one side than the other, can stall or surge. And it must do this from a standstill on a hot runway to the cruise, from straight flight to a steep climb with the wind across the runway.

The capture streamtube

Far ahead of the inlet, the air that will enter the engine fills a streamtube whose area follows from the mass flow:

Free-stream capture area

\[ A_0 = \frac{\dot m}{\rho V} \]

\(\dot m\) the engine's air mass flow, \(\rho\) and \(V\) the free-stream density and speed.

An inlet is sized for the cruise, where it spends most of its life. There the streamtube is smaller than the inlet's lip, the highlight area \(A_{hl}\): the air slows down before it reaches the lip, and the excess spills around the outside. At low speed the opposite happens. With the engine at full power and the aircraft barely moving, \(A_0\) is far larger than the lip, and air is sucked in from the sides and even from behind the lip. That is why subsonic inlets have thick, rounded lips: a sharp lip would let the flow separate as it turns into the inlet, and the fan would see a distorted, low-pressure flow.

Example 5.1 — The business jet's inlet streamtube

In cruise each engine passes about \(19.6\ \text{kg/s}\) (Lesson 1, with \(V_j = 350\ \text{m/s}\)) at Mach 0.80 and 12 km, where \(\rho = 0.3108\ \text{kg/m}^3\) and \(V = 236.1\ \text{m/s}\). At takeoff it passes about \(30\ \text{kg/s}\). Find the capture area and its equivalent diameter in cruise, and at 60 m/s on the takeoff run.

Show solution
\[ \text{Cruise: } A_0 = \frac{19.6}{0.3108(236.1)} = 0.267\ \text{m}^2, \qquad D_0 = \sqrt{\frac{4(0.267)}{\pi}} = 0.583\ \text{m} \] \[ \text{Takeoff: } A_0 = \frac{30}{1.225(60)} = 0.408\ \text{m}^2, \qquad D_0 = 0.721\ \text{m} \]

An inlet with a lip about 0.6 m across, inside a 0.80 m nacelle, swallows the cruise streamtube with a little spillage, and on the takeoff run draws in a stream half as large again as its own area.

Inlet types and locations

  • A pitot inlet is a short, round duct at the front of a podded nacelle: the lightest, shortest and most efficient, used on nearly all transports and business jets.
  • An S-duct brings air to an engine buried in the fuselage or the tail, as for the center engine of a trijet; it is longer and heavier, and its bends add pressure loss and distortion.
  • Side and chin inlets feed engines inside a fighter's fuselage; supersonic aircraft add ramps or cones that slow the air through shock waves before it reaches the duct.

Rear-mounted engines have their own concern: at high angles of attack the wing's wake rises toward them, and a stalled wing can starve the inlets of smooth air. The business jet's inlets sit high on the tail cone, 2.8 m behind the wing's trailing edge, which is typical of the type.

Keeping the boundary layer out

The air next to the fuselage has been slowed by friction along its whole length: the boundary layer. An inlet that swallows it gets slow, uneven air. Engines on the fuselage therefore stand off on pylons, and inlets built into the fuselage have a diverter, a gap or ramp that sends the boundary layer around them. For a turbulent boundary layer, the flat-plate estimate of its thickness at a distance \(x\) from the nose is

Turbulent boundary-layer thickness

\[ \delta \approx \frac{0.37\,x}{Re_x^{0.2}}, \qquad Re_x = \frac{\rho V x}{\mu} \]

\(\mu\) the air's viscosity, \(1.42 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{Pa s}\) at 12 km and \(1.79 \times 10^{-5}\) at sea level.

Example 5.2 — How far the nacelles stand off

The business jet's inlets are about 10 m from the nose, with a 0.29 m gap between the nacelle and the fuselage. How thick is the boundary layer there in cruise?

Show solution
\[ Re_x = \frac{0.3108(236.1)(10)}{1.42 \times 10^{-5}} = 5.16 \times 10^7, \qquad \delta = \frac{0.37(10)}{(5.16 \times 10^7)^{0.2}} = 0.106\ \text{m} \]

The gap is nearly three times the boundary layer: the inlets see clean air. At takeoff the layer is about the same, 0.11 m, because the Reynolds number changes little.

Nozzles and thrust reversers

The nozzle turns the pressure of the hot gas, and of the bypass air, into jet velocity. Subsonic transports use simple convergent nozzles; supersonic aircraft need convergent-divergent ones that can change shape. A turbofan can exhaust its two streams separately, through a short fan duct and a core nozzle, or combine them in a mixer inside a full-length bypass duct, which gains a little thrust and noise reduction for the weight of a longer nacelle. The FJ44's data sheet records such changes: the FJ44-2A and FJ44-1AP added an exhaust mixer to the original FJ44-1A [5].

A thrust reverser turns the jet forward after touchdown. Engines on wings use cascades that open in the fan duct; engines on the tail cone often use clamshell or bucket doors that close behind the nozzle. Module 5's landing distance credited reversers with a factor of 0.66 on the braking distance (Chapter 5 of [1]); the reversers cost weight and maintenance, and many light jets do without them.

Key takeaways

  • The inlet slows the air to about Mach 0.5 at the fan with little pressure loss and even flow, from the runway to the cruise.
  • Capture area \(A_0 = \dot m/(\rho V)\): smaller than the lip in cruise (spillage), larger at low speed (suction), hence rounded lips.
  • Pitot inlets on pods suit transports; buried engines need S-ducts; rear engines must stay clear of the wing's wake.
  • Turbulent boundary layer \(\delta \approx 0.37x/Re_x^{0.2}\): about 0.11 m at the business jet's inlets, well inside its 0.29 m gap.
