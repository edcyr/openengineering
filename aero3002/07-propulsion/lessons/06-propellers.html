Propellers

A propeller is the extreme of Lesson 1's rule: it moves a very large mass of air a little faster, so it is the most efficient way to make thrust at low speeds. The business jet does not need one, but regional transports, commuters, trainers and light aircraft do. This lesson treats the propeller as a disk that speeds up the air, finds its thrust and efficiency, and shows what limits its size.

Learning objectives

  • Find a propeller's thrust and ideal efficiency from momentum theory.
  • Explain why a large, lightly loaded propeller is efficient.
  • Compute the helical tip Mach number and the diameter it allows.
  • State the clearances a propeller needs from the ground and the airframe.

The propeller as an actuator disk

Momentum theory replaces the blades by a thin disk of area \(A = \pi D^2/4\) that adds a pressure jump to the air passing through it. The air speeds up by an induced velocity \(v_i\) at the disk and by \(2v_i\) far behind it. Applying the momentum and energy equations to the streamtube through the disk gives:

Actuator-disk thrust, power and efficiency

\[ T = 2\rho A\,v_i\,(V + v_i), \qquad P = T\,(V + v_i), \qquad \eta_i = \frac{TV}{P} = \frac{V}{V + v_i} \]

\(P\) the shaft power; \(\eta_i\) the ideal efficiency, which a real propeller does not reach because of blade drag, swirl and tip losses.

For a given thrust, a larger disk needs a smaller induced velocity, so less of the power is wasted in the slipstream: the efficiency rises as the disk loading \(T/A\) falls. At zero speed the efficiency is zero by definition, but the thrust is not; for a given power the static thrust is

\[ T_{\text{static}} = \left(2\rho A P^2\right)^{1/3} \]

Example 6.1 — A regional turboprop's propeller

A turboprop of 2051 kW (the PW127M's maximum take-off power) turns a propeller 3.9 m in diameter. Find the ideal static thrust at sea level, and the ideal thrust and efficiency at 100 m/s.

Show solution
\[ A = \frac{\pi(3.9)^2}{4} = 11.95\ \text{m}^2 \] \[ T_{\text{static}} = \left[2(1.225)(11.95)(2.051 \times 10^6)^2\right]^{1/3} = 49.7\ \text{kN} \]

At 100 m/s, solve \(P = 2\rho A v_i(V + v_i)^2\) for the induced velocity:

\[ v_i = 6.2\ \text{m/s}, \qquad T = \frac{P}{V + v_i} = \frac{2.051 \times 10^6}{106.2} = 19.3\ \text{kN}, \qquad \eta_i = \frac{100}{106.2} = 0.94 \]

Real propellers reach about 80 to 88% in cruise, and much less than the ideal static thrust. The thrust falls steeply with speed at constant power: \(T = \eta P/V\).

The tip Mach number

A blade tip moves around the disk at \(V_t = \pi D n\), with \(n\) the rotational speed in revolutions per second, while the aircraft carries it forward at \(V\). It meets the air at the helical tip speed:

Helical tip Mach number

\[ M_{\text{tip}} = \frac{\sqrt{(\pi D n)^2 + V^2}}{a} \]

Above about 0.85 to 0.9 the tips lose efficiency and grow very noisy, as the flow over them approaches the speed of sound.

The tip Mach number is what caps the diameter, and it is why propellers lose out above about 650 km/h: at high flight speed the forward component alone uses up most of the allowance. Turboprops therefore turn their propellers slowly, through a reduction gearbox, and often slower still in cruise than at takeoff. More blades let a propeller absorb more power without a larger diameter, at the cost of weight and complexity: regional turboprops use four to six blades.

Example 6.2 — Turning the propeller slower in cruise

The 3.9 m propeller of Example 6.1 turns at 1200 rpm. Find its helical tip Mach number in cruise at 510 km/h and 7 km (\(a = 312.3\ \text{m/s}\)), and again at 1000 rpm.

Show solution
\[ V = \frac{510}{3.6} = 141.7\ \text{m/s} \] \[ V_t = \pi(3.9)\frac{1200}{60} = 245.0\ \text{m/s} \] \[ M_{\text{tip}} = \frac{\sqrt{245.0^2 + 141.7^2}}{312.3} = 0.906 \] \[ \text{At 1000 rpm: } V_t = 204.2\ \text{m/s}, \qquad M_{\text{tip}} = \frac{\sqrt{204.2^2 + 141.7^2}}{312.3} = 0.796 \]

Too fast at 1200 rpm; comfortable at 1000. Turboprops cruise at a lower propeller speed for exactly this reason, which also makes the cabin quieter.

Placing the propeller

The certification rules for transports (CS/FAR 25.925) ask for at least 7 in of ground clearance with the nose-wheel landing gear compressed and the aircraft in its most adverse attitude, and at least 1 in of radial clearance between the blade tips and the structure, with more where vibration calls for it. In practice the gaps to the fuselage are much larger, for noise. A tractor propeller, ahead of the engine, works in clean air; a pusher, behind, works in the wake of the wing and fuselage, which makes it noisier and less efficient but frees the nose and cleans the wing. And as Module 6 noted, no passenger should sit in the propeller's plane.

Key takeaways

  • Actuator disk: \(T = 2\rho Av_i(V + v_i)\), \(P = T(V + v_i)\), \(\eta_i = V/(V + v_i)\); static thrust \((2\rho AP^2)^{1/3}\).
  • A larger, lightly loaded disk is more efficient; real propellers reach about 0.8 to 0.88 in cruise.
  • The helical tip Mach number \(\sqrt{(\pi Dn)^2 + V^2}/a\) must stay below about 0.85 to 0.9: it caps the diameter and the flight speed.
  • Clearances: 7 in to the ground, 1 in to the structure at least (25.925); no seats in the propeller's plane.
