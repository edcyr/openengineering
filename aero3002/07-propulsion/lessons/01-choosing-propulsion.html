Choosing the Propulsion

Module 5 asked the business jet's engines for 10.6 kN each at takeoff, and Module 6 hung two placeholder nacelles on its tail cone. Before choosing a real engine, this lesson asks what kind of engine it should be. Every engine makes thrust the same way, by throwing air backward, and the best way to throw it depends on how fast the aircraft flies.

Learning objectives

  • Explain thrust as the momentum an engine adds to a stream of air.
  • Use the propulsive efficiency to explain why engines move more air more slowly.
  • Relate an engine's fuel consumption to its thermal and propulsive efficiencies.
  • Describe the piston-propeller, turboprop, turbofan and turbojet, and the speeds each suits.

Thrust from momentum

An engine takes in air at the flight speed \(V\) and sends it out behind at a higher jet velocity \(V_j\). The force needed to speed up the stream is the thrust. For a mass flow \(\dot m\), a nozzle that expands the jet to the outside pressure, and the fuel's own small mass neglected:

Net thrust

\[ T = \dot m\,(V_j - V) \]

\(\dot m V_j\) is the gross thrust of the jet; \(\dot m V\) is the ram drag of the air taken in.

The same thrust can come from a small mass flow thrown back fast or a large one thrown back slowly. That choice is the whole difference between a turbojet and a propeller, and it decides how much fuel the engine burns.

Propulsive efficiency

The useful power is the thrust times the flight speed, \(TV\). The engine has to supply more than that: it also leaves kinetic energy behind in the jet, which moves at \(V_j - V\) relative to the still air. The share that becomes useful work is the propulsive efficiency:

Propulsive efficiency

\[ \eta_p = \frac{TV}{\tfrac12\dot m\left(V_j^2 - V^2\right)} = \frac{2}{1 + V_j/V} \]

It approaches 1 as the jet velocity approaches the flight speed, but then the thrust \(\dot m(V_j - V)\) falls to nothing unless the mass flow is huge. Efficient engines therefore move a lot of air and speed it up a little: a propeller moves the most, a high-bypass turbofan a great deal, a turbojet the least.

Example 1.1 — Two ways to push the business jet

In cruise at Mach 0.80 and 12 km (\(V = 236.1\ \text{m/s}\)), each of the business jet's two engines must give \(2229\ \text{N}\): the drag at \(L/D = 14.6\) of a jet at \(0.9555\,\Wo\), shared between them. Compare an engine with \(V_j = 600\ \text{m/s}\), like a turbojet, with one at \(V_j = 350\ \text{m/s}\), like a small turbofan.

Show solution
\[ \dot m = \frac{T}{V_j - V}: \qquad \frac{2229}{600 - 236.1} = 6.13\ \text{kg/s}, \qquad \frac{2229}{350 - 236.1} = 19.6\ \text{kg/s} \] \[ \eta_p = \frac{2}{1 + 600/236.1} = 0.565, \qquad \eta_p = \frac{2}{1 + 350/236.1} = 0.806 \]

The turbofan moves three times as much air and wastes far less energy in its jet: for the same thrust it needs 30% less power from its core. The price is a bigger fan, nacelle and inlet.

Thermal efficiency and fuel consumption

Two efficiencies stand between the fuel and the thrust. The thermal efficiency \(\eta_{th}\) is the share of the fuel's heat that becomes kinetic energy in the jet; the propulsive efficiency is the share of that energy that does useful work. Their product is the overall efficiency, \(\eta_0 = \eta_{th}\eta_p\). Burning fuel at a rate \(\dot m_f\) with heating value \(h\) releases \(\dot m_f h\), of which \(TV\) is useful work, so

Fuel consumption and overall efficiency

\[ \eta_0 = \frac{TV}{\dot m_f\,h} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_T = \frac{\dot m_f}{T} = \frac{V}{\eta_0\,h} \]

\(c_T\) is the thrust-specific fuel consumption of Module 3; jet fuel has \(h \approx 43\ \text{MJ/kg}\).

An engine that is more efficient overall burns less fuel per newton of thrust at a given speed. The formula also shows why a jet's \(c_T\) rises with speed even when its efficiency does not: thrust at a higher speed delivers more power.

Example 1.2 — The business jet's engines, from Module 3

Module 3 gave the business jet a cruise fuel consumption of \(C = 0.65\ \text{h}^{-1}\), which is \(c_T = 0.65(28.33) = 18.4\ \text{mg/(N s)}\). What overall efficiency does that imply at Mach 0.80 and 12 km?

Show solution
\[ \eta_0 = \frac{V}{c_T\,h} = \frac{236.1}{18.4 \times 10^{-6}\,(43 \times 10^6)} = 0.298 \]

About 30% of the fuel's energy pushes the aircraft. With a propulsive efficiency near 0.8, the thermal efficiency is about 0.37: a small engine's core cannot reach the pressures and temperatures of an airliner's.

Four kinds of engine

  • A piston engine turns a propeller. It is cheap and economical at low power and low speed, but heavy for its power and loses power with altitude unless it is supercharged.
  • A turboprop is a gas turbine that drives a propeller through a gearbox. It is light for its power, and very efficient up to about 600 to 700 km/h; above that the propeller tips approach the speed of sound.
  • A turbofan sends part of its air through the core and the rest, the bypass flow, around it, pushed only by the fan. The bypass ratio is the bypass mass flow divided by the core mass flow. A higher bypass ratio means a larger, slower jet and a better propulsive efficiency, but a bigger, heavier fan and nacelle. Business jets use bypass ratios of about 2 to 5, airliners 5 to 12.
  • A turbojet sends all its air through the core, so its jet is small and fast. It is the lightest engine for its thrust and the best above Mach 2, but wasteful at subsonic speeds. Supersonic combat aircraft use low-bypass turbofans with afterburners, a compromise between the two.

The business jet's engines

The business jet cruises at Mach 0.80, about 850 km/h at 12 km: too fast for a propeller and too slow for a turbojet to be efficient. A turbofan is the answer, with a moderate bypass ratio that keeps the fan small enough to hang on the tail cone. It needs two of them, so that one can fail on takeoff and the other still climbs (Module 5), and the requirement is 10.6 kN each at sea level. Lesson 3 finds a real engine of that class and scales it to fit.

Why not one engine?

Single-engine jets exist, but transport rules for passenger service with more than a few seats, and most operators, want an engine failure on takeoff to be survivable without a forced landing. That is the engine-out climb of Module 5: \(\TW \ge [N/(N-1)](1/(L/D) + G)\), which a single engine cannot meet.

Key takeaways

  • Thrust is the momentum added to the air: \(T = \dot m(V_j - V)\).
  • Propulsive efficiency \(\eta_p = 2/(1 + V_j/V)\): more air, thrown back more slowly, wastes less energy.
  • Fuel consumption follows the overall efficiency: \(c_T = V/(\eta_0 h)\), with \(\eta_0 = \eta_{th}\eta_p\).
  • Propellers suit low speeds, turbofans high subsonic speeds, low-bypass engines supersonic flight; the business jet takes two turbofans.
