The Fuel System

Module 6 checked the business jet's fuel against a statistical estimate of its wing's tank volume and found room to spare. Now the wing has a layout, with spars where the structure will put them, and the tank can be measured instead of estimated. This lesson compares fuels, measures the wing box, and lays out the system that keeps the engines fed.

Learning objectives

  • Compare aviation fuels by density and energy, and the volume each needs for a mission.
  • Describe the kinds of tanks and where they go.
  • Estimate a wing box's tank volume from its geometry, and allow for unusable fuel and expansion.
  • Describe the feed system, and how burning fuel moves the center of gravity.

Fuels

Turbine engines burn kerosene: Jet A in North America and Jet A-1, with a lower freezing point, elsewhere; piston engines burn aviation gasoline. Sustainable aviation fuels, made from waste oils, crops or captured carbon, are blended into kerosene and burned in the same engines. Hydrogen holds almost three times the energy of kerosene per kilogram, but even as a liquid at −253 °C it is about eleven times less dense.

Example 7.1 — The business jet on hydrogen

The business jet carries 1991 kg of Jet A-1 (43.2 MJ/kg, 800 kg/m³). What mass and volume of liquid hydrogen (120 MJ/kg, 70.8 kg/m³) hold the same energy?

Show solution
\[ E = 1991(43.2) = 86\,000\ \text{MJ} \] \[ m_{H_2} = \frac{86\,000}{120} = 717\ \text{kg} \] \[ V_{H_2} = \frac{717}{70.8} = 10.1\ \text{m}^3 \]

A third of the mass, but four times the volume of the kerosene's 2.49 m³, and in tanks that must stay at −253 °C: thick, round, insulated tanks that cannot go in a thin wing. A hydrogen business jet would carry its fuel in the fuselage, and grow longer to do it.

Where the fuel goes

  • Integral wing tanks use the sealed wing box itself, between the front and rear spars, as the tank. They are light, keep the fuel near the center of gravity, and relieve the wing's bending in flight.
  • A center tank uses the wing box where it crosses the fuselage. On a low wing it sits under the cabin floor.
  • Fuselage and tail tanks add range, and some airliners pump fuel to a tail tank in cruise to move the center of gravity aft and reduce the trim drag.
  • Bladder tanks, flexible bags inside a cavity, suit structures that cannot be sealed, and military aircraft that need self-sealing tanks.

Not all the volume is usable. The tanks stop short of the wing tips, which hold the vent and surge tanks and need protection from lightning; the landing gear bays and the moving surfaces take space; some fuel is trapped at the bottom of every tank, and some space is left for the fuel to expand on a hot day. Allow 3% of the tank volume for the unusable fuel and the expansion space together.

Module 6's tank formula is a statistical fit to transport wings. With a layout, the wing box can be measured instead: between the spars, whose positions the structure sets, with a depth a little less than the airfoil's maximum thickness:

Geometric wing-box volume

\[ V_{\text{box}} = 2\int_{d/2}^{\eta\,b/2} \left(x_{rs} - x_{fs}\right) c(y)\cdot k\,t(y)\,dy \]

Spars at chord fractions \(x_{fs}\) and \(x_{rs}\); box depth \(k \approx 0.9\) of the local thickness \(t(y) = (t/c)\,c(y)\); from the fuselage side to a share \(\eta\) of the half span; plus the center section across the fuselage if it is a tank.

Example 7.2 — Does the business jet's fuel fit, measured?

Put the business jet's spars at 15% and 60% of the chord, run the tanks to 85% of the half span, and take \(k = 0.9\). The integral gives 2.05 m³ for the two wings, and the center section across the 1.597 m fuselage at the root chord of 2.772 m adds \(1.597(0.45)(2.772)(0.9)(0.12)(2.772) = 0.60\ \text{m}^3\). Does the mission's 2.49 m³ fit?

Show solution

The wings alone hold 2.05 m³: not enough. With the center tank:

\[ V = 2.05 + 0.60 = 2.65\ \text{m}^3 \] \[ V_{\text{usable}} = 0.97(2.65) = 2.57\ \text{m}^3 \] \[ \frac{2.49}{2.57} = 0.97 \]

The fuel fits, with 3% to spare, but only with the center tank. The statistical formula's 2.74 m³ overstates this wing's outboard tanks by a third. A light business jet's thin wing is tight; the CJ3 carries 2.66 m³ of usable fuel [10] in a wing of similar size.

The fuel system

Each engine normally draws from its own tank through boost pumps, with a crossfeed valve that lets either tank feed either engine, to balance the wings or to feed both engines from one side after a failure. Vents keep the tanks near outside pressure as the aircraft climbs and descends; the fuel cools the engine oil on the way to the engine; and single-point pressure refueling fills every tank from one connector in a few minutes. Transports whose maximum landing weight is far below their takeoff weight may need a fuel jettison system, so that they can return and land soon after takeoff; light jets, whose landing weight is close to their takeoff weight, do not.

Burning fuel moves the center of gravity. Fuel in the wing box sits close to it, so the balance changes little through the flight, which is a further reason to keep the fuel there. A fuselage or tail tank moves the center of gravity as it empties, and the order in which the tanks are used is then part of the design. Key takeaways

  • Kerosene: about 800 kg/m³ and 43 MJ/kg; liquid hydrogen has nearly three times the energy per kilogram but needs four times the volume.
  • Fuel goes in the sealed wing box first: light, near the center of gravity, and relieving the wing's bending.
  • Measured between the spars, the business jet's wings hold 2.05 m³; with the center tank, 2.57 m³ usable, enough for its 2.49 m³.
  • Allow for unusable fuel and expansion (take 3%); feed each engine from its own tank, with crossfeed.
