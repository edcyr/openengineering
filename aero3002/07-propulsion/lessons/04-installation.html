Nacelles and Installation

An engine on a test stand is a bare machine. On the aircraft it needs an inlet in front, a nozzle behind, a smooth cowling around it, a pylon to carry it, and room around it for maintenance, fire protection and the debris of a failure. This lesson wraps the business jet's rubber engines in nacelles, replaces Module 6's placeholders, and looks at what an engine failure does to the aircraft's balance.

Learning objectives

  • Estimate a nacelle's size from the engine's, and its wetted area.
  • Describe what the pylon, the cowling and the installation must provide.
  • Explain the rotor-burst zones and how they constrain the engine's position.
  • Compute the yawing moment of a failed engine for rear-mounted and wing-mounted engines.

From engine to nacelle

The nacelle is everything around the engine: the inlet lip and diffuser ahead of the fan, the cowling around the casing and accessories, and the exhaust nozzle. Its size follows the engine's. For a first layout this module takes a nacelle about 10% larger in diameter than the engine's overall height and 20% longer than the engine, enough for the inlet, the cowl and the nozzle, and checks the result against similar aircraft:

A first nacelle, and its wetted area

\[ D_n \approx 1.1\,H_{\text{eng}}, \qquad l_n \approx 1.2\,L_{\text{eng}}, \qquad S_{\text{wet},n} \approx \pi D_n l_n \]

The wetted area is that of a cylinder of the nacelle's diameter and length, as in Module 6; the curved inlet and nozzle make the real one slightly smaller.

Example 4.1 — The business jet's nacelles

The rubber engines of Lesson 3 are 1.475 m long and 0.725 m high. Size the nacelles and their wetted area, and compare them with Module 6's placeholders of 0.8 m by 2.4 m. What does the change do to the wetted area of the whole aircraft (146.5 m²) and to \(\LDmax\) (\(A = 8\), \(e = 0.75\), \(C_{fe} = 0.0030\), \(S = 28\ \text{m}^2\))?

Show solution
\[ D_n = 1.1(0.725) = 0.797\ \text{m}, \qquad l_n = 1.2(1.475) = 1.770\ \text{m} \]

For the two nacelles together:

\[ 2\pi(0.797)(1.770) = 8.87\ \text{m}^2, \qquad \text{against } 2\pi(0.8)(2.4) = 12.06\ \text{m}^2 \] \[ \Swet = 146.5 - 12.06 + 8.87 = 143.3\ \text{m}^2 \] \[ \frac{\Swet}{\Sref} = 5.12 \] \[ \LDmax = \frac12\sqrt{\frac{\pi(8)(0.75)}{0.0030(5.12)}} = 17.5 \]

The diameter of the placeholder was about right; its length was too generous. The real engines are short, and the aircraft gains 1% in \(\LDmax\).

The installation

  • The pylon carries the engine's weight, thrust and gyroscopic loads into the structure, through mounts that let the hot casing expand. It also carries the fuel, electrical, bleed-air and control lines, and it must hold the engine through the loads of a fan blade coming off.
  • The cowling opens wide for maintenance: mechanics must reach the accessories, change the engine in a few hours, and inspect the fan and the hot section. Engines on the tail cone are high off the ground, which keeps out runway debris but needs platforms to reach them.
  • The nacelle is a fire zone, with fire detectors, extinguishers and fire walls between the engine and the pylon.
  • The engine needs clearances: from the fuselage, so the flow between them does not choke and the noise stays outside the cabin; from the ground for a wing-mounted engine; and from the wing's wake at high angles of attack for a rear-mounted one (Lesson 5).

Rotor burst

A fan, compressor or turbine disc spins with enormous energy, and a disc that breaks sends fragments out at high speed, mostly within a narrow band of angles around the plane of the rotor. The casing contains a lost blade but cannot contain a disc. The certification rules therefore ask the designer to place the engines so that fragments in these zones do not hit the passengers, the fuel tanks, both sets of flight controls or both engines.

For the business jet this is one more reason to put the engines behind the cabin's rear pressure bulkhead, at FS 10.7 m and aft, where the planes of the rotors cross only the tail cone. On aircraft with engines on the wing, the rotor planes cross the fuselage, and designers keep seats, tanks and critical lines out of the narrow band, or protect them.

An engine failure and the yawing moment

When one engine fails, the other pushes off the centerline, and the drag of the failed engine adds to the turn. The yawing moment is about the thrust times its lateral offset, \(N \approx T_{\text{eng}}\,y_e\). The rudder must balance it at the lowest speed at which the aircraft can be controlled, the minimum control speed, which sets the size of the fin and rudder (Module 11).

Example 4.2 — Rear engines against wing engines

The business jet's engines give 10.56 kN each, with their thrust lines 1.26 m from the centerline. Compare the yawing moment with that of engines under the wing at 34% of the half span (\(b = 14.97\ \text{m}\)).

Show solution
\[ N = 10.56(1.26) = 13.3\ \text{kN m} \] \[ y_e = 0.34\,\frac{14.97}{2} = 2.54\ \text{m} \] \[ N = 10.56(2.54) = 26.9\ \text{kN m} \]

Twice the moment for engines on the wing: the fin and rudder would have to be larger, or the minimum control speed higher. This is one of the reasons most business jets carry their engines on the tail cone.

Key takeaways

  • A first nacelle is about 10% larger in diameter and 20% longer than the engine; its wetted area is about \(\pi D_n l_n\).
  • The business jet's nacelles: 0.80 m by 1.77 m, 8.87 m² for the pair; the aircraft's \(\Swet/\Sref\) falls to 5.12 and \(\LDmax\) rises to 17.5.
  • Pylons, cowlings, fire zones and clearances all take space; rotor-burst zones must miss the cabin, tanks and critical systems.
  • A failed engine yaws the aircraft with \(N \approx T_{\text{eng}}\,y_e\): rear engines halve the moment of wing engines.
