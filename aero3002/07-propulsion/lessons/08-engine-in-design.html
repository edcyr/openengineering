Lesson 8 · 30 min

The Engine in the Design

Module 5 chose the thrust, Module 6 left a placeholder where the engines would go, and this module has sized them. This lesson takes stock of what changed, checks the thrust where it is scarcest, at the ceiling, and runs the sizing loop once more.

Learning objectives

  • List what the sized engines changed in the business jet's weight, drag and fuel volume.
  • Check the takeoff thrust at the ceiling with two models of the thrust lapse, and explain the choices when they disagree.
  • Run the sizing of Module 3 again with a new lift-to-drag ratio and an added weight.
  • Explain why a design settles over several turns of the loop.

Most of the changes are small, and most are good news: the sized nacelles are shorter than the placeholder, so the drag fell. One question is not small: whether the thrust Module 5 chose is there at the ceiling.

The thrust at the ceiling

Module 5 set the business jet's thrust by its cruise ceiling: a climb of 300 ft/min at Mach 0.80 and 12 km, at 95.55% of the takeoff weight, with the thrust lapse \(\alpha = \sigma\). Lesson 2 showed that a turbofan with a high bypass ratio loses thrust faster than that. With the layout's drag, the question can be asked again.

Example 8.1 — The ceiling with the layout's drag

With \(\CDz = 0.003(5.119) = 0.01536\), \(\WSo = 250\ \text{kg/m}^2\), \(\beta = 0.9555\), \(A = 8\) and \(e = 0.75\), find the thrust the ceiling needs, as a share of the takeoff weight, and the takeoff \(\TW\) with \(\alpha = \sigma = 0.2537\) and with the high-bypass correlation's \(\alpha = 0.1633\).

Show solution
\[ q = 0.7(19\,330)(0.80)^2 = 8660\ \text{Pa} \] \[ \beta g\,\WSo = 0.9555(9.81)(250) = 2343\ \text{N/m}^2 \] \[ \frac{q\CDz}{\beta g\,\WSo} = \frac{8660(0.01536)}{2343} = 0.0567 \] \[ \frac{\beta g\,\WSo}{q\pi Ae} = \frac{2343}{8660\,\pi(8)(0.75)} = 0.0144 \] \[ \frac{1}{V}\frac{\dd h}{\dd t} = \frac{1.524}{236.1} = 0.0065 \] \[ \frac{T}{\Wo g} = 0.9555(0.0567 + 0.0144 + 0.0065) = 0.0741 \] \[ \alpha = \sigma\!: \ \TWo = \frac{0.0741}{0.2537} = 0.292 \] \[ \text{high bypass: } \TWo = \frac{0.0741}{0.1633} = 0.454 \]

The layout's lower drag lowers both, from 0.308 and 0.478, but the gap stays. The business jet's 0.31 is enough if its engines lapse like \(\sigma\), and far too little if they lapse like an airliner's turbofan: with the correlation, 0.31 cannot hold Mach 0.80 with 300 ft/min to spare at any altitude.

A designer with two answers this far apart has three ways forward:

  • Ask for the engine deck. The engine maker's data at altitude settles which model is nearer. The business jet's engines have a moderate bypass ratio, so the truth lies between the two; the Citation CJ3's \(\TW\) of 0.406 suggests where.
  • Fly slower or lower. With the correlation, the 0.31 engines reach a cruise ceiling of 6.9 km at Mach 0.70 and 9.4 km at Mach 0.60. The customer decides whether that is still the aircraft they wanted.
  • Install more thrust. Sizing to the correlation means \(\TW = 0.454\): two engines of 15.45 kN, each a 305 kg rubber FJ44-3A (scale factor 1.232), 209 kg more for the pair than now, in larger nacelles.

One more turn of the loop

Module 3 sized the business jet with \(\LDmax = 16.9\) from a sketch. Since then, each module has replaced an assumption with a number from the design: the wing and its tails, the layout's wetted area, and now the engines and nacelles. Running the sizing again with those numbers closes one turn of the design loop.

Example 8.2 — Sizing again with the new \(\LDmax\)

With \(\LDmax = 17.52\), and otherwise the mission and empty-weight trend of Module 3, find \(\Wf/\Wo\) and \(\Wo\).

Show solution

Cruise at \(0.866(17.52) = 15.17\); loiter at 17.52.

\[ \frac{W_3}{W_2} = \exp\left[-\frac{4600(0.65)}{850(15.17)}\right] = 0.7931, \qquad \frac{W_4}{W_3} = \exp\left[-\frac{0.75(0.55)}{17.52}\right] = 0.9767 \] \[ \frac{W_x}{\Wo} = 0.970(0.985)(0.7931)(0.9767)(0.995) = 0.7364 \] \[ \frac{\Wf}{\Wo} = 1.06(1 - 0.7364) = 0.2794 \] \[ \Wo = \frac{980}{1 - 0.2794 - 0.9727\,\Wo^{-0.06}} = 6665\ \text{kg} \]

4% lighter than 6943 kg. Its 1862 kg of fuel, 2.33 m³, fits easily in the 2.57 m³ of Lesson 7. At the same wing loading and \(\TW\), the lighter aircraft needs a smaller wing (26.7 m²) and smaller engines (10.13 kN), with smaller nacelles again: each turn changes less, and the loop settles.

Example 8.3 — What the extra thrust would cost

Suppose the engine deck shows the high-bypass lapse, and the business jet installs \(\TW = 0.454\), adding 209 kg to the engines. Treating that as an addition to the empty weight, with \(\LDmax = 17.52\), what is \(\Wo\)?

Show solution
\[ \Wo = \frac{980 + 209}{1 - 0.2794 - 0.9727\,\Wo^{-0.06}} = 7801\ \text{kg} \]

1136 kg more than 6665 kg: each kilogram added to the engines adds 5.4 kg to the takeoff weight, the true growth factor of Module 3. And the heavier aircraft needs larger engines still; followed around the loop, the takeoff weight settles near 7990 kg, 20% more. The thrust lapse is worth a call to the engine maker.

What the course carries forward

The business jet keeps its first estimate (6943 kg, 28 m², two engines of 10.56 kN) as the baseline for the next modules, so that their numbers can be compared. Module 11 replaces the empty-weight trend with a weight for each component, the engines' 402 kg among them, and the loop is then run with better numbers. The thrust at the ceiling stays an open question until the performance analysis, with an engine deck, answers it.

Key takeaways

  • The sized nacelles cut the business jet's wetted area to \(\Swet/\Sref = 5.119\) and raised \(\LDmax\) to 17.52; its engines weigh 402 kg.
  • The ceiling needs 0.0741 of \(\Wo\) in thrust at 12 km: a takeoff \(\TW\) of 0.292 with \(\alpha = \sigma\), but 0.454 with the high-bypass correlation.
  • When the models disagree: get the engine deck, fly slower or lower, or install more thrust.
  • Sized again with \(\LDmax = 17.52\), the business jet weighs 6665 kg; added empty weight costs about 5.5 times its own weight in \(\Wo\).
