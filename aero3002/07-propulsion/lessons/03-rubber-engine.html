Engine Data and the Rubber Engine

A new aircraft rarely gets a new engine. It gets an existing engine, or a derivative of one, and in conceptual design that engine is treated as rubber: stretched or shrunk to give exactly the thrust the design point asks for. This lesson starts from certified engine data, shows how weight and size grow with thrust, and sizes the business jet's engines.

Learning objectives

  • Read an engine's certified thrust or power, dry weight and dimensions from its type-certificate data sheet.
  • Describe how engine weight, length and diameter grow with thrust.
  • Scale a base engine to a required thrust with the rubber-engine exponents.
  • Size the business jet's engines and check the method against a real engine family.

Engine data

Every certified engine has a type-certificate data sheet, published by the certifying authority. It lists each model's takeoff thrust (or shaft power for a turboprop) and the ambient temperature it is held to, its dry weight, its overall dimensions, and its operating limits. Table 3.1 gives values from the sheets of the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA) for a family of business-jet engines and for three engines of other sizes.

The dry weight is the bare engine with its accessories: no nacelle, no inlet, no exhaust, no fluids. The length is measured flange to flange or overall, as the sheet says, and the height includes accessories hung under the casing, so it is a little more than the fan diameter. Turboprops are rated by shaft power, in kW; the PW127M in the table drives the propellers of regional turboprops.

How engines grow with thrust

Plotted against thrust on logarithmic scales, the dry weights of these turbofans lie close to a straight line, a power law:

Fitted to the turbofans in Table 3.1

\[ W_{\text{eng}} \approx 20.17\,T^{1.005}, \qquad L \approx 0.71\,T^{0.30}, \qquad H \approx 0.30\,T^{0.39} \]

\(W_{\text{eng}}\) in kg, \(L\) and \(H\) in m, \(T\) the take-off thrust in kN; one point per engine design.

Across a hundredfold range of thrust the weight grows about in proportion to the thrust: these engines all give about 50 newtons of thrust per kilogram, or a thrust-to-weight ratio near 5. The length grows much more slowly: a big engine is mostly wider, because the thrust follows the mass flow, which follows the frontal area. Within one family of engines the picture differs: later FJ44 models gained thrust with little extra weight, as better parts replaced old ones.

The rubber engine

The statistics describe engines in general. The rubber engine starts from one real engine of the right kind and scales it, keeping its cycle: the same pressure ratio, bypass ratio and turbine temperature, so the same fuel consumption. The scale factor is the thrust ratio, and the dimensions and weight follow from it, with the exponents of Chapter 10 of [1]:

Rubber-engine scaling

\[ SF = \frac{T_{\text{req}}}{T_{\text{base}}}, \qquad L = L_{\text{base}}\,SF^{0.4}, \qquad D = D_{\text{base}}\,SF^{0.5}, \qquad W = W_{\text{base}}\,SF^{1.1} \]

The diameter follows the square root, so the frontal area, and the mass flow, scale with the thrust.

The exponents are empirical, and they work best for small changes: a scale factor between about 0.8 and 1.25. Beyond that a new engine design would choose a different cycle, and a different base engine is a better start.

Example 3.1 — The business jet's engines

The business jet needs \(T = 0.31(6943)(9.81)/2 = 10.56\ \text{kN}\) per engine. Scale the FJ44-3A (12.54 kN, 242.7 kg, 1.58 m long, 0.79 m high [5]), the engine of the Citation CJ3 Gen2 [10].

Show solution
\[ SF = \frac{10.56}{12.54} = 0.842 \] \[ L = 1.58(0.842)^{0.4} = 1.475\ \text{m}, \qquad H = 0.79(0.842)^{0.5} = 0.725\ \text{m} \] \[ W = 242.7(0.842)^{1.1} = 200.8\ \text{kg} \]

Two engines weigh 402 kg, 5.8% of the takeoff weight. The fitted statistic gives \(20.17(10.56)^{1.005} = 215.5\ \text{kg}\) each, 7% more: the FJ44-3A is lighter than the average engine of its thrust.

A check against a real family

The data sheet itself offers a test of the exponents. It says the FJ44-4A "is similar to the FJ44-3A, geometrically larger by a factor of 1.1" [5], and it gives 16.10 kN against 12.54 kN.

Example 3.2 — From the FJ44-3A to the FJ44-4A

Scale the FJ44-3A to the FJ44-4A's thrust and compare the result with the larger engine's data: 1.74 m long, 0.82 m high and 304.0 kg.

Show solution
\[ SF = \frac{16.10}{12.54} = 1.284 \] \[ L = 1.58(1.284)^{0.4} = 1.746\ \text{m}, \qquad W = 242.7(1.284)^{1.1} = 319\ \text{kg} \]

The length is right: \(1.284^{0.4} = 1.105\), the sheet's factor of 1.1. The weight comes out 5% high, which is on the safe side for a first estimate. The height grows less than \(SF^{0.5}\) predicts, because it includes accessories that do not scale with the fan.

Key takeaways

  • Type-certificate data sheets give each engine's certified thrust or power, dry weight and dimensions.
  • Turbofan weight grows about in proportion to thrust (a thrust-to-weight ratio near 5); the length grows much more slowly.
  • Rubber engine: \(SF = T_{\text{req}}/T_{\text{base}}\); \(L \propto SF^{0.4}\), \(D \propto SF^{0.5}\), \(W \propto SF^{1.1}\); keep \(SF\) near 1.
  • The business jet's engines: 10.56 kN, 1.475 m long, 0.725 m high, 200.8 kg each, scaled from the FJ44-3A.
