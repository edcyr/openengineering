Contributions to the Pitching Moment

Every part of the aircraft pushes on the pitching moment about the CG. The wing lifts ahead of or behind the CG and twists nose-down on its own; the fuselage tends to turn into any change of angle; the tail, behind everything, works against them. This lesson estimates each part for the business jet, with Module 11's layout.

Learning objectives

  • Write the pitching moment about the CG as the sum of the wing, fuselage, tail and engine contributions.
  • Estimate the wing's lift-curve slope, its moment about its aerodynamic center, and the fuselage's destabilizing moment.
  • Estimate the tail's effectiveness: its lift-curve slope, the dynamic-pressure ratio and the wing's downwash.

The moment about the CG

Measure positions along the mean aerodynamic chord as fractions of it, and write \(\bar x_{cg-ac}\) for the CG's distance behind the wing's aerodynamic center, about its quarter-chord. Taking moments about the CG, with the tail's lift \(C_{L,H}\) on its own area and \(l_H\) the distance from the wing's aerodynamic center to the tail's:

Pitching moment about the CG

\[ \begin{aligned} C_{M,cg} = C_{M,W} + C_{L,W}\,\bar x_{cg-ac} + C_{M,\text{fus}} + C_{M,E} \\ - \eta_H\frac{S_H}{S}\,C_{L,H}\left(\frac{l_H}{\cbar} - \bar x_{cg-ac}\right) \end{aligned} \] \[ \begin{aligned} C_{M\alpha} = C_{L\alpha,W}\,\bar x_{cg-ac} + C_{M\alpha,\text{fus}} \\ - \eta_H\frac{S_H}{S}\,C_{L\alpha,H}\left(1 - \frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial\alpha}\right)\left(\frac{l_H}{\cbar} - \bar x_{cg-ac}\right) \end{aligned} \]

\(C_{M,W}\), the wing's moment about its aerodynamic center; \(C_{M,E}\), the engines' moment; \(\eta_H = q_H/q\), the tail's dynamic-pressure ratio; \(\varepsilon\), the downwash angle at the tail. Source: Scholz, Aircraft Design lecture notes, Ch. 11, with the fuselage term of Raymer's method.

The wing's term is stabilizing when the CG is ahead of its aerodynamic center, the tail's always is, and the fuselage's always destabilizes.

The wing

The lift-curve slope is Module 4's, with the sweep of the half-chord line and an airfoil efficiency of 0.95:

\[ C_{L\alpha} = \frac{2\pi A}{2 + \sqrt{4 + \dfrac{A^2\beta^2}{0.95^2}\left(1 + \dfrac{\tan^2\Lambda_{c/2}}{\beta^2}\right)}}, \qquad \beta^2 = 1 - M^2 \]

For wings of aspect ratio 5 or more and moderate sweep the aerodynamic center lies close to the quarter-chord of the mean aerodynamic chord. Its moment about that point comes from the airfoil's, reduced by aspect ratio and sweep:

\[ C_{M,W} = c_{m,0}\,\frac{A\cos^2\Lambda_{c/4}}{A + 2\cos\Lambda_{c/4}} \]

The business jet's supercritical airfoil carries its lift well aft, so it pitches nose-down; this module assumes \(c_{m,0} = -0.10\), at the top of the range for cambered airfoils. Then \(C_{M,W} = -0.10(0.670) = -0.067\), and \(C_{L\alpha,W} = 4.507\) per radian at Mach 0.2 (\(\Lambda_{c/2} = 22.1^\circ\)) and 5.969 at Mach 0.80.

The fuselage

A fuselage at an angle of attack makes little lift but a strong nose-up moment, like any slender body; the wing's flow field around it adds to that ahead of the wing and reduces it behind. An empirical factor captures both:

Fuselage pitching-moment slope

\[ C_{M\alpha,\text{fus}} = \frac{K_f\,W_f^2\,L_f}{\cbar\,S}\ \text{per degree}, \qquad K_f = 0.01 - 0.063\,x_0 + 0.211\,x_0^2 \]

\(W_f\) and \(L_f\), the fuselage's maximum width and length; \(x_0\), the position of the wing root's quarter-chord from the nose as a fraction of \(L_f\), from 0.1 to 0.62. The fit of \(K_f\) is FAST-GA's, of Raymer's chart.

Example 2.1 — The business jet's fuselage

With Module 11's wing, the root's quarter-chord is at 39.10% of the 15.5 m fuselage, which is 1.597 m wide. With \(\cbar = 2.015\) m and \(S = 28\) m², find \(C_{M\alpha,\text{fus}}\) per radian.

Show solution
\[ K_f = 0.01 - 0.063(0.3910) + 0.211(0.3910)^2 = 0.01762 \] \[ C_{M\alpha,\text{fus}} = \frac{0.01762(1.597)^2(15.5)}{2.015(28)} = 0.012346\ \text{per degree} = 0.707\ \text{per rad} \]

Positive: destabilizing. Moving the wing aft would raise \(x_0\) and \(K_f\), and destabilize the fuselage further.

The tail and the downwash

The horizontal tail works in air the wing has already turned and slowed. The wing's drag wake lowers the tail's dynamic pressure: \(\eta_H\) lies between 0.85 and 0.95, typically 0.9. The wing's lift deflects the flow downward by the downwash angle \(\varepsilon\), which grows with the angle of attack, so the tail's angle grows by only \(1 - \partial\varepsilon/\partial\alpha\) for each degree the aircraft's does. The downwash gradient depends on the wing and on where the tail sits behind it:

Downwash gradient at the horizontal tail (DATCOM)

\[ \frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial\alpha} = 4.44\left[k_A\,k_\lambda\,k_H\sqrt{\cos\Lambda_{c/4}}\right]^{1.19}\frac{C_{L\alpha}(M)}{C_{L\alpha}(0)} \] \[ k_A = \frac1A - \frac{1}{1 + A^{1.7}}, \qquad k_\lambda = \frac{10 - 3\lambda}{7}, \qquad k_H = \frac{1 - |z_H/b|}{\sqrt[3]{2l_H/b}} \]

\(z_H\): the tail's height above the plane of the wing's root chord; \(l_H\): its distance behind the wing's aerodynamic center. Source: Scholz, after DATCOM.

Example 2.2 — The business jet's downwash

The business jet's wing: \(A = 8\), \(\lambda = 0.35\), \(\Lambda_{c/4} = 25^\circ\), \(b = 14.97\) m. Its horizontal tail sits 1.139 m above the wing's root chord and 6.734 m behind its aerodynamic center. Find \(\partial\varepsilon/\partial\alpha\) at low speed and at Mach 0.80.

Show solution
\[ k_A = \frac18 - \frac{1}{1 + 8^{1.7}} = 0.09667 \] \[ k_\lambda = \frac{10 - 1.05}{7} = 1.2786 \] \[ k_H = \frac{1 - 0.0761}{\sqrt[3]{0.8999}} = 0.9569 \] \[ \frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial\alpha} = 4.44\left[0.09667(1.2786)(0.9569)(0.9520)\right]^{1.19} = 4.44(0.11260)^{1.19} = 0.330 \]

That is the incompressible value, for the wing's slope at Mach 0, 4.446 per rad. The downwash grows with the wing's slope: at Mach 0.2, the low-speed case from Lesson 3 on, the slope is 4.507 and the gradient \(0.330(4.507/4.446) = 0.335\); at Mach 0.80 the slope grows by \(5.969/4.446 = 1.343\), and the gradient to 0.443. The tail loses a third of its angle at low speed and 44% in cruise.

The tail's own lift-curve slope comes from the same formula as the wing's: \(C_{L\alpha,H} = 3.583\) per radian at low speed for its aspect ratio of 4 and \(30^\circ\) of sweep. A tail placed high, out of the wing's wake and downwash, works better: that is part of the case for a T-tail.

The engines

An engine's thrust line passing above or below the CG adds a moment, \(C_{M,E} = -T z_E/(q S\cbar)\), with \(z_E\) the height of the thrust line above the CG. The business jet's rear engines are 0.459 m above its CG, so full thrust pitches it nose-down: that matters at low speed, where the thrust is large and \(q\) small (Lesson 5). The engines' other effects, through their inlet forces and the flow they induce, are what cost a jet 1% to 3% of static margin.

Key takeaways

  • \(C_{M\alpha}\) adds the wing (\(C_{L\alpha,W}\bar x_{cg-ac}\)), the fuselage (destabilizing) and the tail (\(\eta_H(S_H/S)C_{L\alpha,H}(1 - \partial\varepsilon/\partial\alpha)\) times its arm).
  • The business jet: \(C_{L\alpha,W} = 4.507\) per radian, \(C_{M,W} = -0.067\), \(C_{M\alpha,\text{fus}} = +0.707\) per radian.
  • Downwash takes a third of the tail's angle at low speed (\(\partial\varepsilon/\partial\alpha = 0.335\) at Mach 0.2) and 44% in cruise.
  • The rear engines' thrust line above the CG pitches the nose down at full thrust.
