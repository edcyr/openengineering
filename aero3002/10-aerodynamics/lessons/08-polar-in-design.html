The Drag Polar in the Design

The build-up gave the business jet a cruise polar. The design also needs its polars with the flaps and gear down, and it needs to know where on the cruise polar the aircraft actually flies. This lesson assembles the three polars, checks the takeoff climb against Module 5's assumption, and finds what the build-up does to the sizing of Module 3.

Learning objectives

  • Build the takeoff and landing polars from the clean polar with the drag of flaps and landing gear.
  • Check a climb gradient assumption against the takeoff polar.
  • Find the cruise lift coefficient and lift-to-drag ratio at a given altitude, and the altitude of the best range.
  • Run the sizing loop again with the build-up's lift-to-drag ratios, and weigh the options.

Takeoff and landing polars

At takeoff and landing speeds the Mach number is low, so the form factors are smaller (Lesson 4): at sea level and Mach 0.2 the business jet's clean \(C_{D0}\) is 0.0189, against 0.0202 in cruise. To it the configuration adds the flaps' drag, which grows with their deflection, and the landing gear's; the flaps and the fuselage also spoil the span loading, lowering the Oswald factor.

Configuration drag (first estimates)

\[ \Delta C_{D0,\text{flap}} = F\,\frac{c_f}{c}\,\frac{S_{\text{flapped}}}{\Sref}\,(\delta_f - 10^\circ), \qquad \Delta C_{D0,\text{gear}} = \frac{1.2\sum (D/q)_i}{\Sref} \]

\(F\) is 0.0074 for slotted and Fowler flaps and 0.0144 for plain and split flaps; \(c_f/c\) the flap's share of the chord; \(\delta_f\) its deflection in degrees. Each gear item's \(D/q\) is a drag coefficient times its frontal area: about 0.25 for a wheel and tire and 0.30 for a round strut, with 20% for interference. With the flaps down, the Oswald factor drops by about 0.05 at the takeoff setting and 0.10 at the landing setting.

Example 8.1 — The business jet's takeoff climb

The Fowler flaps of Lesson 2 (30% of the chord, \(S_{\text{flapped}}/\Sref = 0.628\)) are set to \(15^\circ\) for takeoff. With the gear up, find the takeoff \(C_{D0}\) and the lift-to-drag ratio at the climb-out speed \(V_2 = 1.2V_s\), where \(C_L = 1.7/1.2^2\). Module 5 assumed \(L/D = 10\) for the one-engine-inoperative climb.

Show solution
\[ \Delta C_{D0,\text{flap}} = 0.0074(0.30)(0.628)(15 - 10) = 0.0070 \] \[ C_{D0} = 0.0189 + 0.0070 = 0.0258 \] \[ K = \frac{1}{\pi(8)(0.811 - 0.05)} = 0.0523, \qquad C_L = \frac{1.7}{1.44} = 1.181 \] \[ \frac LD = \frac{1.181}{0.0258 + 0.0523(1.181)^2} = \frac{1.181}{0.0987} = 12.0 \]

Better than Module 5's 10: its one-engine climb constraint was conservative, before the windmilling drag of the failed engine and the rudder's trim drag, which Module 14 adds.

For landing, the flaps at \(40^\circ\) add 0.0418 and the gear (two H22x8.25-10 wheels, an 18x4.4 nose wheel and their struts, Module 8) about 0.0041, so \(C_{D0} = 0.0648\). At the approach speed \(1.3V_s\), \(C_L = 2.1/1.69 = 1.24\) and \(L/D = 8.2\): a steep, controllable approach, as the landing setting intends.

Where the business jet cruises

Lesson 1 showed that at 12 km and Mach 0.80 the business jet's wing works at \(C_L = 0.253\) at mid-cruise, far below the best-range 0.371 of its polar. On the build-up polar its cruise \(L/D\) is 10.8, not the 14.6 that Module 3 assumed. The reason is the wing loading: Module 5 set it at 250 kg/m² for the landing, and that is light for cruise at 12 km.

Example 8.2 — The altitude for the best range

At what pressure, and so what altitude, does the business jet reach its best-range \(C_L = 0.371\) at Mach 0.80 and 90% of \(\Wo\)?

Show solution
\[ p = \frac{W}{0.7M^2 S\,C_L} = \frac{0.90(6943)(9.81)}{0.7(0.64)(28)(0.371)} = 13\,170\ \text{Pa} \] \[ h = 11\,000 + 6342\ln\frac{22\,632}{13\,170} = 14\,430\ \text{m}\ (47\,300\ \text{ft}) \]

Higher than business jets usually fly. At 13.7 km (FL450), a common ceiling for the class, \(C_L = 0.331\) and \(L/D = 12.9\). Module 7 found that the business jet's engines, under the high-bypass thrust lapse, cannot hold Mach 0.80 that high: the drag and the engines pull the cruise in opposite directions.

Example 8.3 — The range of the aircraft as sized

The 6943 kg business jet carries the fuel Module 3 sized for 4600 km at \(L/D = 0.866(16.9) = 14.6\). How far does that fuel take it at 12 km and Mach 0.80, where \(L/D = 10.8\)?

Show solution

The Breguet range is proportional to \(L/D\) for the same fuel, speed and fuel consumption:

\[ R = 4600\,\frac{10.8}{14.6} = 3390\ \text{km} \]

A quarter short of the requirement.

What the course carries forward

The business jet's aerodynamics after this module: \(C_{D0} = 0.0202\) in cruise and 0.0189 at low speed; \(e = 0.811\), \(K = 0.04909\); \((L/D)_{\max} = 15.87\) at \(C_L = 0.642\); \(\CLmax\) of 1.3, 1.7 and 2.1 with Fowler flaps to 67% of the semispan; \(\MDD = 0.868\) at cruise. The design as laid out cannot fly its 4600 km mission at 12 km and Mach 0.80 on the fuel Module 3 gave it. The options are to cruise higher, which the engines of Module 7 cannot yet support, to cruise slower, to carry more fuel in a heavier aircraft, or to revisit the wing loading. The 6943 kg layout stays the baseline while Module 12 estimates the component weights; Module 14 brings the drag, the weights and the engines together and closes the design.

Key takeaways

  • Takeoff and landing polars: the low-speed \(C_{D0}\) plus flap drag \(F(c_f/c)(S_{\text{flapped}}/\Sref)(\delta_f - 10^\circ)\) and gear drag; a lower Oswald factor with flaps.
  • The business jet climbs out at \(L/D = 12.0\), better than Module 5's assumed 10, and approaches at 8.2.
  • At 12 km and Mach 0.80 it cruises at \(C_L = 0.253\) and \(L/D = 10.8\); its best-range \(C_L\) needs 14.4 km.
  • Sized again: 7503 kg with Module 3's rule, 10 720 kg at 12 km, 8142 kg at FL450; the fuel it has reaches 3390 km at 12 km.
  • The wing loading, the cruise altitude, the engines and the drag are coupled; Module 14 closes them together.
