Lesson 6 · 30 min

Induced Drag and the Lift-to-Drag Ratio

A wing that makes lift leaves a pair of trailing vortices behind it, and the energy that goes into them is the induced drag. It is the second term of the polar, and at the lift coefficients of a long cruise it is as large as all the parasite drag. This lesson estimates the Oswald factor that sets it, and puts the business jet's complete polar together.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the origin of induced drag and what the Oswald efficiency factor includes.
  • Estimate the Oswald factor from the aspect ratio.
  • Assemble a drag polar from the build-up, and find its maximum lift-to-drag ratio and best-range point.

Induced drag and the Oswald factor

Lift pushes air down. The wing's tips let the high pressure below spill round to the low pressure above, the flow rolls up into two trailing vortices, and the downwash they induce tilts the wing's lift backward. For a given span, an elliptic distribution of lift gives the least induced drag, \(C_L^2/(\pi A)\). Module 4 found how twist and taper move the business jet's loading away from elliptic.

The whole aircraft's drag due to lift is larger still: the fuselage interrupts the loading, and the airfoil's own friction and pressure drag grow with lift. The Oswald factor \(e\) gathers all of these into one number, so that \(K = 1/(\pi Ae)\) in the polar. Raymer's statistical fit to real aircraft [1], [4] estimates it from the aspect ratio:

An estimate of the Oswald factor

\[ e = 1.78\left(1 - 0.045A^{0.68}\right) - 0.64 \]

For wings swept less than about \(30^\circ\); highly swept wings have lower factors. It does not apply to very high aspect ratios, such as sailplanes'.

Example 6.1 — The business jet's Oswald factor and \(K\)

Estimate the business jet's Oswald factor (\(A = 8\), quarter-chord sweep \(25^\circ\)) and \(K\).

Show solution
\[ e = 1.78\left(1 - 0.045(8)^{0.68}\right) - 0.64 = 1.78(1 - 0.045(4.113)) - 0.64 = 0.811 \] \[ K = \frac{1}{\pi(8)(0.811)} = 0.04909 \]

A little better than Module 3's 0.75.

The business jet's polar

With the build-up's \(C_{D0}\) and the estimated \(e\), the polar is complete below the drag rise of Lesson 7:

\[ C_D = 0.0202 + 0.04909\,C_L^2 \]

Example 6.2 — The best lift-to-drag ratio and the best range

Find the business jet's \((L/D)_{\max}\) and \(C_L^*\), and its best-range lift coefficient and \(L/D\). Compare with Module 7's 17.52.

Show solution
\[ C_L^* = \sqrt{\frac{0.0202}{0.04909}} = 0.642, \qquad \left(\frac LD\right)_{\max} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{0.0202(0.04909)}} = 15.87 \]

The best range is at \(C_L = 0.642/\sqrt 3 = 0.371\), with \(L/D = 0.866(15.87) = 13.74\). The maximum is 9% below Module 7's 17.52 and 6% below Module 3's 16.9: the higher \(C_{D0}\) outweighs the better \(e\).

Key takeaways

  • Induced drag comes from the trailing vortices; the Oswald factor \(e\) adds the fuselage's effect and the lift-dependent profile drag.
  • \(e \approx 1.78(1 - 0.045A^{0.68}) - 0.64\) for moderately swept wings; the business jet's is 0.811, \(K = 0.04909\).
  • The business jet's polar: \(C_D = 0.0202 + 0.04909C_L^2\); \((L/D)_{\max} = 15.87\) at \(C_L^* = 0.642\); best range at \(C_L = 0.371\), \(L/D = 13.74\).
  • At \(C_L^*\) the two kinds of drag are equal; below it, parasite drag dominates.
