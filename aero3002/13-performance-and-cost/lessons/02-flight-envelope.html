Level Flight and the Flight Envelope

In steady level flight, lift balances weight and thrust balances drag. Where the thrust available meets the thrust required lie the aircraft's fastest and slowest speeds, and at some altitude the two meet: the ceiling. Traced over every altitude, they draw the flight envelope. This lesson draws it for the business jet as Lesson 1 closed it.

Learning objectives

  • Compute the thrust required (the drag) against speed, with its parasite, induced and wave parts, and find the speed for minimum drag.
  • Find the maximum level speed where the thrust available meets the drag.
  • Draw the flight envelope: the stall and thrust limits against altitude, and the absolute ceiling where they meet.

Thrust required

In level flight \(L = W\), so the lift coefficient is set by the speed: \(C_L = W/(qS)\). The drag is then a function of speed alone, at a given altitude and weight:

Thrust required in level flight

\[ D = qS\,C_{D0} + \frac{K W^2}{qS} + qS\,C_{D,\text{wave}}, \qquad q = \tfrac12\rho V^2 \]

\(W\) in newtons. The parasite drag grows with \(V^2\), the induced drag falls with \(1/V^2\); the wave drag appears near the drag-divergence Mach number (Module 9).

The two main terms are equal at the bottom of the curve, the speed for minimum drag, where \(L/D\) is greatest:

Minimum drag

\[ C_L^* = \sqrt{\frac{C_{D0}}{K}}, \qquad V_{md} = \sqrt{\frac{2W}{\rho S\,C_L^*}}, \qquad D_{\min} = \frac{W}{(L/D)_{\max}} \]

Example 2.1 — The business jet's minimum-drag speed at 12 km

Lesson 1's business jet: \(W_0 = 9240\) kg, \(S = 36.96\) m², \(K = 0.04909\); at 12 km and around Mach 0.5, \(C_{D0} = 0.0203\) (its Reynolds number and form factors change a little with speed). At the start of cruise it weighs \(0.9555\,W_0\). Find \(V_{md}\), its Mach number and the minimum drag, with \(\rho = 0.3108\) kg/m³.

Show solution

\(W = 0.9555(9240)(9.807) = 86\,580\) N.

\[ C_L^* = \sqrt{\frac{0.0203}{0.04909}} = 0.643 \] \[ V_{md} = \sqrt{\frac{2(86\,580)}{0.3108(36.96)(0.643)}} = 153.1\ \text{m/s} \]

Mach 0.519 at 295.1 m/s. \((L/D)_{\max} = 1/(2\sqrt{0.0203(0.04909)}) = 15.8\), so \(D_{\min} = 86\,580/15.8 = 5480\) N. At its cruise Mach of 0.80 the business jet flies 54% faster than this, at \(C_L = 0.27\) and \(L/D = 11.7\): fast, but far from efficient (Lesson 3).

Thrust available and the maximum speed

The course has let a jet's thrust fall with the air's density since Module 5: \(T = T_0\,\sigma\), with \(\sigma = \rho/\rho_0\). It is a simple and slightly optimistic model at high Mach number (Module 7). Where \(T = D\) on the right of the curve lies the maximum level speed; between the two curves, the excess thrust can climb or accelerate the aircraft (Lesson 4).

Example 2.2 — The maximum level speed at 12 km

The business jet's two engines give 15.66 kN each at sea level. At 12 km (\(\sigma = 0.2537\)) and \(0.9555\,W_0\), show that it reaches Mach 0.83 and no more.

Show solution

Thrust available: \(T = 2(15\,660)(0.2537) = 7946\) N. At Mach 0.831, \(V = 245.3\) m/s and \(q = 9353\) Pa:

\[ C_L = \frac{86\,580}{9353(36.96)} = 0.250 \] \[ C_D = 0.0193 + 0.04909(0.250)^2 + 0.0005 = 0.0229 \]

\(D = 9353(36.96)(0.0229) = 7920\) N: the thrust available. The wave drag, 0.0005 here, is what stops it: the drag-divergence Mach number at this \(C_L\) is 0.869 (Module 9), and wave drag rises steeply beyond about 0.83. At Mach 0.80 the drag is 7390 N, leaving 560 N: exactly the 300 ft/min climb Lesson 1 sized the engines for.

The flight envelope

Repeating the calculation at every altitude draws the envelope. At low altitude the slowest speed is the stall, at the clean \(C_{L\max}\) of 1.305; the fastest is limited by thrust and wave drag. Climbing, the stall speed rises (the air is thinner) and the thrust falls, until above about 13 km the thrust, not the stall, sets the low-speed limit too. Where the two limits meet is the absolute ceiling: 14.25 km at the start of cruise, where the aircraft can just hold level flight at one speed.

The envelope is drawn by the aircraft's thrust and drag alone. A real one is narrower: the structure limits the speed (the design dive speed of Module 10 and the maximum operating speed below it), the cabin's pressure differential limits the altitude (Module 8), and the certification rules add margins on the stall. At sea level the business jet could in principle fly at Mach 0.83, over 1000 km/h; its structure will be designed for far less.

Key takeaways

  • Thrust required is the drag in level flight: parasite (\(\propto V^2\)), induced (\(\propto 1/V^2\)) and wave drag near the drag-divergence Mach number.
  • Minimum drag at \(C_L^* = \sqrt{C_{D0}/K}\): for the business jet at 12 km, Mach 0.52 and 5480 N; its cruise at Mach 0.80 is far faster and less efficient.
  • The maximum level speed is where thrust meets drag: about Mach 0.83 at 12 km, held by wave drag.
  • The flight envelope's two limits meet at the absolute ceiling, 14.25 km at the start of cruise.
