Closing the Design Loop

Module 3 sized the business jet at 6943 kg with an assumed lift-to-drag ratio and a statistical empty weight. Every module since has replaced an assumption with an estimate, and each one said what its change would do upstream. This lesson runs them all together until they agree: the design loop, closed.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the design loop as an algorithm, and say what each module contributes to it.
  • Carry out one step of the weight loop by hand: layout, polar, mission fuel and group weights at a guessed takeoff weight.
  • Close the thrust loop too: check the requirements with the real drag and resize the engines.

What has changed since Module 3

Module 3's sizing rested on three numbers: a cruise lift-to-drag ratio of 14.6 (from a maximum of 16.9, guessed from the wetted aspect ratio), an empty-weight fraction from a statistical trend, and Module 5's thrust-to-weight ratio of 0.31. Since then the business jet has acquired a wing, a fuselage, engines, a landing gear, a drag polar, design loads, a weight statement and tails sized for stability. The polar is the surprise: Module 9 found a zero-lift drag of 0.0202 where Module 3 assumed 0.0154, and at 12 km the business jet cruises far below its best lift-to-drag ratio, at about 10.8. Module 12's larger tails took it to 10.6.

The weight loop

The sizing equation is Module 3's, but every term now comes from the design itself:

The sizing equation, closed

\[ W_0 = W_{\text{crew}} + W_{\text{payload}} + \frac{W_f}{W_0}\,W_0 + W_e(W_0) \]

\(W_f/W_0\) from the mission flown with the design's own polar; \(W_e\) from Module 11's group weights for the design's own geometry. Both depend on \(W_0\), so the equation is solved by repeating it.

The group weights take the ultimate load factor \(N_z\) of Module 10: 1.5 times the larger of the maneuvering limit of 25.337, which falls as the aircraft grows, and the gust load factor, which rises as the wing loading falls. The loop recomputes it for every design. For the business jet the gust governs throughout: \(N_z = 4.86\) at Module 3's 6943 kg and 250 kg/m², against 4.57 from the maneuvering limit alone, and 4.83 for the closed design below, against 4.33.

The cruise is flown in steps, because the lift-to-drag ratio changes as fuel burns off. At constant altitude and Mach the dynamic pressure is fixed, so the lift coefficient falls with the weight, and so does \(L/D\) for an aircraft already cruising below its best \(C_L\). Each step of distance \(\Delta R\) uses Breguet's equation with the \(L/D\) at its middle:

Cruise in steps

\[ \frac{W_{i+1}}{W_i} = \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta R\,C}{V\,(L/D)_i}\right), \qquad C_{L,i} = \frac{W_i g}{q S} \]

\(C\) per hour, \(V\) in km/h; \((L/D)_i\) at the step's middle weight. The other segments keep Module 3's fractions: warm-up and takeoff 0.970, climb 0.985, loiter for 45 min at \((L/D)_{\max}\) with \(C = 0.55\) per h, landing 0.995, and 6% for reserves and trapped fuel.

Example 1.1 — One step of the weight loop

Start at Module 3's \(W_0 = 6943\) kg, with Module 5's wing loading (250 kg/m²) and \(T/W = 0.31\). Lay out the aircraft, estimate its polar, fly the 4600 km mission at Mach 0.80 and 12 km with \(C = 0.65\) per h, weigh it, and find the next estimate of \(W_0\).

Show solution

The wing: \(S = 6943/250 = 27.77\) m². Module 9's build-up for this layout, with Module 12's tails: \(C_{D0} = 0.0208\) at 12 km and Mach 0.80, \(K = 0.0491\). In cruise \(q = 8660\) Pa, and \(C_L\) falls from 0.268 to 0.190 as the fuel burns; \(L/D\) falls from 11.0 to 8.4, 9.74 on average. Through the mission:

\[ \frac{W_{\text{end}}}{W_0} = 0.6437, \qquad \frac{W_f}{W_0} = 1.06(1 - 0.6437) = 0.3776 \]

Module 11's equations for this geometry, at \(N_z = 4.86\) and times \(k = 1.151\), give \(W_e = 3984\) kg (Module 11's statement, with its smaller tails, gave 3972 kg). Then:

\[ W_0 = 980 + 0.3776(6943) + 3984 = 7586\ \text{kg} \]

A 9% step up, almost all of it fuel: the mission needs 0.378 of the takeoff weight in fuel, not Module 3's 0.287.

Repeating the step converges, but slowly, because each heavier guess needs a bigger wing and more fuel, which feed the next. The figure moves each step 60% of the way to the new estimate, a common way of keeping such loops stable.

At \(T/W = 0.31\) the weight loop closes at 8898 kg: 28% above Module 3's estimate, with a fuel fraction of 0.361 and an empty-weight fraction of 0.529, lower than Module 3's 0.572 because the fuselage, set by the cabin, does not grow with the aircraft.

The thrust loop

Module 5 chose \(T/W = 0.31\) with an assumed \(C_{D0}\) of 0.0165. With the real polar the requirements must be checked again, at the closed design's weight and wing area: cruise at Mach 0.80 and 12 km, a 300 ft/min climb there (the cruise ceiling), a 2.4% climb gradient with one engine failed, and a 1200 m takeoff field (Lesson 5).

Example 1.2 — The ceiling with the real polar

The weight loop at \(T/W = 0.31\) closed at \(W_0 = 8898\) kg, \(S = 35.59\) m², \(C_{D0} = 0.01947\) and \(K = 0.04909\). At the start of cruise the aircraft weighs \(0.9555\,W_0\). What sea-level static \(T/W\) gives 300 ft/min at Mach 0.80 and 12 km, with thrust falling as \(\sigma = 0.2537\)?

Show solution

\(V = 0.80(295.07) = 236.06\) m/s, \(q = 8660\) Pa and \(W = 0.9555(8898)(9.807) = 83\,380\) N:

\[ C_L = \frac{83\,380}{8660(35.59)} = 0.2705, \qquad C_D = 0.01947 + 0.04909(0.2705)^2 = 0.02306 \]

With the little wave drag Module 9's Korn estimate adds (0.00006), \(C_D = 0.02312\) and \(D = 7126\) N. Climbing at 300 ft/min (1.524 m/s) adds \(W(1.524/V) = 538\) N:

\[ \frac{T}{W} = \frac{7126 + 538}{0.2537(8898)(9.807)} = 0.346 \]

Above Module 5's 0.31: the ceiling now sizes the engines. The cruise alone needs 0.322, takeoff 0.330 and engine failure only 0.215.

Larger engines weigh more, the heavier aircraft needs a larger wing, and the larger wing changes the drag: the thrust loop wraps the weight loop. Five more turns settle it at \(T/W = 0.346\) and \(W_0 = 9240\) kg, with 15.7 kN engines of 310 kg each: close to an FJ44-4A (16.1 kN, 304 kg, Module 7), the Citation CJ4's engine.

Closed, but not finished

The closed design is consistent, not acceptable. Its landing distance is 979 m against the 840 m required (Lesson 5), and its 3329 kg of fuel needs 4.161 m³ where its tanks hold 4.160 m³: it only just fails to fit (Lesson 3). Lessons 2 to 7 measure this baseline; Lesson 8 changes the design to meet every requirement.

Key takeaways

  • The design loop: guess \(W_0\), size and lay out the aircraft, estimate its polar, fly the mission, weigh it, check the requirements, and repeat until \(W_0\) stops changing.
  • With the real polar the business jet cruises at \(L/D \approx 10.4\), not 14.6; its fuel fraction rises from 0.287 to 0.360.
  • The weight loop alone closes at 8898 kg; with the engines resized for the 300 ft/min ceiling (\(T/W = 0.346\)), at 9240 kg. The group weights take Module 10's gust load factor: \(N_z = 4.83\), against 4.33 from maneuvers.
  • A closed design is consistent, not necessarily acceptable: this one lands long, and its fuel only just fails to fit.
