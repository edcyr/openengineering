Range and Endurance

Module 3 used Breguet's range equation with one lift-to-drag ratio for the whole cruise. With the real polar, the ratio changes as fuel burns, and how the aircraft flies (its altitude, its speed, whether it climbs as it gets lighter) changes how far it goes on the same fuel. This lesson finds the business jet's best way to cruise, and what its tanks allow.

Learning objectives

  • Apply Breguet's equation to a cruise at constant altitude and to a cruise-climb, and explain why they differ.
  • Find the speed and altitude for the best specific air range of a jet, and the condition for its best endurance.
  • Draw a payload-range diagram, with the limits set by the takeoff weight and by the tanks.

Breguet's equation, again

A jet burns fuel in proportion to its thrust, and in cruise its thrust equals its drag, \(W/(L/D)\). Integrating over the weight it loses gives Breguet's equation, exact when \(V\), \(C\) and \(L/D\) are constant:

Breguet range (jet)

\[ R = \frac{V}{C}\,\frac{L}{D}\,\ln\frac{W_1}{W_2} \]

\(C\): the thrust-specific fuel consumption in Breguet's form, per hour (Module 3); \(V\) in km/h gives \(R\) in km.

At constant altitude and Mach number, \(q\) is fixed, so as the weight falls so does \(C_L\), and with it \(L/D\) for an aircraft already flying below \(C_L^*\). To keep \(L/D\) constant, the aircraft must keep \(C_L\) constant, letting \(q\) fall with the weight: it climbs slowly as it burns fuel. This cruise-climb is the condition under which Breguet's equation is exact. Air traffic control rarely allows it; airliners approximate it with step climbs of 2000 or 4000 ft.

Example 3.1 — Constant altitude against cruise-climb

The business jet (Lesson 1) starts its cruise at 8829 kg and ends it at 6289 kg, having flown 4600 km at Mach 0.80 (849.8 km/h) and 12 km with \(C = 0.65\) per h. Its \(L/D\) fell from 11.66 to 9.13, 10.43 on average. How far would the same fuel take it in a cruise-climb at its starting \(C_L\), with \(L/D = 11.66\) throughout?

Show solution

\(\ln(8829/6289) = 0.3392\). With the average \(L/D\), Breguet's equation gives

\[ R = \frac{849.8}{0.65}(10.43)(0.3392) = 4625\ \text{km} \]

within 0.6% of the 4600 km flown in steps (averaging \(L/D\) is not quite averaging its effect). In a cruise-climb:

\[ R = \frac{849.8}{0.65}(11.66)(0.3392) = 5171\ \text{km} \]

12% farther on the same 2540 kg of fuel, ending about 2.2 km higher. Holding 12 km wastes range: the business jet should cruise higher.

The best speed and altitude

The figure of merit is the specific air range, the distance per kilogram of fuel at the present weight:

Specific air range

\[ \text{SAR} = \frac{V}{\dot m_f} = \frac{V\,(L/D)}{C\,W} \propto \frac{\sqrt{C_L}}{C_D}\ \text{(jet)} \]

With \(C\) constant, a jet's range is best at the largest \(\sqrt{C_L}/C_D\), at \(C_L = \sqrt{C_{D0}/(3K)}\) and a speed 1.32 times the minimum-drag speed, as long as the wave drag stays small.

For the business jet, \(C_L = \sqrt{0.0194/(3 \times 0.04909)} = 0.363\). At 12 km and Mach 0.80 it cruises at \(C_L = 0.27\), so at 12 km it should fly slower (about Mach 0.66 for the best range, at 5% better SAR, but 18% slower), or at Mach 0.80 it should fly higher: the same Mach at \(C_L = 0.363\) needs the thinner air of about 13.9 km. Higher is better on both counts, speed and range, up to the ceiling the engines allow.

Example 3.2 — How much is altitude worth?

Halfway through its cruise, at 7559 kg, the business jet's specific air range at Mach 0.80 is 1.812 km/kg at 12 km and 2.161 km/kg at 14 km. If the whole cruise gained that ratio, how much of the 2540 kg of cruise fuel would it save?

Show solution
\[ 2540\left(1 - \frac{1.812}{2.161}\right) = 410\ \text{kg} \]

About 16% of the cruise fuel, and through the sizing loop a lighter aircraft still. The catch: at 14 km the business jet cannot climb (Lesson 4), so its engines would have to grow. Lesson 8 weighs the two.

A jet's endurance, its time aloft on the fuel, is \(E = (L/D)\ln(W_1/W_2)/C\): it is best at \((L/D)_{\max}\), at the minimum-drag speed, and does not depend on altitude except through \(C\). That is how Module 3's 45-minute loiter is flown: for the business jet, at \(L/D = 16.2\), it costs 2.5% of the weight.

The payload-range diagram

An aircraft can trade payload for fuel, up to the limit of its tanks. The payload-range diagram shows the trade: from the maximum payload at the maximum takeoff weight, along the takeoff-weight limit as payload gives way to fuel until the tanks are full, then down to the ferry range with the tanks full and no payload, the aircraft lighter than its maximum. Each point keeps the mission's allowances: takeoff, climb, the 45-minute loiter, landing and 6% reserves.

The business jet only just misses its own design point. Its tanks hold 4.160 m³, 3328 kg of fuel, and the mission needs 3329 kg: with full tanks and full payload it reaches 4598 km, 2 km short, just below its maximum takeoff weight. The diagram has no second corner: the tanks fill before the takeoff weight is reached. This is the fuel-volume check of Modules 6 and 7, which the closed loop now fails by a litre: a design with no margin at all. Lesson 8's design gains one.

Key takeaways

  • Breguet's equation is exact in a cruise-climb at constant \(C_L\); at constant altitude \(L/D\) falls with the weight.
  • A jet's best range is at the largest \(\sqrt{C_L}/C_D\), \(C_L = \sqrt{C_{D0}/(3K)}\): 0.363 for the business jet, reached at Mach 0.80 near 13.9 km.
  • Cruising at 12 km costs the business jet about 16% of its cruise fuel against 14 km.
  • Endurance is best at \((L/D)_{\max}\); the payload-range diagram shows the payload-fuel trade, limited by the takeoff weight and the tanks.
  • The baseline's tanks are just too small: 4598 km with full payload, not 4600, with no margin. Next, Lesson 4 climbs.
