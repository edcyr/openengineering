Operating Cost

The buyer of a business jet pays for it once and then pays to fly it every hour: for the crew, the fuel, the maintenance, the insurance, the fees, and the money tied up in the aircraft. Over its life these add up to more than its price. This lesson builds the business jet's operating cost item by item, and finds what drives it.

Learning objectives

  • List the items of direct operating cost and estimate each per flight.
  • Separate the costs of owning an aircraft from the costs of flying it, and explain the role of utilization.
  • Express the operating cost per hour, per kilometre and per seat-kilometre, and judge which design changes reduce it.

The items

Operating costs are usually split into direct costs, which depend on the aircraft (ownership, crew, fuel, maintenance, fees), and indirect costs, which depend on the operator (administration, sales, ground facilities). Many methods exist, from airlines' associations and manufacturers; they share the same items. This module follows the structure of AeroSandbox's operating-cost model, with fuel in place of its electric energy:

Operating cost per flight

\[ C_{\text{dep}} = \frac{P(1 - r_{\text{resale}})}{N_f\,Y}, \qquad C_{\text{int}} = \frac{P}{N_f\,Y}\left[\frac{iY}{1 - (1 + i)^{-Y}} - 1\right] \] \[ C_{\text{ins}} = \frac{0.025\,P}{N_f}\sqrt{\frac{n_{\text{pax}} + n_{\text{crew}} + 1}{11}}\sqrt{\frac{U}{1200}} \] \[ C_{\text{mx}} = \frac{P}{3 \times 10^6}\left[(65t + 65) + (58t + 50)\right] \] \[ C_{\text{fuel}} = m_f\,p_f, \qquad C_{\text{crew}} = \frac{n_{\text{crew}}\,c_{\text{crew}}}{N_f}, \qquad C_{\text{ind}} = 0.2\,C_{\text{direct}} \]

\(P\): the aircraft's price; \(U\): hours flown a year; \(t\): block hours per flight; \(N_f = U/t\) flights a year; \(Y\): years of life; \(i\): the real interest rate; \(m_f\), \(p_f\): trip fuel and its price per kg. The maintenance terms (airframe, engines) scale with the price, from a study of small aircraft; fees add landing, terminal and parking charges. Structure and constants after AeroSandbox; the interest is that of a loan repaid over the life.

Example 7.1 — Flying costs: fuel and crew

On its 4600 km design mission the baseline burns 3140 kg of fuel (the 6% reserve is carried, not burned) in a block time of 6.41 h, cruising plus 15 min to taxi. Jet fuel at $0.85 per kg; two pilots at $150 000 a year each, salary and benefits; 450 flight hours a year (this module's assumptions). Find the fuel and crew costs per flight.

Show solution

Fuel: \(3140(0.85) = 2669\) dollars. Flights a year: \(450/6.41 = 70.2\).

\[ C_{\text{crew}} = \frac{2(150\,000)}{70.2} = 4275\ \text{dollars} \]

The crew costs more than the fuel: a business jet's crew is paid whether it flies or not, and 450 hours a year is light use. An airliner flies 3000 hours or more, and its fuel bill dominates.

Example 7.2 — Owning costs: depreciation and interest

The baseline's price is $15.44 million (Lesson 6). It is kept 20 years and sold for 40% of its price, financed at a real interest rate of 4%. Find the depreciation and the interest per flight.

Show solution

Over 20 years it makes \(20(70.2) = 1403\) flights.

\[ C_{\text{dep}} = \frac{15.44 \times 10^6(1 - 0.4)}{1403} = 6603\ \text{dollars} \] \[ \frac{0.04(20)}{1 - 1.04^{-20}} - 1 = 0.472 \] \[ C_{\text{int}} = \frac{15.44 \times 10^6(0.472)}{1403} = 5190\ \text{dollars} \]

Together $11 800 a flight: 37% of the total. Owning the aircraft costs more than flying it.

Utilization

Depreciation, interest, insurance and the crew are paid by the year: per flight hour they fall as the aircraft flies more. Fuel, maintenance and fees are paid per flight and barely change. A business jet flown 450 hours a year costs about $5030 an hour; flown 900 hours, about $3340. Airlines keep their aircraft in the air for this reason, and fractional-ownership programs exist to share one business jet's fixed costs among several owners.

Per unit of transport, the baseline's design mission costs $32 300, \(32\,300/4600 = \$7.02\) per kilometre and 88 cents per seat-kilometre with all eight seats filled, many times an airliner's: the price of flying when and where one wants. For the designer the lessons are these. The price drives the owning costs, and the empty weight drives the price (Lesson 6); the fuel matters less to a business jet than to an airliner, but it is the item a better wing and better engines reduce directly.

This module's operating assumptions

450 flight hours a year; 2 crew at $150 000 each a year; jet fuel at $0.85 per kg; 20 years, 40% resale, 4% real interest; 15 minutes of taxi per flight; AeroSandbox's insurance, maintenance and fee constants, from small commuter aircraft; indirect costs 20% of direct. Each is a placeholder for the operator's own numbers, which the Design Closure tool takes.

Key takeaways

  • Direct operating cost: ownership (depreciation, interest, insurance), crew, fuel, maintenance and fees; indirect costs add about 20%.
  • The baseline's design mission costs about $32 300: $5030 per flight hour, $7.02 per km, 88 cents per seat-km.
  • For a lightly used business jet, owning costs more than flying; utilization spreads the fixed costs.
  • The price, and so the empty weight, drives the largest items; fuel is the item better aerodynamics and engines cut directly.
