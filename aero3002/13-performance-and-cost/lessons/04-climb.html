Lesson 4 · 30 min

Climb, Ceiling and Descent

The thrust left over after the drag is paid for lifts the aircraft. How fast it climbs, how steeply, and how high it can go all follow from the gap between the two curves of Lesson 2. This lesson finds the business jet's best climb, its ceilings, the time and fuel it takes to reach cruise, and how far it glides.

Learning objectives

  • Compute the rate and angle of climb from the excess thrust, and the speeds for the best of each.
  • Distinguish the absolute, service and cruise ceilings, and find them from the best rate of climb against altitude.
  • Estimate the time and fuel to climb, and the glide range.

Rate and angle of climb

In a steady climb at angle \(\gamma\), the thrust balances the drag plus the component of weight along the path: \(T = D + W\sin\gamma\). The vertical speed is \(V\sin\gamma\):

Steady climb

\[ \sin\gamma = \frac{T - D}{W}, \qquad \text{RoC} = V\sin\gamma = \frac{V(T - D)}{W} \]

\(V(T - D)/W\) is the specific excess power, \(P_s\): the rate at which the aircraft can add to its energy height \(h + V^2/(2g)\), by climbing or accelerating.

A jet whose thrust does not change with speed climbs steepest where the drag is least, at the minimum-drag speed: \(\sin\gamma_{\max} = T/W - 1/(L/D)_{\max}\). It climbs fastest at a higher speed, because the rate multiplies the excess thrust by \(V\). Setting the derivative of \(V(T - D)\) to zero, for a parabolic polar:

The jet's best climb speed (thrust independent of speed)

\[ V_{\text{RoC}} = \sqrt{\frac{W/S}{3\rho\,C_{D0}}\left[\frac{T}{W} + \sqrt{\left(\frac{T}{W}\right)^2 + 12\,C_{D0}K}\right]} \]

\(W/S\) in N/m². A propeller aircraft, whose power rather than thrust is nearly constant, climbs fastest much nearer its minimum-power speed.

Example 4.1 — The business jet's best climb at sea level

At \(0.9555\,W_0 = 8829\) kg (86 580 N), the business jet has 31 330 N of thrust at sea level (\(T/W = 0.362\)), \(S = 36.96\) m², \(C_{D0} = 0.0170\) at climb speeds near sea level and \(K = 0.04909\). Find its best climb speed and rate, and its steepest climb angle, with \((L/D)_{\max} = 17.1\) there.

Show solution

\(W/S = 86\,580/36.96 = 2343\) N/m², and \(12\,C_{D0}K = 0.01001\):

\[ V = \sqrt{\frac{2343}{3(1.225)(0.0170)}\left[0.362 + \sqrt{0.1310 + 0.0100}\right]} = 166\ \text{m/s} \]

About Mach 0.49. There \(C_L = 0.138\) and the drag is 11 230 N:

\[ \text{RoC} = \frac{166(31\,330 - 11\,230)}{86\,580} = 38.5\ \text{m/s} = 7590\ \text{ft/min} \] \[ \gamma_{\max} = \arcsin\left(0.362 - \frac{1}{17.1}\right) = 17.7^\circ \]

Far more than a real business jet manages: the course's thrust model keeps the static thrust at any speed. A turbofan at Mach 0.5 gives perhaps 0.78 of it (Module 7's correlation, a recalled fit), which would make the rate about 5000 ft/min. The model is kinder still at altitude, where the ceilings below are set.

Ceilings

The best rate of climb falls with altitude as the thrust does. Three ceilings mark it, at the weight considered:

Ceilings

Absolute: the best rate falls to zero (Lesson 2's envelope closes). Service: 100 ft/min (0.5 m/s) left. Cruise: 300 ft/min left, the margin Module 5 asked for at 12 km.

At the start of cruise the business jet's are 14.25, 13.95 and 13.45 km at its best climb speed. Its 300 ft/min at Mach 0.80 is reached at 12 km: that is what Lesson 1 sized its engines for, and why cruising higher, as Lesson 3 recommends, would need larger engines.

Time and fuel to climb, and the glide

The time to climb is \(t = \int dh/\text{RoC}\). If the best rate fell linearly from \(R_0\) at sea level to zero at the absolute ceiling \(H\), the integral would be \(t = (H/R_0)\ln[1/(1 - h/H)]\). The fuel is the fuel flow, \(C\,T/g\), times the time.

Example 4.2 — How long to 12 km?

With \(R_0 = 38.5\) m/s and \(H = 14\,250\) m, estimate the business jet's time to 12 km with the linear model, and compare it with the step-by-step climb, which takes 13.8 min and 219 kg of fuel.

Show solution
\[ t = \frac{14\,250}{38.5}\ln\frac{1}{1 - 12\,000/14\,250} = 370(1.846) = 683\ \text{s} = 11.4\ \text{min} \]

18% short: the rate falls faster than linearly above the tropopause at 11 km, where the density drops quickest, and the last kilometres take longest. The step-by-step climb also burns 219 kg, 2.4% of the takeoff weight: close to Module 3's historical climb fraction of 0.985, which the mission keeps.

With the engines at idle, the aircraft glides: the flattest glide is at \((L/D)_{\max}\), where \(\tan\gamma = 1/(L/D)\). From 12 km the business jet could glide about \(12 \times 15.8 = 190\) km. In practice an airliner or business jet begins its descent at idle about that far out, so the descent costs little fuel; Module 3's mission folds it into the cruise and landing fractions.

Key takeaways

  • \(\text{RoC} = V(T - D)/W\), the specific excess power; the climb angle \(\sin\gamma = (T - D)/W\).
  • A jet climbs steepest at the minimum-drag speed and fastest at a higher speed: 166 m/s for the business jet at sea level.
  • Absolute, service and cruise ceilings: zero, 100 and 300 ft/min left. The business jet's cruise ceiling at Mach 0.80 is 12 km by design.
  • To 12 km in 13.8 min and 219 kg; the glide from there covers about 190 km.
