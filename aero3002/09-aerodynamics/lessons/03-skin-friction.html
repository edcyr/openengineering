Skin Friction

Most of a subsonic aircraft's zero-lift drag is friction: the air dragged along in the thin boundary layer over every wetted surface. The component build-up starts from the friction of a flat plate of the same length at the same Reynolds number. This lesson finds that friction, laminar and turbulent, with the corrections for Mach number and roughness, for each of the business jet's components.

Learning objectives

  • Compute a component's Reynolds number from the flight condition and its length.
  • Compute the laminar and turbulent flat-plate skin-friction coefficients, with the Mach correction.
  • Estimate the effect of a share of laminar flow, and of surface roughness through the cutoff Reynolds number.

The Reynolds number of each component

Each component is treated as a flat plate of its own length \(l\) in the flow direction: the fuselage's length, the nacelle's length, and the mean aerodynamic chord of the wing and each tail.

A component's Reynolds number

\[ Re = \frac{\rho V l}{\mu}, \qquad \mu = \frac{1.458\times 10^{-6}\,T^{1.5}}{T + 110.4} \]

\(\rho\), \(T\) and the speed of sound come from the standard atmosphere; the viscosity \(\mu\) (Pa s) from Sutherland's law with \(T\) in kelvins.

Example 3.1 — The business jet's Reynolds numbers in cruise

At 12 km (216.65 K, 0.3108 kg/m³, \(a = 295.1\ \text{m/s}\)) and Mach 0.80, find the Reynolds number per meter, and those of the wing (MAC 2.015 m) and the fuselage (15.5 m).

Show solution
\[ \mu = \frac{1.458\times10^{-6}(216.65)^{1.5}}{216.65 + 110.4} = 1.422\times10^{-5}\ \text{Pa s} \] \[ V = 0.80(295.1) = 236.1\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \frac{Re}{l} = \frac{0.3108(236.1)}{1.422\times10^{-5}} = 5.16\times 10^{6}\ \text{per m} \]

The wing's Reynolds number is \(1.04\times10^7\), the fuselage's \(8.0\times10^7\). An airliner at the same Mach number has components two or three times longer, so higher Reynolds numbers and lower friction.

Laminar and turbulent friction

Near the leading edge the boundary layer is laminar: smooth layers sliding over each other, with little friction. After a short distance, at a local Reynolds number of a few hundred thousand on a smooth plate, it becomes turbulent, mixing fast air down to the surface and rubbing it harder. On most of an aircraft the flow is turbulent.

Flat-plate skin friction (average over the plate)

\[ C_{f,\text{lam}} = \frac{1.328}{\sqrt{Re}}, \qquad C_{f,\text{turb}} = \frac{0.455}{(\log_{10} Re)^{2.58}\,(1 + 0.144M^2)^{0.65}} \]

The first is Blasius's laminar solution; the second is Schlichting's turbulent formula with a correction for the Mach number \(M\), which lowers the friction a few percent at high subsonic speeds.

Example 3.2 — Friction on the business jet's wing

Find the business jet wing's turbulent skin-friction coefficient in cruise, and compare it with laminar flow at the same Reynolds number, \(1.04\times10^7\).

Show solution
\[ C_{f,\text{turb}} = \frac{0.455}{(7.017)^{2.58}\,(1 + 0.144(0.80)^2)^{0.65}} = \frac{0.455}{152.4(1.059)} = 0.00282 \] \[ C_{f,\text{lam}} = \frac{1.328}{\sqrt{1.04\times10^7}} = 0.00041 \]

Laminar flow would have a seventh of the friction. The fuselage, at \(8.0\times10^7\), has \(C_f = 0.00207\).

Laminar flow

Real surfaces keep some laminar flow near their leading edges. For a first estimate the build-up takes a share of each surface as laminar and the rest as turbulent, both at the component's Reynolds number. Typical metal wings and tails keep perhaps 10 to 20% laminar flow, fuselages almost none; airfoils and noses shaped for natural laminar flow, with very smooth composite skins, keep much more, which some recent business jets exploit. The business jet's build-up assumes fully turbulent flow, the cautious choice.

Example 3.3 — Twenty percent laminar flow on the wing

If 20% of the business jet's wing kept laminar flow, what would its skin-friction coefficient be?

Show solution
\[ C_f = 0.2(0.00041) + 0.8(0.00282) = 0.00234 \]

17% less friction on the wing, the largest component of the drag (Lesson 5).

Roughness

On a smooth surface the turbulent friction keeps falling as the Reynolds number grows. On a rough one, once the boundary layer's viscous sublayer is thinner than the roughness, the friction stops falling: it stays at its value at a cutoff Reynolds number set by the ratio of the length to the roughness height \(k\).

The cutoff Reynolds number (subsonic)

\[ Re_{\text{cutoff}} = 38.21\left(\frac{l}{k}\right)^{1.053} \]

If the component's \(Re\) is higher, use \(Re_{\text{cutoff}}\) in the turbulent formula.

For the business jet's painted surfaces the cutoffs (\(2.4\times10^7\) for the wing, \(2.0\times10^8\) for the fuselage) are above the actual Reynolds numbers: roughness does not limit them in cruise. Long components of large aircraft at low altitude are where it matters.

Key takeaways

  • Each component is a flat plate of its own length: \(Re = \rho Vl/\mu\), with Sutherland's viscosity.
  • \(C_{f,\text{lam}} = 1.328/\sqrt{Re}\); \(C_{f,\text{turb}} = 0.455/[(\log_{10}Re)^{2.58}(1 + 0.144M^2)^{0.65}]\).
  • A laminar share \(f\) mixes the two: \(C_f = fC_{f,\text{lam}} + (1 - f)C_{f,\text{turb}}\).
  • Roughness caps the Reynolds number at \(38.21(l/k)^{1.053}\).
  • The business jet in cruise: \(5.16\times10^6\) per meter; \(C_f = 0.00282\) on the wing, 0.00207 on the fuselage.
