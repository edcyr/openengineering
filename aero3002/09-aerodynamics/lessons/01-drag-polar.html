Lift, Drag and the Drag Polar

Every performance number of the design, from the takeoff distance to the range, rests on two aerodynamic descriptions: how much lift the wing can give in each configuration, and how much drag the aircraft has at each lift. Module 3 estimated the drag from the wetted area alone. This module builds it up component by component. This lesson sets out what is needed and the shape of the answer: the drag polar.

Learning objectives

  • List the aerodynamic data a conceptual design needs, and where each is used.
  • Write the parabolic drag polar and explain its two terms.
  • Find the maximum lift-to-drag ratio and the lift coefficient where it occurs.
  • Find the lift coefficient and lift-to-drag ratio for the best range of a jet.

What the design needs

  • The lift curve, \(C_L\) against angle of attack, and its slope \(C_{L\alpha}\): Module 4 found it for the business jet's wing. It sets the attitude at takeoff and landing, and the response to gusts.
  • The maximum lift coefficient \(\CLmax\), clean and with the flaps at their takeoff and landing settings: it sets the stall speeds, so the wing loading of Module 5 (Lesson 2).
  • The drag polar, \(C_D\) against \(C_L\), in cruise and in the takeoff and landing configurations: it sets the cruise fuel, the climb gradients, the ceiling and the thrust needed (Lessons 3 to 8).

Module 3 estimated the drag with one number, an equivalent skin-friction coefficient times the ratio of wetted to reference area, and an assumed span efficiency. That is enough to size the aircraft roughly, before it has a shape. Now that the aircraft has a layout (Module 6) and engines (Module 7), each component's drag can be estimated from its own size, shape and Reynolds number.

The parabolic drag polar

Below the speeds where shock waves form, the drag of a whole aircraft is well described by a constant plus a term that grows with the square of the lift:

The parabolic drag polar

\[ C_D = C_{D0} + K C_L^2, \qquad K = \frac{1}{\pi A e} \]

\(C_{D0}\) is the zero-lift (parasite) drag coefficient, mostly skin friction and the pressure drag of thickness; \(KC_L^2\) is the drag due to lift, mostly induced drag, with \(A\) the aspect ratio and \(e\) the Oswald efficiency factor (Lesson 6). Both are referred to the wing's reference area \(\Sref\).

The lift-to-drag ratio \(L/D = C_L/C_D\) is largest where the two drag terms are equal, \(C_{D0} = KC_L^2\):

The maximum lift-to-drag ratio

\[ C_L^* = \sqrt{\frac{C_{D0}}{K}}, \qquad \left(\frac{L}{D}\right)_{\max} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{C_{D0}K}} \]

\(C_L^*\) is the lift coefficient of the best \(L/D\); a jet flies there for the longest endurance (loiter).

For the longest range a jet maximizes not \(L/D\) but \(V\,L/D\), since its fuel flow follows its thrust. At a given altitude \(V \propto 1/\sqrt{C_L}\), so it maximizes \(\sqrt{C_L}/C_D\), which happens where \(C_{D0} = 3KC_L^2\):

\[ C_{L,\text{range}} = \frac{C_L^*}{\sqrt 3} = 0.577\,C_L^*, \qquad \frac{L}{D} = \frac{\sqrt 3}{2}\left(\frac{L}{D}\right)_{\max} = 0.866\left(\frac{L}{D}\right)_{\max} \]

This is where Module 3's rule came from: a jet cruises at 0.866 times its maximum \(L/D\), if it flies at the right lift coefficient.

Example 1.1 — The business jet's polar so far

Module 7 left the business jet with \(C_{D0} = 0.003(5.119) = 0.01536\), \(A = 8\) and \(e = 0.75\). Find \(K\), \(C_L^*\), \((L/D)_{\max}\) and the best-range point.

Show solution
\[ K = \frac{1}{\pi(8)(0.75)} = 0.05305, \qquad C_L^* = \sqrt{\frac{0.01536}{0.05305}} = 0.538 \] \[ \left(\frac{L}{D}\right)_{\max} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{0.01536(0.05305)}} = 17.52 \]

The best range is at \(C_L = 0.577(0.538) = 0.311\), with \(L/D = 0.866(17.52) = 15.17\).

Where the aircraft actually flies

An aircraft does not choose its lift coefficient freely: in level flight the lift equals the weight, so

\[ C_L = \frac{W}{qS} = \frac{W/S}{\tfrac12 \rho V^2} = \frac{W/S}{0.7\,p M^2} \]

with \(q = \tfrac12\rho V^2 = 0.7pM^2\) the dynamic pressure. The wing loading, the altitude and the Mach number together fix \(C_L\), and so where on the polar the aircraft cruises. Lesson 8 returns to this: the business jet's wing was sized for landing (Module 5), and at 12 km and Mach 0.80 it flies well below \(C_L^*\).

Example 1.2 — The business jet's cruise lift coefficient

At mid-cruise the business jet weighs about 90% of \(\Wo = 6943\ \text{kg}\). Find its lift coefficient at 12 km (19 330 Pa) and Mach 0.80 on its 28 m² wing, and its \(L/D\) on Module 7's polar.

Show solution
\[ q = 0.7(19\,330)(0.80)^2 = 8660\ \text{Pa}, \qquad C_L = \frac{0.90(6943)(9.81)}{8660(28)} = 0.253 \] \[ \frac{L}{D} = \frac{0.253}{0.01536 + 0.05305(0.253)^2} = \frac{0.253}{0.01876} = 13.5 \]

Below the best-range \(C_L\) of 0.311, so already short of the 15.2 that Module 3's rule assumed.

Key takeaways

  • Conceptual design needs the lift curve, \(\CLmax\) in each configuration, and the drag polar in each configuration.
  • The parabolic polar: \(C_D = C_{D0} + KC_L^2\), \(K = 1/(\pi Ae)\).
  • \((L/D)_{\max} = 1/(2\sqrt{C_{D0}K})\) at \(C_L^* = \sqrt{C_{D0}/K}\); a jet's best range is at \(0.577\,C_L^*\) with \(0.866\,(L/D)_{\max}\).
  • In level flight \(C_L = (W/S)/q\): the wing loading, altitude and Mach number decide where on the polar the aircraft flies.
