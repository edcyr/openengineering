Maximum Lift and High-Lift Devices

Module 5 chose the business jet's wing loading with maximum lift coefficients of 1.3 clean, 1.7 with takeoff flaps and 2.1 with landing flaps. Those were targets. This lesson checks the clean value against the wing of Module 4, and finds what flaps, and how much of the span, it takes to reach the other two.

Learning objectives

  • Estimate a clean swept wing's maximum lift coefficient from its airfoil's.
  • Describe the trailing- and leading-edge high-lift devices and how each raises the maximum lift.
  • Compute the wing's increment in maximum lift from a device's section increment, the flapped area and the hinge-line sweep.
  • Size the flap span needed for a required landing or takeoff lift coefficient.

The clean wing

A wing stalls before its best airfoil section does: the tips and the root carry less than their share, the boundary layer thickens toward the tips of a swept wing, and the section with the most lift for its \(c_{l,\max}\) stalls first. Module 4 took, for a wing of moderate to high aspect ratio,

The clean wing's maximum lift

\[ C_{L,\max,\text{clean}} \approx 0.9\,c_{l,\max}\cos\Lambda_{c/4} \]

\(c_{l,\max}\) is the airfoil's maximum lift coefficient at the flight Reynolds number and \(\Lambda_{c/4}\) the quarter-chord sweep. At high subsonic Mach numbers shocks lower it further; the value here is for takeoff and landing speeds.

With Module 4's \(c_{l,\max} = 1.6\) and \(25^\circ\) of sweep, the business jet's clean wing reaches \(0.9(1.6)\cos 25^\circ = 1.305\): Module 5's 1.3.

High-lift devices

  • Trailing-edge flaps add camber, and the slotted and Fowler types add chord: a plain flap hinges down; a slotted flap opens a slot that feeds high-energy air over its upper surface, so it can deflect further without separating; a Fowler flap also slides aft, increasing the chord to \(c'\); double- and triple-slotted Fowler flaps go further.
  • Leading-edge devices delay the stall to a higher angle of attack rather than adding lift at a given angle: a slat opens a slot ahead of the wing and extends the chord; a Krueger flap hinges out from the lower surface; a fixed slot is simpler and draggier.
Increments in the section's maximum lift coefficient (source: Raymer, Table 12.2)
Device\(\Delta c_{l,\max}\)

The airfoil's increment becomes the wing's through the share of the wing that the device covers, the flapped area \(S_{\text{flapped}}\): the wing area ahead of and behind the flap, between its inboard and outboard ends. The sweep of the hinge line reduces it, as sweep reduces everything:

The wing's increment in maximum lift

\[ \Delta C_{L,\max} = 0.9\,\Delta c_{l,\max}\,\frac{S_{\text{flapped}}}{\Sref}\cos\Lambda_{\text{H.L.}} \]

\(\Lambda_{\text{H.L.}}\) is the sweep of the device's hinge line. For Fowler-type devices \(\Delta c_{l,\max}\) is multiplied by the extended chord ratio \(c'/c\), as in the table. The increments of trailing- and leading-edge devices add.

Example 2.1 — Flaps for the business jet's landing

The business jet needs \(\CLmax = 2.1\) for landing, without slats. Its flaps start at the fuselage side and hinge at 70% of the chord, a line swept \(19.7^\circ\). How far out must a single-slotted flap reach? A Fowler flap that extends the chord by 15%? A double-slotted Fowler flap with \(c'/c = 1.2\)? The semispan is 7.48 m.

Show solution

The flaps must add \(2.1 - 1.305 = 0.795\). For the single-slotted flap,

\[ \frac{S_{\text{flapped}}}{\Sref} = \frac{0.795}{0.9(1.3)\cos 19.7^\circ} = \frac{0.795}{1.101} = 0.722 \]

which needs flaps out to 5.96 m, 80% of the semispan: too far, with no room left for ailerons. The Fowler flap's increment is \(1.3(1.15) = 1.495\), so it needs \(S_{\text{flapped}}/\Sref = 0.628\), flaps to 5.05 m (67% of the semispan); the double-slotted flap's is \(1.6(1.2) = 1.92\), so 0.489, flaps to 3.90 m (52%). The business jet takes the Fowler flap: simpler than the double-slotted one, and it leaves the outer third of the span for the ailerons.

What the devices do to the lift curve

A flap shifts the lift curve up: more lift at every angle of attack, and a higher maximum, reached at a slightly lower angle. A slat stretches the lift curve: the same lift at a given angle, but the stall moves to a higher angle and a higher \(C_L\). That is why the two are used together on airliners, and why a slat alone would demand a nose-high attitude at the stall, the reason the tail-down angle of Module 8 matters.

Example 2.2 — The takeoff setting

What share of the landing increment must the takeoff setting give for Module 5's \(\CLmax = 1.7\)?

Show solution
\[ \frac{1.7 - 1.305}{2.1 - 1.305} = \frac{0.395}{0.795} = 0.50 \]

Half the landing increment, from a smaller deflection that also adds less drag: the takeoff must climb away on one engine (Module 5), so the flaps are set for lift with as little drag as possible, while the landing setting adds drag on purpose for a steep approach.

Key takeaways

  • Clean wing: \(\CLmax \approx 0.9\,c_{l,\max}\cos\Lambda_{c/4}\); the business jet's is 1.305.
  • Wing increment: \(\Delta\CLmax = 0.9\,\Delta c_{l,\max}(S_{\text{flapped}}/\Sref)\cos\Lambda_{\text{H.L.}}\); Fowler devices multiply \(\Delta c_{l,\max}\) by \(c'/c\).
  • Flaps add lift at every angle; slats delay the stall to a higher angle.
  • The business jet reaches 2.1 for landing with Fowler flaps (\(c'/c = 1.15\)) to 67% of the semispan; its takeoff setting gives half that increment, for 1.7.
