A

Back to top
Area ruling
Shaping the aircraft so that its total cross-sectional area grows and shrinks smoothly along its length, which lowers the wave drag near Mach 1; transports use it in their wing-body fairings and nacelle positions.
See: Lesson 7Related: Wave drag, Supercritical airfoil

B

Back to top
Best-range altitude
The altitude at which an aircraft flying at a given Mach number and weight reaches its best-range lift coefficient. The business jet's, at Mach 0.80, is 14.4 km.
See: Lesson 8Related: Cruise lift coefficient, Best-range lift coefficient
Best-range lift coefficient
The lift coefficient at which a jet goes farthest on its fuel, \(C_L^*/\sqrt3\), where \(C_{D0} = 3KC_L^2\); its \(L/D\) there is 0.866 of the maximum. The business jet's is 0.371, with \(L/D = 13.74\).
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 6Related: Lift coefficient of the best L/D \(C_L^*\), Maximum lift-to-drag ratio \(\LDmax\), Range factor \(M\,L/D\)
Boundary layer
The thin layer of air next to a surface, slowed by viscosity from the surface's speed to the free stream's; its shear is the skin friction.
See: Lesson 3Related: Laminar flow, Turbulent boundary layer, Skin-friction coefficient \(C_f\)

C

Back to top
Component build-up
Estimating the zero-lift drag as the sum over the components of \(C_f\,FF\,Q\,\Swet/\Sref\), plus the miscellaneous drags and leakage and protuberances.
See: Lesson 5Related: Zero-lift drag coefficient \(C_{D0}\), Skin-friction coefficient \(C_f\), Form factor \(FF\), Interference factor \(Q\)
Configuration takeoff, landing
The state of the flaps, slats and gear. Each configuration has its own polar: clean for cruise, takeoff flaps with the gear up for the climb, landing flaps with the gear down for the approach.
See: Lesson 8Related: Flap drag, Landing gear drag
Critical Mach number \(M_{\text{crit}}\)
The flight Mach number at which the flow somewhere on the aircraft first reaches the speed of sound; about \(\MDD - 0.108\) for a first estimate. The business jet's is 0.760.
See: Lesson 7Related: Drag-divergence Mach number \(\MDD\), Wave drag
Cruise lift coefficient
The lift coefficient set by the weight, wing area and dynamic pressure in level flight, \(C_L = W/(qS)\). The business jet's at 12 km and Mach 0.80 is 0.253, far below its best-range 0.371.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 8Related: Dynamic pressure \(q\), Best-range lift coefficient, Best-range altitude
Cutoff Reynolds number
The Reynolds number above which a rough surface's turbulent friction stops falling: \(38.21(l/k)^{1.053}\). If it is below the component's Reynolds number, the friction is taken at the cutoff.
See: Lesson 3Related: Roughness height \(k\), Skin-friction coefficient \(C_f\)

D

Back to top
Drag polar
The drag coefficient against the lift coefficient. Below the drag rise it is close to a parabola, \(C_D = C_{D0} + KC_L^2\). The business jet's is \(C_D = 0.0202 + 0.04909\,C_L^2\).
See: Lesson 1Related: Zero-lift drag coefficient \(C_{D0}\), Drag-due-to-lift factor \(K\), Oswald efficiency factor \(e\)
Drag-divergence Mach number \(\MDD\)
The Mach number at which the drag rise becomes steep, by one definition where \(dC_D/dM = 0.1\). The business jet's at cruise is 0.868; transports cruise near theirs.
See: Lesson 7Related: Critical Mach number \(M_{\text{crit}}\), Korn equation, Wave drag
Drag-due-to-lift factor \(K\)
The factor in \(C_D = C_{D0} + KC_L^2\), \(K = 1/(\pi Ae)\). The business jet's is 0.04909.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 6Related: Oswald efficiency factor \(e\), Drag polar
Dynamic pressure \(q\)
\(q = \tfrac12\rho V^2 = 0.7pM^2\). The business jet's at 12 km and Mach 0.80 is 8660 Pa.
See: Lesson 1Related: Cruise lift coefficient

E

Back to top
Equivalent skin-friction coefficient \(C_{fe}\)
The zero-lift drag over the ratio of wetted to reference area, \(C_{fe} = C_{D0}/(\Swet/\Sref)\): Module 3's first-estimate statistic. The business jet's build-up gives 0.0040, against the 0.0030 Module 3 assumed.
See: Lesson 5Related: Zero-lift drag coefficient \(C_{D0}\), Wetted area \(\Swet\)
Extended chord ratio \(c'/c\)
The chord with a Fowler-type device deployed over the clean chord; it multiplies the device's increment in maximum lift. The business jet's Fowler flaps have 1.15.
See: Lesson 2Related: Fowler flap, Slat

F

Back to top
Fineness ratio \(f\)
A body's length over its diameter (of a circle of the same cross-sectional area). The business jet's fuselage has 9.71, its nacelles 2.22.
See: Lesson 4Related: Form factor \(FF\)
Flap drag
The zero-lift drag of deployed flaps, \(F(c_f/c)(S_{\text{flapped}}/\Sref)(\delta_f - 10^\circ)\), with \(F\) 0.0074 for slotted and Fowler flaps and 0.0144 for plain ones: 0.0070 for the business jet at takeoff, 0.0418 at landing.
See: Lesson 8Related: Configuration, Flapped area \(S_{\text{flapped}}\)
Flapped area \(S_{\text{flapped}}\)
The wing area ahead of and behind a high-lift device, between its ends. The wing's increment is \(0.9\,\Delta c_{l,\max}(S_{\text{flapped}}/\Sref)\cos\Lambda_{\text{H.L.}}\); the business jet needs 0.628 for 2.1.
See: Lesson 2Related: Maximum lift coefficient \(\CLmax\), Hinge-line sweep \(\Lambda_{\text{H.L.}}\)
Form factor \(FF\)
The factor by which a component's drag exceeds a flat plate's of the same area and length, for the supervelocities and wake of its thickness. The business jet's wing has 1.581, its fuselage 1.090.
See: Lesson 4, Lesson 4Related: Component build-up, Supervelocity, Fineness ratio \(f\), Maximum-thickness position \((x/c)_m\)
Fowler flap
A slotted flap that slides aft on tracks before it deflects, extending the chord; its section increment is \(1.3\,c'/c\). The business jet's reach 67% of the semispan.
See: Lesson 2Related: Slotted flap, Extended chord ratio \(c'/c\), Maximum lift coefficient \(\CLmax\)

H

Back to top
Hinge-line sweep \(\Lambda_{\text{H.L.}}\)
The sweep of the line a flap or slat hinges about; it reduces the device's increment by \(\cos\Lambda_{\text{H.L.}}\). The business jet's flap hinge, at 70% of the chord, is swept 19.7°.
See: Lesson 2Related: Flapped area \(S_{\text{flapped}}\)

I

Back to top
Induced drag
The drag that comes with lift: the energy left in the trailing vortices, which tilt the lift backward. At least \(C_L^2/(\pi A)\), for an elliptic loading.
See: Lesson 6Related: Oswald efficiency factor \(e\), Drag-due-to-lift factor \(K\)
Interference factor \(Q\)
A factor on a component's drag for the flow interactions where it meets others: 1.0 for a fuselage or filleted wing, about 1.04 for conventional tails, 1.5 to 1.0 for nacelles as they move away from a surface. The business jet's nacelles take 1.3.
See: Lesson 4Related: Component build-up, Form factor \(FF\)

K

Back to top
Korn equation
\(\MDD = \kappa_A/\cos\Lambda - (t/c)/\cos^2\Lambda - C_L/(10\cos^3\Lambda)\), with \(\kappa_A\) 0.87 for conventional airfoils and 0.95 for supercritical ones.
See: Lesson 7Related: Drag-divergence Mach number \(\MDD\), Supercritical airfoil
Krueger flap
A leading-edge device that hinges forward from the lower surface; its section increment is about 0.3.
See: Lesson 2Related: Slat

L

Back to top
Laminar flow
Boundary-layer flow in smooth layers, with a seventh of the turbulent friction at a Reynolds number of \(10^7\). It lasts only a short way on most surfaces.
See: Lesson 3, Lesson 3Related: Turbulent boundary layer, Transition, Laminar share, Natural laminar flow
Laminar share
The share \(f\) of a surface taken as laminar: \(C_f = fC_{f,\text{lam}} + (1 - f)C_{f,\text{turb}}\). Perhaps 10 to 20% on metal wings and tails, almost none on fuselages; 20% on the business jet's wing would cut its friction 17%.
See: Lesson 3Related: Laminar flow, Skin-friction coefficient \(C_f\)
Landing gear drag
The drag of the extended gear, from each item's drag coefficient times its frontal area (about 0.25 for a wheel and tire, 0.30 for a round strut), plus 20% for interference: about 0.0041 for the business jet.
See: Lesson 8Related: Configuration, Flap drag
Leakage and protuberance drag
The drag of leaking seals, antennas, lights, drains, steps and gaps: a few percent of the parasite drag on a clean jet, more on propeller and military aircraft. The business jet takes 5%.
See: Lesson 5Related: Miscellaneous drag, Component build-up
Lift coefficient of the best L/D \(C_L^*\)
The lift coefficient of the maximum lift-to-drag ratio, \(\sqrt{C_{D0}/K}\), where the zero-lift drag equals the drag due to lift. The business jet's is 0.642.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 6Related: Maximum lift-to-drag ratio \(\LDmax\), Best-range lift coefficient
Lift curve
The lift coefficient against the angle of attack: straight with slope \(C_{L\alpha}\) until near the stall. Flaps shift it up; slats extend it to a higher angle.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 2Related: Maximum lift coefficient \(\CLmax\), Slat
Lift-to-drag ratio \(L/D\)
The ratio of lift to drag, \(C_L/C_D\): it sets the thrust in level flight and the range. The business jet cruises at 10.8 at 12 km and Mach 0.80.
See: Lesson 1Related: Maximum lift-to-drag ratio \(\LDmax\), Cruise lift coefficient

M

Back to top
Maximum lift coefficient \(\CLmax\)
The highest lift coefficient before the wing stalls; about \(0.9\,c_{l,\max}\cos\Lambda\) clean. The business jet's is 1.305 clean, 1.7 with takeoff flaps and 2.1 with landing flaps.
See: Lesson 2Related: Flapped area \(S_{\text{flapped}}\), Fowler flap, Slat
Maximum lift-to-drag ratio \(\LDmax\)
\(1/(2\sqrt{C_{D0}K})\), at \(C_L^*\). The business jet's build-up gives 15.87, against 17.52 in Module 7 and 16.9 in Module 3.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 6Related: Lift coefficient of the best L/D \(C_L^*\), Best-range lift coefficient
Maximum-thickness position \((x/c)_m\)
Where along the chord an airfoil is thickest: about 0.3 for older airfoils, 0.4 to 0.5 for laminar-flow and supercritical ones; farther aft lowers the form factor.
See: Lesson 4Related: Form factor \(FF\), Sweep of the maximum-thickness line \(\Lambda_m\)
Miscellaneous drag
Drags the component formulas miss: the upswept tail cone, blunt bases, open gear and the like, added as \(D/q\) over \(\Sref\).
See: Lesson 5Related: Upsweep drag, Leakage and protuberance drag

N

Back to top
Natural laminar flow
Laminar flow kept over a large share of a surface by its shape (a pressure that falls far aft) and a very smooth skin, without suction; some recent business jets use it on wings and noses.
See: Lesson 3Related: Laminar flow, Laminar share

O

Back to top
Oswald efficiency factor \(e\)
The factor that scales the ideal induced drag for the span loading, the fuselage and the lift-dependent profile drag: \(K = 1/(\pi Ae)\). Estimated as \(1.78(1 - 0.045A^{0.68}) - 0.64\); 0.811 for the business jet.
See: Lesson 6Related: Drag-due-to-lift factor \(K\), Induced drag

P

Back to top
Plain flap
A trailing-edge flap that simply hinges down, adding camber; section increment about 0.9.
See: Lesson 2Related: Slotted flap, Fowler flap
Pressure drag form drag
The drag from the pressures on a body, unbalanced because its boundary layer thickens toward the rear and leaves a wake; the form factor includes it with the friction.
See: Lesson 4Related: Form factor \(FF\), Supervelocity

R

Back to top
Range factor \(M\,L/D\)
For a jet at a given altitude, the range on the fuel burned is proportional to \(M\,L/D\). At 12 km the business jet's is best near Mach 0.66 (9.1), against 8.6 at Mach 0.80.
See: Lesson 7Related: Best-range lift coefficient, Wave drag
Reference area \(\Sref\)
The wing area that every lift and drag coefficient refers to: 28 m² for the business jet.
See: Lesson 1Related: Wetted area \(\Swet\)
Reference length Reynolds number
The length used for a component's Reynolds number: the mean aerodynamic chord for wings and tails, the overall length for fuselages and nacelles.
See: Lesson 3Related: Reynolds number \(Re\)
Reynolds number \(Re\)
\(Re = \rho Vl/\mu\), with \(l\) the component's length. The business jet in cruise has \(5.16\times10^6\) per meter: \(1.04\times10^7\) on its wing, \(8.0\times10^7\) on its fuselage.
See: Lesson 3Related: Skin-friction coefficient \(C_f\), Sutherland's law, Reference length
Roughness height \(k\)
The equivalent sand-grain height of a surface's roughness, from about 0.5 μm for smooth molded composite to 10 μm for camouflage paint; it sets the cutoff Reynolds number.
See: Lesson 3Related: Cutoff Reynolds number

S

Back to top
Skin-friction coefficient \(C_f\)
A flat plate's friction drag over its wetted area and the dynamic pressure: \(1.328/\sqrt{Re}\) laminar, \(0.455/[(\log_{10}Re)^{2.58}(1 + 0.144M^2)^{0.65}]\) turbulent. The business jet's wing has 0.00282 in cruise.
See: Lesson 3Related: Reynolds number \(Re\), Laminar flow, Turbulent boundary layer, Cutoff Reynolds number
Slat
A leading-edge device that opens a slot ahead of the wing and extends the chord; section increment \(0.4\,c'/c\). It delays the stall to a higher angle rather than adding lift at a given angle.
See: Lesson 2, Lesson 2Related: Krueger flap, Maximum lift coefficient \(\CLmax\), Lift curve
Slotted flap
A trailing-edge flap that opens a slot feeding high-energy air over its upper surface, so it can deflect further without separating; section increment about 1.3.
See: Lesson 2Related: Plain flap, Fowler flap
Supercritical airfoil
An airfoil with a flat upper surface that keeps its supersonic pocket weak, and aft camber for lift; it raises Korn's \(\kappa_A\) from about 0.87 to 0.95. The business jet's wing uses one.
See: Lesson 7Related: Korn equation, Drag-divergence Mach number \(\MDD\)
Supervelocity
The local speed over a thick body above the free stream's; it raises the friction and is one reason the form factor exceeds 1.
See: Lesson 4Related: Form factor \(FF\), Pressure drag
Sutherland's law
The viscosity of air against temperature: \(\mu = 1.458\times10^{-6}T^{1.5}/(T + 110.4)\) Pa s, with \(T\) in kelvins; \(1.422\times10^{-5}\) at 12 km.
See: Lesson 3Related: Reynolds number \(Re\)
Sweep of the maximum-thickness line \(\Lambda_m\)
The sweep used in the wing's form factor. The business jet's 30% line is swept 24.4°.
See: Lesson 4Related: Form factor \(FF\), Maximum-thickness position \((x/c)_m\)

T

Back to top
Transition
The change of a boundary layer from laminar to turbulent, at a local Reynolds number of a few hundred thousand on a smooth plate, earlier with roughness or an adverse pressure gradient.
See: Lesson 3Related: Laminar flow, Turbulent boundary layer
Turbulent boundary layer
A boundary layer in which eddies mix fast air down to the surface, with much more friction than a laminar one; most of an aircraft's surface is turbulent.
See: Lesson 3Related: Laminar flow, Skin-friction coefficient \(C_f\), Transition

U

Back to top
Upsweep drag
The drag of an upswept aft fuselage: \(D/q = 3.83u^{2.5}A_{\max}\), with \(u\) the upsweep of the tail cone's centerline in radians. The business jet's 7.41° adds 0.00165.
See: Lesson 5Related: Miscellaneous drag

W

Back to top
Wave drag
The drag of shock waves and the separation they cause, above the critical Mach number; a first estimate is \(20(M - M_{\text{crit}})^4\). Negligible for the business jet at Mach 0.80.
See: Lesson 7Related: Critical Mach number \(M_{\text{crit}}\), Drag-divergence Mach number \(\MDD\), Area ruling
Wetted area \(\Swet\)
The area of a component's surface in contact with the air; Module 6's for the business jet total 143.3 m², 5.12 times the reference area.
See: Lesson 5Related: Component build-up, Equivalent skin-friction coefficient \(C_{fe}\), Reference area \(\Sref\)

Z

Back to top
Zero-lift drag coefficient \(C_{D0}\)
The drag coefficient at zero lift, mostly skin friction and the pressure drag of thickness. The business jet's build-up gives 0.0202 in cruise and 0.0189 at low speed, against Module 3's 0.0154.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 5Related: Component build-up, Equivalent skin-friction coefficient \(C_{fe}\), Drag polar
