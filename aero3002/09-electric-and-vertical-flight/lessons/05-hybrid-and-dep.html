Hybrid-Electric and Distributed Propulsion

If batteries cannot carry the whole mission, they might carry part of it, or electric power might earn its place by what it does for the airframe rather than by the energy it stores. This lesson measures both ideas on the business jet: hybrids that keep the fuel and add a battery, and distributed propulsion, where many small electric propellers let a much smaller wing do the work, as on NASA's X-57.

Learning objectives

  • Define the power and energy degrees of hybridization of a hybrid-electric aircraft.
  • Compute the range cost of replacing part of the fuel with batteries.
  • Size a battery boost and decide whether it saves weight against a larger engine.
  • Explain how high-lift propellers raise the wing loading, and compute the cruise \(L/D\) of the smaller wing at constant span and at constant aspect ratio.

Degrees of hybridization

Lesson 1's series and parallel hybrids have two sources of power, fuel and battery. Two ratios describe how much of each they use:

Degrees of hybridization

\[ H_P = \frac{P_{\text{motor}}}{P_{\text{motor}} + P_{\text{engine}}}, \qquad H_E = \frac{E_{\text{bat}}}{E_{\text{bat}} + E_{\text{fuel}}} \]

\(H_P\) is the share of the installed power that is electric, \(H_E\) the share of the stored energy. A turboelectric aircraft has \(H_E = 0\); an all-electric one has \(H_P = H_E = 1\).

A hybrid can run its engine at its best point and let the battery cover the peaks, taxi and descend on electric power, and keep flying if the engine fails. Whether it saves fuel depends on whether those gains beat the battery's mass and the second conversion of a series system (Lesson 1: 0.2102 against 0.2352).

Swapping fuel for batteries

The simplest hybrid keeps the business jet's 6943 kg and replaces part of its 1990.6 kg of fuel with batteries, used first, at \(\LDmax\), then the fuel by Breguet. For the cruise alone,

\[ R = \frac{e_b\,\eta}{g}\,\LDmax\,\frac{m_b}{m_0} + \frac{V}{C}\,(0.866\,\LDmax)\,\ln\frac{m_0}{m_0 - m_f} \]

Example 5.1 — 500 kg of batteries

Replace 500 kg of the business jet's fuel with 200-Wh/kg batteries. Find the cruise range and the energy degree of hybridization.

Show solution
\[ R_b = \frac{200(3600)(0.7076)}{9.807}(17.52)\frac{500}{6943} = 65.5\ \text{km} \] \[ R_f = \frac{850}{0.65}(15.17)\ln\frac{6943}{6943 - 1490.6} = 4794\ \text{km}, \qquad R = 4859\ \text{km} \] \[ H_E = \frac{500(0.2)}{500(0.2) + 1490.6(12)} = \frac{100}{17\,987} = 0.0056 \]

A quarter of the store is now battery, but it holds 0.56% of the energy, and the range has fallen from 6702 to 4859 km. The first 100 kg of batteries cost about 380 km. A hybrid must earn its battery some other way.

Battery boost

One way is to let the battery supply the power that only a short part of the flight needs, so the engine can be sized for the rest. Module 5's constraint diagram is the place to look. There the business jet's engines were set by the ceiling, 300 ft/min at 12 km, at \(\TWo = 0.308\); cruise needed only 0.284, takeoff 0.287 and the one-engine-inoperative climb 0.248. A boost for takeoff would save nothing, because takeoff does not size the engines. A boost for the climb near the ceiling might.

Example 5.2 — Boost for the ceiling

Size the engines for cruise (\(\TWo = 0.284\)) and let a battery-driven fan supply the rest of the ceiling's thrust for 10 minutes. The thrust lapses with \(\sigma = 0.2537\) at 12 km, where \(V = 236.1\) m/s. Use 200 Wh/kg, at most 3C, motors at 5 kW/kg and Lesson 1's efficiencies; Module 7's engines weigh 2 × 200.8 kg and scale with thrust.

Show solution
\[ \Delta T = (0.308 - 0.284)(68\,090)(0.2537) = 414.6\ \text{N}, \qquad \Delta T\,V = 97.9\ \text{kW} \] \[ P_{\text{shaft}} = \frac{97.9}{0.80} = 122.3\ \text{kW} \] \[ P_{\text{bat}} = \frac{122.3}{0.8845} = 138.3\ \text{kW} \] \[ E = 138.3\,\frac{10}{60} = 23.05\ \text{kWh} \]

The energy alone needs 115 kg of battery, but delivering 138.3 kW at 3C needs 46.1 kWh, 230.5 kg: power sets the battery. With 24.5 kg of motor, the boost weighs 255 kg. The engines, sized for 0.284 instead of 0.308, are 7.8% smaller and 31.3 kg lighter. The hybrid is 224 kg heavier.

Boost pays when the engine would otherwise be much larger than cruise needs for a short time, as in a vertical takeoff (Lesson 8) or a short-field aircraft, and when the battery's specific power is high. For the business jet, whose constraints are close together, a slightly larger turbofan is lighter than any battery.

Distributed electric propulsion: the X-57

Electric motors are about as efficient small as large, and a wire is easier to route than a shaft. That lets the propulsion be split among many propellers placed where they help the airframe: distributed electric propulsion (DEP). NASA's SCEPTOR project (Lesson 1), designated the X-57 Maxwell [9], uses two kinds [7]:

  • High-lift propellers along the leading edge blow the wing at low speed, raising the dynamic pressure over it and so its maximum lift. They fold against their nacelles in cruise.
  • Wingtip cruise propellers turn against the tip vortex, which can cut the propulsive power by about 10% [7].

Because the stall speed no longer sets the wing's size, the wing can be sized for cruise. SCEPTOR replaces the Tecnam P2006T's wing with a much smaller one (Figure 1.1):

The wing is about 2.5 times smaller, and the aircraft is estimated to use 4.8 times less energy than the stock one at its cruise point, 150 knots and 8000 ft [7]. About threefold comes from replacing 30%-efficient piston engines with a 90%-efficient electric drive (Lesson 1); the rest, about 1.6 times, from the higher wing loading and the tip propellers, which the project's preliminary studies showed could exceed 1.5 times [7]. The design gross weight is 3000 lb, with 60-kW cruise motors [7].

A business jet with blown high lift

Module 5 found that the business jet's landing distance sets its wing loading: 840 m at 0.9 \(W_0\) with \(C_{L,L} = 2.1\) gave \(\WSo \le 250\) kg/m². At a given field length that limit is proportional to the landing lift coefficient, so doubling it with high-lift propellers would allow 500 kg/m². What would a smaller wing do in cruise?

Hold the fuselage, tails and nacelles and Module 7's skin-friction coefficient \(C_{fe} = 0.003\), and scale the wing's 48.88 m² of wetted area with its area. The zero-lift drag coefficient, referred to the smaller wing, rises; whether the induced drag rises too depends on the span.

Example 5.3 — A 400-kg/m² wing

High-lift propellers raise \(C_{L,L}\) to 3.36. Find the wing, and its cruise \(L/D\) at constant span and at \(A = 8\), against today's 14.37.

Show solution
\[ \WSo = 250\,\frac{3.36}{2.1} = 400\ \text{kg/m}^2, \qquad S = \frac{6943}{400} = 17.36\ \text{m}^2 \]

At constant span, \(A = 14.97^2/17.36 = 12.91\); the wetted area is \(94.45 + 48.88(17.36/28) = 124.8\) m², so

\[ \CDz = \frac{0.003(124.8)}{17.36} = 0.02156 \] \[ K = \frac{1}{\pi(12.91)(0.75)} = 0.03289 \] \[ \CL = \frac{68\,090}{8660(17.36)} = 0.4530 \] \[ \frac{L}{D} = \frac{0.4530}{0.02156 + 0.03289(0.4530)^2} = 16.00 \]

11% better. At \(A = 8\) the span shrinks to 11.78 m, \(K\) stays 0.05305 and \(L/D = 13.96\), 3% worse. Like the X-57, a DEP wing pays only if it keeps its span, which makes it a slender, high-aspect-ratio wing.

Around the design loop, the smaller wing changes much more than the drag. Its box holds less fuel: at constant span its volume goes roughly as \(S^2\), so Module 7's 2.05 m³ of wing tanks would shrink to about 0.79 m³, and with the 0.60 m³ center tank the jet could not carry its 2.49 m³ of fuel. A wing of aspect ratio 12.9 is heavier (Module 12's weight equations), the blowing needs a dozen motors with their power and redundancy, the cruise lift coefficient of 0.45 at Mach 0.80 brings wave drag closer (Module 10), and a highly loaded wing raises the sink rate the landing gear must absorb [7]. Each of these would send the design back to Module 3.

Key takeaways

  • \(H_P\) is the electric share of the power, \(H_E\) of the stored energy.
  • Swapping 500 kg of the business jet's fuel for batteries cuts its cruise range from 6702 to 4859 km and gives \(H_E = 0.0056\).
  • Battery boost pays only where an engine would be much larger than cruise needs; for the business jet's ceiling it adds 224 kg.
  • SCEPTOR's high-lift propellers let its wing shrink from 158.9 to 66.7 ft² at aspect ratio 15; it is estimated to use 4.8 times less energy in cruise [7].
  • A business jet wing at 400 kg/m² gains 11% in cruise \(L/D\) if it keeps its span, and loses 3% if it keeps its aspect ratio; it also loses most of its fuel volume.
