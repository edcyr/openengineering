Electric Propulsion Systems

An electric motor turns more than 90% of the energy it is given into shaft work; a gas turbine or piston engine turns about 30% of its fuel's heat into work. That difference, and the freedom to put small motors anywhere on an airframe, is why designers are looking hard at electric and hybrid-electric aircraft. This lesson takes the system apart, from the energy store to the propulsor, and asks what an all-electric version of the business jet would draw in cruise.

Learning objectives

  • Name the components of an electric propulsion system and the efficiency of each.
  • Compute the efficiency of a power chain, from the energy store to the thrust power.
  • Describe the all-electric, series hybrid, parallel hybrid and turboelectric architectures, and the paths their power takes.
  • Compare the energy per kilometer of the business jet with that of an all-electric version of it.

Why electric?

NASA's Scalable Convergent Electric Propulsion Technology Operations Research project, SCEPTOR, set out to show what electric propulsion can do for a light aircraft [7]. It retrofits a Tecnam P2006T light twin, in stages called "Mods", with two kinds of distributed electric propulsion: small high-lift propellers along the wing's leading edge, which blow the wing at low speed and fold flat against their nacelles in cruise, and two larger cruise propellers at the wingtips [7]. NASA designated the demonstrator the X-57 "Maxwell", its first all-electric experimental aircraft, or X-plane; in the final configuration, Mod IV, twelve high-lift motors line the leading edge and the cruise motors sit at the wingtips [9]. The new wing has about 2.5 times less area than the stock one (Figure 1.1). The design targets a gross weight of 3000 lb and a cruise of 150 knots true airspeed at 8000 ft, where it is estimated to use 4.8 times less energy than the stock aircraft; the stock P2006T weighs up to 2712 lb and has two 74-kW Rotax 912S engines, and the cruise motors are rated at 60 kW maximum continuous power [7].

The same design paper puts the case for electric propulsion in two numbers: a typical aviation combustion engine is about 30% efficient against the lower heating value of its fuel, while modern electric motors "can exceed 90%" when the motor, its controller and the battery's discharge are taken together [7]. For the same aircraft at the same speed, that alone is about a threefold cut in the energy used. Electric motors are also compact, quiet and reliable, emit nothing in flight, and keep their efficiency over a wide range of sizes [7]. Because a small motor is about as efficient as a large one, the power can be split among many propulsors placed where they help the airframe most: distributed electric propulsion, the subject of Lesson 5.

The difficulty is the energy store: batteries hold 60 to 100 times less energy per kilogram than aviation fuel [7]. Lessons 2 to 4 measure what that does to range and to the takeoff weight; Lesson 5 looks at hybrids that keep fuel and add electric power where it pays. Chapter 20 of [1] treats electric aircraft at greater length.

The parts of an electric propulsion system

Each part of the chain turns one form of power into another and loses a little of it as heat. The FAA's special conditions for the first electric aircraft engines, the magniX magni350 and magni650 [2] and the Safran ENGINeUS 100A1 [3], define the novel part of an electric engine as an electric motor, its controller and the high-voltage systems that join them, used as the primary source of propulsion; the battery and the propulsor belong to the aircraft. Table 1.1 gives typical efficiencies for each part; use them in the examples that follow.

Table 1.1 The parts of an electric propulsion system and typical efficiencies
PartWhat it doesEfficiency
BatteryStores chemical energy; its internal resistance turns part of the current into heat as it discharges0.95
Inverter (motor controller)Turns the battery's direct current into the alternating current the motor needs, at the frequency its speed needs0.98
MotorTurns electrical power into shaft power; permanent-magnet machines are the usual choice0.95
PropulsorPropeller or ducted fan; its propulsive efficiency turns shaft power into thrust power \(TV\)0.80
GeneratorIn a hybrid, turns an engine's shaft power into electrical power0.96
RectifierIn a hybrid, turns the generator's alternating current into direct current0.98
GearboxMatches a fast motor or turbine to a slower propeller0.98
Gas turbine or piston engineTurns the fuel's heat into shaft power (its thermal efficiency)0.30

Typical values. Real components vary with their size, speed and load.

The efficiency of the whole system is the product of its parts'. Written from the energy store to the thrust power,

The efficiency chain

\[ \eta = \prod_i \eta_i, \qquad \eta_{\text{elec}} = \eta_{\text{bat}}\,\eta_{\text{inv}}\,\eta_{\text{mot}}\,\eta_p = 0.95(0.98)(0.95)(0.80) = 0.7076 \]

Power drawn from the store is the thrust power over the chain's efficiency: \(P_{\text{store}} = TV/\eta = DV/\eta\) in level flight. Every loss on the way becomes heat that the aircraft must reject (Lesson 6).

Architectures

The same components can be arranged in several ways. Figure 1.2 draws the five that matter for conceptual design, with NASA's colors: green for fuel, blue for electrical power and orange for shaft power.

  • All-electric: battery, inverter, motor, propulsor. No fuel, no combustion, and no weight change in flight (Lesson 3).
  • Series hybrid: an engine drives only a generator; the battery and the generator feed the motors. The engine can run at its best speed and the propulsors can be anywhere, but every watt from the fuel is converted twice.
  • Parallel hybrid: the engine and a motor turn the same shaft. The engine does the work in cruise; the battery adds power for takeoff and climb, so the engine could be sized for cruise.
  • Turboelectric: a series hybrid without the battery (or with only a small one). Wires instead of shafts let one gas turbine drive many distributed propulsors.
  • Conventional: the reference, fuel to engine to propulsor, through a gearbox for a turboprop.

Example 1.1 — How efficient is each chain?

With Table 1.1, find the efficiency from the energy store to the thrust power of the all-electric, series hybrid and conventional turboprop chains. Compare them with the business jet's turbofans in cruise: Module 3 gave them \(C = 0.65\) per hour, \(c_T = 18.41\) mg/(N s), at \(V = 236.1\) m/s, with Jet A-1's \(h = 43.2\) MJ/kg (Module 7).

Show solution
\[ \eta_{\text{elec}} = 0.95(0.98)(0.95)(0.80) = 0.7076 \] \[ \eta_{\text{series}} = 0.30(0.96)(0.98)(0.98)(0.95)(0.80) = 0.2102 \] \[ \eta_{\text{conv}} = 0.30(0.98)(0.80) = 0.2352 \]

The turbofan's overall efficiency, from Module 7, is the thrust power over the fuel's heat:

\[ \eta_0 = \frac{V}{c_T\,h} = \frac{236.1}{18.41\times10^{-6}(43.2\times10^6)} = 0.2968 \]

The all-electric chain is 2.4 times as efficient as the turbofan, and 3.4 times as efficient as the series hybrid, whose two conversions cost it 11% against the conventional turboprop. A hybrid earns its losses only by what it does for the rest of the aircraft (Lesson 5).

Power through the chain

Suppose the business jet were all-electric, keeping its takeoff weight of 6943 kg, its wing and Module 7's polar: \(\CDz = 0.01536\), \(A = 8\), \(e = 0.75\), so \(\LDmax = 17.52\) at \(\CL^* = 0.538\). A battery aircraft does not get lighter as it flies, and Lesson 3 shows that it flies farthest at \(\LDmax\). At 12 km (\(\rho = 0.3108\) kg/m³) that is at

\[ V_{md} = \sqrt{\frac{2W}{\rho S \CL^*}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(6943)(9.807)}{0.3108(28)(0.538)}} = 170.5\ \text{m/s} \]

Mach 0.58, slower than the jet's Mach 0.80. Figure 1.3 follows that cruise's power back through the chain.

Example 1.2 — The all-electric business jet in cruise

Find the thrust power, the shaft power and the power drawn from the battery for the all-electric business jet at 12 km and 170.5 m/s.

Show solution
\[ TV = \frac{WV}{\LDmax} = \frac{6943(9.807)(170.5)}{17.52} = 662.9\ \text{kW} \] \[ P_{\text{shaft}} = \frac{662.9}{0.80} = 828.6\ \text{kW}, \qquad P_{\text{bat}} = \frac{662.9}{0.7076} = 936.8\ \text{kW} \]

About 274 kW, nearly a third of what the battery gives, ends up as heat: 166 kW of it in the propulsor's wake and 108 kW in the battery, inverter and motor, which must be cooled. Lesson 2 asks how large a battery can deliver 937 kW, and for how long.

Energy per kilometer

Dividing the power drawn from the store by the speed gives the energy per unit distance, the measure SCEPTOR uses to compare its electric aircraft with the original [7]: \(E/R = P_{\text{store}}/V = W/(\eta\,\LD)\), so it depends only on the weight, the lift-to-drag ratio and the chain. For the business jet at its takeoff weight,

\[ \text{turbofan: } \frac{E}{R} = \frac{6943(9.807)}{0.2968(15.17)} = 15.12\ \text{MJ/km} = 4.20\ \text{kWh/km} \] \[ \text{electric: } \frac{E}{R} = \frac{6943(9.807)}{0.7076(17.52)} = 5.49\ \text{MJ/km} = 1.53\ \text{kWh/km} \]

(the jet flies at \(0.866\,\LDmax = 15.17\), Module 3's best-range point for a jet). The electric version needs 2.75 times less stored energy per kilometer. But a kilogram of battery stores far less than a kilogram of fuel, which is where Lesson 2 begins.

Key takeaways

  • An electric engine is a motor, its controller and the high-voltage systems between them; the battery stores the energy and the propulsor makes the thrust.
  • NASA's SCEPTOR (X-57) replaces a light twin's wing with one 2.5 times smaller, blown by 12 high-lift propellers, with cruise propellers at the tips.
  • The chain's efficiency is the product of its parts': about 0.71 from battery to thrust power, against 0.30 for the business jet's turbofans and 0.21 for a series hybrid.
  • All-electric, series, parallel and turboelectric architectures differ in where the energy is stored and how often it is converted.
  • An all-electric business jet would draw 937 kW from its battery to cruise at 12 km, and need 2.75 times less stored energy per kilometer than the jet.
  • Next, Lesson 2 asks how much energy and power a battery can hold.
