Electric Motors and Their Certification

An electric motor's mass follows its torque, its losses come out as low-temperature heat that has to be carried away, and the rules that certify aircraft engines were written for engines that burn fuel. This lesson sizes the business jet's motors for torque and heat, and reads the special conditions under which the FAA has certified the first electric engines.

Learning objectives

  • Relate a motor's power, torque and speed, and explain why its mass follows its torque.
  • Decide when a gearbox between a fast motor and a slow propeller saves weight.
  • Compute the heat an electric drive must reject and the coolant flow that carries it.
  • Describe how the FAA certifies electric engines, and what the special conditions add to 14 CFR Part 33.

Power, torque and speed

A motor's shaft power is its torque times its angular speed:

Motor power, torque and mass

\[ P = Q\,\omega = Q\,\frac{2\pi N}{60}, \qquad \frac{m_1}{m_2} = \left(\frac{Q_1}{Q_2}\right)^{0.8587} \]

\(Q\) in N·m, \(N\) in rpm. The second relation is an empirical motor-weight fit, weight proportional to \(Q^{0.8587}\), used with an extra 63% for the inverter, cooling, wiring and shielding (a "tech factor" of 1.63) in NASA's air-taxi study [8].

Aircraft motors are mostly permanent-magnet synchronous machines: magnets on the rotor, copper windings on the stator, fed by an inverter. The force between them per unit of rotor surface is limited by the magnets and the cooling, so the torque a motor can give grows with its rotor's size, and its mass with its torque. For a given power a fast motor is small and light; a propeller wants to turn slowly. Safran's ENGINeUS 100A1, for example, is a direct-drive, radial-flux, permanent-magnet motor in two sections, each a three-phase motor with its own inverter [3]: direct drive, so no gearbox, and two of everything, so no single fault stops it.

Example 6.1 — Direct drive or gearbox?

Each of the all-electric business jet's two motors delivers 1.04 MW (Lesson 4: 2083 kW in all). Find its torque driving a fan directly at 2000 rpm, and through a 5:1 gearbox at 10 000 rpm, and the ratio of the two motors' masses.

Show solution
\[ Q_{2000} = \frac{1.04\times10^6}{2\pi(2000)/60} = 4966\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}, \qquad Q_{10\,000} = 993.1\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m} \] \[ \frac{m_{2000}}{m_{10\,000}} = 5^{0.8587} = 3.98 \]

The direct-drive motor weighs four times the fast one. The gearbox gives some of that back in its own mass and in a 2% loss, and it multiplies the inertia the motor must accelerate by the square of the gear ratio, which slows a propeller controlled by its speed [8]. Large, slow rotors (Lesson 7) favor gearboxes; small, fast propellers favor direct drive.

Heat

A motor that delivers \(P\) at efficiency \(\eta\) takes \(P/\eta\) and turns the difference into heat, in its windings, magnets and iron:

Heat and coolant flow

\[ \dot Q = P\,\frac{1 - \eta}{\eta}, \qquad \dot m = \frac{\dot Q}{c_p\,\Delta T} \]

\(\dot m\) is the coolant flow that carries \(\dot Q\) away while warming by \(\Delta T\) (for a water-glycol mixture, \(c_p \approx 3500\) J/(kg·K)).

The inverter and the battery lose heat the same way. A turbofan also loses most of its fuel's energy as heat, but it carries it away in its hot exhaust. An electric drive's heat comes out at the temperature its magnets, semiconductors and cells can stand, typically well under 200 °C, and for the battery near 60 °C, and it has to be pushed through radiators into the air. The radiators and their ducts add mass and drag. SCEPTOR's cruise motors and controllers are air-cooled, with a radial inlet on each nacelle to feed the stator and magnets [7].

Example 6.2 — Cooling the business jet's motors

Each 1.04-MW motor is 95% efficient. How much heat does it reject, and what water-glycol flow carries it away with a 10-K rise? What is the whole drive's heat at takeoff (2083 kW of shaft power, with the inverters at 98% and the battery at 95%)?

Show solution
\[ \dot Q_{\text{mot}} = 1.04\times10^6\,\frac{0.05}{0.95} = 54.7\ \text{kW}, \qquad \dot m = \frac{54\,700}{3500(10)} = 1.564\ \text{kg/s} \]

For the drive at takeoff: the motors take \(2083/0.95 = 2193\) kW, the inverters \(2193/0.98 = 2238\) kW and the battery gives \(2238/0.95 = 2355\) kW, so

so the motors, inverters and battery lose

\[ \dot Q = 109.6 + 44.7 + 117.8 = 272\ \text{kW} \]

About 272 kW of low-grade heat, 13% of the shaft power, to be rejected on a takeoff that may start on a hot day.

Certifying an electric engine

Aircraft engines are type-certificated to 14 CFR Part 33, written in the 1960s for piston and turbine engines that burn fuel [2], [3]. Its subparts B to G apply only to those, and they guard against the failures of combustion and of hot, highly stressed rotating parts. When an engine has a "novel or unusual design feature" that the rules do not cover, the FAA issues special conditions (14 CFR 21.16) that set the extra standards it needs for an equivalent level of safety. For electric engines the novel feature is the same in both cases issued so far: an electric motor, its controller and high-voltage systems used as the primary source of propulsion [2], [3].

  • magniX magni350 and magni650, Special Conditions No. 33-022-SC, effective 27 October 2021 [2]. magniX applied on 18 April 2019; the models were first called the magni250 and magni500.
  • Safran ENGINeUS 100A1, Special Conditions No. 33-23-01-SC, effective 27 January 2025, for FAA validation of an engine for a single-engine airplane [3].

Both were developed from the technical criteria of ASTM F3338-18, Standard Specification for Design of Electric Propulsion Units for General Aviation Aircraft [4], and Safran's also from magniX's [3]. The special conditions keep the parts of Part 33 that still apply (materials, fire protection, cooling, mounts) and add or replace others:

Neither document states the engines' power ratings; those go in the type certificate data sheets [2]. For the designer the message is that an electric engine is certified as an engine, with its controller and wiring, and that its duty cycle, cooling and power supply are part of its rating: the aircraft must provide them.

Key takeaways

  • \(P = Q\omega\); a motor's mass follows its torque, about as \(Q^{0.86}\), plus 63% for its accessories [8].
  • A 1.04-MW direct-drive motor at 2000 rpm weighs about four times one at 10 000 rpm; a gearbox costs mass, 2% and responsiveness.
  • Heat \(P(1 - \eta)/\eta\) comes out at low temperature: the business jet's drive would reject 272 kW at takeoff.
  • Electric engines are certified to Part 33 with special conditions: 33-022-SC (magniX) [2] and 33-23-01-SC (Safran) [3], built on ASTM F3338-18 [4].
