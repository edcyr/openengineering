Lesson 3 · 30 min

Electric Range and Endurance

Module 3's Breguet equation has a logarithm because a jet gets lighter as it burns fuel. A battery aircraft lands as heavy as it took off, so its range equation is simpler, and it says something different about how to fly. This lesson derives it, finds the speeds for the longest range and the longest flight, and measures an all-electric business jet against its 4600-km requirement.

Learning objectives

  • Derive the range and endurance of a battery-electric aircraft in level flight.
  • Explain why its best range is at \(\LDmax\) and its best endurance at minimum power, and find both speeds.
  • Compute the range of an all-electric business jet, and the specific energy its requirement would need.
  • Compare battery range with the Breguet range of a jet carrying the same mass of fuel.

The battery range equation

In level flight the thrust equals the drag, and the battery delivers the thrust power through the chain of Lesson 1:

\[ P_{\text{bat}} = \frac{DV}{\eta} = \frac{W V}{\eta\,(L/D)} \]

A battery of mass \(m_b\) and usable specific energy \(e_b\) stores \(E = m_b e_b\). If the weight \(W = m_0 g\) and the lift-to-drag ratio stay constant, it lasts \(t = E/P_{\text{bat}}\) and carries the aircraft \(R = Vt\):

Range and endurance of a battery-electric aircraft

\[ R = \frac{e_b\,\eta}{g}\,\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{m_b}{m_0}, \qquad t = \frac{e_b\,\eta}{g}\,\frac{L}{D}\,\frac{m_b}{m_0}\,\frac{1}{V} \]

\(e_b\) in J/kg (Wh/kg times 3600), \(\eta\) from the battery to the thrust power, \(m_b/m_0\) the battery's share of the takeoff mass. No logarithm: nothing is burned, so the weight is the same at every point of the flight.

Three things follow. The range grows only linearly with the battery fraction, while a jet's grows as \(\ln[1/(1 - W_f/W_0)]\), which rises faster and faster. The speed does not appear, so the best range is wherever \(\LD\) is largest: at \(\LDmax\), not at the jet's \(0.866\,\LDmax\). And with \(\LD\) independent of altitude, flying high costs no range and gains speed; electric motors also keep their power with altitude, though their cooling air thins.

Example 3.1 — The all-electric business jet's range

The all-electric business jet carries batteries in place of its fuel, \(m_b/m_0 = 0.2867\), and flies at \(\LDmax = 17.52\) with \(\eta = 0.7076\). Find its range at 200 and at 400 Wh/kg, and the specific energy that would give 4600 km. Compare the jet's cruise range with the same fraction of fuel (Module 3: \(V = 850\) km/h, \(C = 0.65\) per hour, at \(0.866\,\LDmax = 15.17\)).

Show solution
\[ R = \frac{200(3600)(0.7076)}{9.807}(17.52)(0.2867) = 260.9\ \text{km} \]

and twice that, 521.7 km, at 400 Wh/kg. For 4600 km the specific energy would have to be

\[ e_b = 200\,\frac{4600}{260.9} = 3527\ \text{Wh/kg} \] \[ R_{\text{jet}} = \frac{850}{0.65}(15.17)\ln\frac{1}{1 - 0.2867} = 6702\ \text{km} \]

(the jet's cruise alone; Module 3 kept part of its fuel for the climb, the loiter and the reserve). The electric version flies 4% as far, and would need batteries 18 times better than today's best packs, still under a third of jet fuel's 12 000 Wh/kg.

Speed for best range and best endurance

Module 5's parabolic polar puts \(\LDmax\) at \(\CL^* = \sqrt{\CDz/K}\), the speed for minimum drag:

\[ V_{md} = \sqrt{\frac{2W}{\rho S\,\CL^*}} \]

For the longest time aloft the battery should give the least power, \(WV/(\eta\,L/D)\), which is least where \(\CL^{3/2}/C_D\) is largest: at \(\CL = \sqrt3\,\CL^*\), where \(L/D = 0.866\,\LDmax\) and the speed is \(V_{mp} = V_{md}/3^{1/4} = 0.760\,V_{md}\). The endurance also falls as the speed rises, so, unlike a jet's, a battery aircraft's endurance is best at low altitude, where the air is dense and \(V_{mp}\) small.

Example 3.2 — How fast, and how long?

The all-electric business jet weighs 6943 kg with \(S = 28\) m² and \(\CL^* = 0.538\). Find its best-range speed at 12 km (\(\rho = 0.3108\) kg/m³) and how long its 260.9 km takes; its range at Mach 0.80, where its \(L/D\) at takeoff weight is only 14.37; and its endurance at 12 km and at sea level.

Show solution
\[ V_{md} = \sqrt{\frac{2(6943)(9.807)}{0.3108(28)(0.538)}} = 170.5\ \text{m/s}\ (\text{Mach } 0.578) \] \[ t = \frac{260\,900}{170.5} = 25.5\ \text{min} \] \[ R_{M0.80} = 260.9\,\frac{14.37}{17.52} = 214.0\ \text{km} \]

For endurance, \(V_{mp} = 0.760(170.5) = 129.6\) m/s at 12 km and \(0.760(85.9) = 65.3\) m/s at sea level, with \(L/D = 15.17\):

\[ t_{12\,\text{km}} = \frac{200(3600)(0.7076)(15.17)(0.2867)}{9.807(129.6)} = 1744\ \text{s} = 0.484\ \text{h} \] \[ t_{\text{SL}} = 0.961\ \text{h} \]

Flying at the jet's Mach 0.80 would cost 18% of the range. And the battery would keep the business jet aloft for under an hour even at sea level: the 45-minute reserve of Module 2 alone needs most of it, which Lesson 4 counts.

Battery range against fuel range

Dividing the battery range by Breguet's at the same mass fraction shows what limits electric aircraft. With \(V/C\) written through the overall efficiency of Lesson 1, \(V/C = \eta_0 h/g\), Breguet's range is \(R = (\eta_0 h/g)(L/D)\ln[1/(1 - m_f/m_0)]\), so

\[ \frac{R_{\text{bat}}}{R_{\text{fuel}}} = \frac{e_b\,\eta}{h\,\eta_0}\;\frac{(L/D)_{\text{bat}}}{(L/D)_{\text{fuel}}}\;\frac{m_b/m_0}{\ln[1/(1 - m_f/m_0)]} \]

For the business jet: \(\frac{200}{12\,000}\cdot\frac{0.7076}{0.2968}\cdot\frac{17.52}{15.17}\cdot\frac{0.2867}{0.3378} = 0.0389\). The specific energy costs a factor of 60, the better chain gives back 2.4, the better \(\LD\) 1.15 and the missing logarithm 0.85. Nothing in the airframe can make up the first factor; only a better store, or a shorter mission, can.

Key takeaways

  • Battery range: \(R = (e_b\eta/g)(L/D)(m_b/m_0)\). No logarithm, because the weight does not change.
  • Best range at \(\LDmax\) and \(V_{md}\); best endurance at minimum power, \(V_{mp} = 0.760\,V_{md}\), \(L/D = 0.866\,\LDmax\), and best at low altitude.
  • The all-electric business jet flies 260.9 km at 200 Wh/kg (214 km at Mach 0.80) against 4600 km required: it would need 3527 Wh/kg.
  • Against fuel, specific energy costs a factor of 60 that the electric chain's efficiency recovers only 2.4 times.
