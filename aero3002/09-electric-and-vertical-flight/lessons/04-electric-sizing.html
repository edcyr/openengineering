Sizing an Electric Aircraft

Lesson 3 kept the business jet's takeoff weight and asked how far its batteries would carry it. Design runs the other way: given the payload, the range and the reserves, how heavy must the aircraft be? This lesson puts the battery into Module 3's sizing equation, finds the battery wall where the answer runs away, and asks what an electric business jet would change elsewhere in the design.

Learning objectives

  • Write the sizing equation of a battery-electric aircraft and solve it for the takeoff weight.
  • Compute the battery fraction for a range and for a timed reserve, and an electric empty-weight fraction.
  • Explain the battery wall, and find the specific energy a mission needs.
  • Say what an electric design changes in the wing loading, the layout and the landing gear.

The sizing equation with a battery

Module 3 split the takeoff weight into crew and payload, empty weight and fuel. An electric aircraft has a battery instead of fuel:

Sizing a battery-electric aircraft

\[ W_0 = \frac{W_{\text{crew}} + W_{\text{payload}}}{1 - \dfrac{W_e}{W_0} - \dfrac{W_b}{W_0}}, \qquad \frac{W_b}{W_0} = \frac{g\,R}{e_b\,\eta\,\LDmax} + \frac{g\,V_{mp}\,t_{\text{res}}}{e_b\,\eta\,(0.866\,\LDmax)} \]

The first term carries the range \(R\) (Lesson 3), the second a reserve of \(t_{\text{res}}\) flown at the best-endurance speed \(V_{mp}\). At a given wing loading both depend on the mission and the battery, not on \(W_0\).

As in Module 3, \(W_0\) appears on both sides through the empty-weight fraction, and the equation is solved by iteration. Start from Module 3's jet-transport trend, \(W_e/W_0 = 0.9727\,W_0^{-0.06}\), take out the jet engines (2 × 200.8 kg from Module 7, 5.78% of the takeoff weight) and add the electric drive. Take 0.30 kW of motor power per kilogram of takeoff weight, and motors with their controllers at 5 kW/kg, typical values; at 6943 kg the drive's 2083 kW weighs 417 kg, about what the engines did, so

\[ \frac{W_e}{W_0} = 0.9727\,W_0^{-0.06} - 0.0578 + \frac{0.30}{5} \]

which at 6943 kg is \(0.5721 - 0.0578 + 0.0600 = 0.5743\).

Example 4.1 — What does 6943 kg leave for the battery?

Keep the business jet's 6943 kg and 980 kg of crew and payload. How heavy a battery is left, and how far does it carry the aircraft at 200 Wh/kg, with no reserve?

Show solution
\[ \frac{W_b}{W_0} = 1 - 0.5743 - \frac{980}{6943} = 0.2846, \qquad W_b = 1976\ \text{kg} \] \[ R = \frac{200(3600)(0.7076)}{9.807}(17.52)(0.2846) = 258.9\ \text{km} \]

Slightly less than Lesson 3's 260.9 km, because the electric drive weighs 15 kg more than the engines.

Reserves

Module 2 gave the business jet a 45-minute reserve. A battery aircraft must keep that energy unused, and it flies the reserve best at its minimum-power speed, at low altitude (Lesson 3). Assume the hold is flown at 1500 m (\(\rho = 1.058\) kg/m³), where \(V_{mp} = 70.23\) m/s and \(L/D = 15.17\):

Example 4.2 — The 45-minute reserve

Find the battery fraction the 45-minute reserve needs at 200 Wh/kg, and the range that is left at 6943 kg.

Show solution
\[ \frac{W_{b,\text{res}}}{W_0} = \frac{9.807(70.23)(2700)}{200(3600)(0.7076)(15.17)} = 0.2406 \]

That is 445.5 kW for 45 minutes, 334 kWh. Of the 0.2846 available, 0.0440 is left for the range:

\[ R = 258.9\,\frac{0.0440}{0.2846} = 40.0\ \text{km} \]

The reserve takes 85% of the battery. With a 20-minute reserve (NASA's air-taxi study used 20 minutes at cruise power [8]) the fraction would be 0.1069 and the range 162 km. Reserve rules written for fuel are among the first things electric aircraft rules will have to rethink.

The battery wall

Solved for the takeoff weight, the sizing equation behaves very differently from the jet's. A jet's fuel fraction grows slowly with range, because of Breguet's logarithm and because \(C\) and \(\LD\) are good; a battery's grows in proportion to range and starts from a large reserve. When the battery and empty-weight fractions together approach one, the denominator approaches zero and \(W_0\) runs away: every kilogram of battery added to fly farther needs structure, wing and motor to carry it, which need more battery. That is the battery wall.

Example 4.3 — The specific energy the mission needs

Sized by Module 3's method with Module 7's \(\LDmax = 17.52\), the jet weighs 6666 kg for 4600 km and a 45-minute loiter. What usable specific energy would let an electric business jet fly the same mission at the same weight?

Show solution

At 6666 kg, \(W_e/W_0 = 0.9727(6666)^{-0.06} - 0.0578 + 0.0600 = 0.5757\) and the payload is \(980/6666 = 0.1470\), so the battery may be \(1 - 0.5757 - 0.1470 = 0.2773\) of \(W_0\). The range and the reserve need

\[ \frac{9.807(4.6\times10^6)}{0.7076(17.52)} = 3.640\ \text{MJ/kg} \] \[ \frac{9.807(70.23)(2700)}{0.7076(15.17)} = 0.173\ \text{MJ/kg} \]

per kilogram of \(W_0\): 3.813 MJ/kg, or 1059 Wh/kg. The battery must hold that in 0.2773 of the mass:

\[ e_b = \frac{1059}{0.2773} = 3820\ \text{Wh/kg} \]

Nineteen times the 200 Wh/kg of today's best packs, and still under a third of jet fuel's 12 000 Wh/kg: batteries good enough for the business jet's mission are not in sight.

Example 4.4 — A mission batteries can fly

Size an electric business jet for 300 km and a 20-minute reserve at NASA's projected 400 Wh/kg.

Show solution
\[ \frac{W_b}{W_0} = \frac{9.807(300\,000)}{400(3600)(0.7076)(17.52)} + 0.0535 = 0.1649 + 0.0535 = 0.2183 \]

Iterating \(W_0 = 980/(1 - W_e/W_0 - 0.2183)\) from 6943 kg settles at \(W_0 = 5000\) kg (\(W_e/W_0 = 0.5856\)), with a 1092-kg, 437-kWh battery: lighter than the jet, for a fifteenth of its range. Electric aircraft close only when the mission is short, which is why the first ones are trainers, commuters and air taxis.

What an electric design changes

Making the business jet electric would not stop at its weight. Following the design loop of Modules 2 to 8:

  • Wing loading (Module 5). The landing constraint was set at 0.9 \(W_0\), because a jet lands lighter. An electric aircraft lands at \(W_0\), so its wing loading must come down by 10%, from 250 to 225 kg/m², and its wing grow from 28 to 30.9 m² at the same weight.
  • Power (Module 5). The constraint diagram is drawn in power loading; motors keep their power with altitude, so climb and ceiling constraints ease, but the battery's C-rate (Lesson 2) may set the takeoff power.
  • Layout and tanks (Modules 6 and 7). A 2000-kg battery fills about 1 m³; it may go in the wing, as the fuel did, to relieve the wing's bending, but it brings cooling, firewalls and high-voltage cabling with it.
  • Landing gear (Module 8). The gear lands at the takeoff weight every time.
  • Empty weight. The jet-transport trend of Module 3 was fitted to jets. How much an electric aircraft's empty weight differs is one of the largest uncertainties here: Lesson 6's cooling and the battery's packaging are not in it.

Each of these would send the design around the loop again; none of them moves the battery wall, which comes from the specific energy.

Key takeaways

  • \(W_0 = W_{\text{pay}}/(1 - W_e/W_0 - W_b/W_0)\), with the battery fraction \(gR/(e_b\eta\,\LDmax)\) plus a reserve flown at minimum power.
  • At 6943 kg and 200 Wh/kg the business jet's battery gives 258.9 km; its 45-minute reserve alone takes 0.2406 of \(W_0\), leaving 40 km.
  • The battery wall: as the fractions approach one, \(W_0\) runs away. The business jet's full mission at its own weight would need 3820 Wh/kg.
  • Short missions close: 300 km with a 20-minute reserve at 400 Wh/kg gives a 5000-kg aircraft.
