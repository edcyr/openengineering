VTOL Configurations and the Business Jet

There are many ways to lift an aircraft vertically and then fly it like an airplane, and each trades hover power against cruise speed and weight. This lesson surveys the configurations, draws four vertical-takeoff versions of the business jet and sizes their rotors, measures what the vertical-lift system costs in takeoff weight or in range, and reads the rules under which such aircraft are being certified.

Learning objectives

  • Compare the VTOL configurations by disk loading, hover power and cruise speed.
  • Size the rotors or fans of a VTOL variant and find its hover power.
  • Find the takeoff weight, or the range, of a design with an added vertical-lift system.
  • State what EASA's SC-VTOL-01 and the FAA's powered-lift rule cover, and whether the business jet falls under them.

The configurations

A VTOL aircraft must lift its weight with powered thrust and, if it is to fly far, carry it on a wing in cruise. The configurations differ in how the thrust that lifts it becomes the thrust that drives it.

Four VTOL business jets

Figure 8.1 gives the business jet four ways to take off vertically, each with Lesson 7's momentum theory at sea level: a tiltrotor at 800 N/m², lift+cruise with eight lift rotors at 13.1 lb/ft² (627 N/m², as on NASA's battery-powered lift+cruise concept [8]) and the jet engines kept for cruise, four lift fans at 10 000 N/m², and lift jets at 100 000 N/m². Each variant's download and figure of merit, given in the figure, are typical values.

Example 8.1 — The lift+cruise business jet

Eight lift rotors at 627 N/m², a download of 5% and \(FM = 0.74\). Find the rotors' diameter and the hover power at sea level.

Show solution
\[ T = 1.05(68\,090) = 71.49\ \text{kN} \] \[ A = \frac{71\,490}{627} = 114.0\ \text{m}^2 \] \[ D = \sqrt{\frac{4(114.0/8)}{\pi}} = 4.26\ \text{m} \] \[ v_h = \sqrt{\frac{627}{2(1.225)}} = 16.00\ \text{m/s}, \qquad P = \frac{71\,490(16.00)}{0.74} = 1.546\ \text{MW} \]

Less power than the tiltrotor (1.805 MW), because the rotors are more lightly loaded and nothing sits under them. But eight 4.3-m rotors on booms are carried all the way to the destination doing nothing, and their 1.5 MW must come from somewhere: a battery that Lesson 2 would size by power, or the jet engines through generators.

What the vertical-lift system costs

Whatever lifts the business jet adds to its empty weight: rotors, gearboxes and shafts or motors, booms or tilt mechanisms, and engines sized for hover rather than cruise. NASA's concepts put 11 to 18% of their gross weight into their rotors and drive systems alone (Table 8.2, [8]). Call the added fraction \(f_v\) and put it into Module 3's sizing equation:

Sizing with a vertical-lift system

\[ W_0 = \frac{W_{\text{crew}} + W_{\text{payload}}}{1 - \dfrac{W_e}{W_0} - \dfrac{W_f}{W_0} - f_v} \]

\(W_e/W_0\) and \(W_f/W_0\) are the jet's, from Module 3's trend and mission; \(f_v\) is the vertical-lift system's share of the takeoff weight.

Example 8.2 — A tiltrotor's 12%

With Module 3's method and Module 7's \(\LDmax = 17.52\), the jet sizes to 6666 kg with \(W_f/W_0 = 0.2795\). Add \(f_v = 0.12\) for a tiltrotor system. Find the new takeoff weight; then, keeping 6943 kg instead, find the range that is left.

Show solution

The denominator is \(1 - 0.9727\,W_0^{-0.06} - 0.2795 - 0.12\). At 6666 kg it would be \(1 - 0.5735 - 0.3995 = 0.0270\), giving 36 000 kg; iterated, the empty-weight fraction falls as the aircraft grows and the loop settles at

\[ W_0 = 16\,869\ \text{kg}, \qquad \frac{W_e}{W_0} = 0.5425 \]

two and a half times the jet. Keeping 6943 kg instead, the fuel fraction left is \(1 - 0.5721 - 0.12 - 980/6943 = 0.1667\); Module 3's mission fractions then give a cruise fraction of 0.9076 and

\[ R = \frac{850}{0.65}(15.17)\ln\frac{1}{0.9076} = 1924\ \text{km} \]

A vertical-lift system of 12% of the weight costs the business jet either 150% in takeoff weight or 60% of its range. At \(f_v = 0.15\) the 4600-km mission needs 24.5 t, and the curve is turning almost vertical.

Speed

The vertical-lift system also limits how fast the aircraft can cruise. A tiltrotor's proprotors are large for propellers, and their tips meet the air at the helical Mach number of Module 7, \(\sqrt{M^2 + (V_{\text{tip}}/a)^2}\). NASA's concepts hover at a tip speed of 550 ft/s (167.6 m/s) [8]. Even slowed to 80% of that in cruise, at the business jet's Mach 0.80 and 12 km (\(a = 295.1\) m/s):

\[ M_{\text{tip}} = \sqrt{0.80^2 + \left(\frac{0.8(167.6)}{295.1}\right)^2} = 0.92 \]

deep into compressibility losses. Tiltrotors cruise far slower than jets, and the tiltrotor business jet would give up its Mach 0.80. Lift+cruise and lift-fan designs keep the jet's cruise, at the price of carrying a lift system that does nothing in cruise; jet lift keeps it at the price of ten times the hover power. No configuration gives the business jet both vertical takeoff and its mission at its weight: the requirement itself, from Module 2, would have to change.

Certifying VTOL and powered-lift aircraft

EASA's SC-VTOL-01 (issue 1, 2 July 2019) [5] is a special condition for person-carrying VTOL aircraft in the small category, "with lift/thrust units used to generate powered lift and control" and more than two lift/thrust units used to provide lift in vertical takeoff or landing. It is built on CS-23 Amendment 5 with elements of CS-27, because EASA found most of the 150-odd configurations it had reviewed fit neither the aeroplane nor the rotorcraft rules. Its scope (VTOL.2000 and 2005):

  • a passenger seating configuration of 9 or fewer and a maximum certified takeoff mass of 3175 kg (7000 lb) or less;
  • unpressurized aircraft, with \(V_{NO}\) or \(V_{MO} \le 250\) KCAS or \(M_{MO} \le 0.6\);
  • two categories: Enhanced, able to continue safe flight and landing after a failure, required for commercial air transport of passengers and for flight over congested areas; and Basic, able to make a controlled emergency landing.

It also asks for independent energy storage systems (VTOL.2430, Lesson 2) and, for Category Enhanced, the safety objectives of large aircraft and rotorcraft. The business jet at 6943 kg, pressurized and cruising at Mach 0.80 is outside it on three counts.

In the United States, the FAA's powered-lift rule (published 21 November 2024, effective 21 January 2025) [6] integrates powered-lift into the airspace. 14 CFR Part 1 defines powered-lift as heavier-than-air aircraft capable of vertical takeoff, vertical landing and low-speed flight that depend principally on engine-driven lift devices or engine thrust in those regimes and on nonrotating airfoils in horizontal flight; the rule's preamble notes that tiltrotors, lift+cruise designs and vectored-thrust aircraft like the AV-8 Harrier all meet it. The rule set no new airworthiness standards: powered-lift are type-certificated as a special class under 14 CFR 21.17(b). It requires a type rating for each powered-lift a pilot flies and adds a Special Federal Aviation Regulation, SFAR No. 120 (new Part 194), for training and operations. For fuel reserves, a powered-lift may use helicopter minimums only if it can land in the vertical-lift mode along its whole route; otherwise it must meet the airplane minimums.

Around the design loop

Each of these excursions would send the business jet back through the course:

  • Requirements (Module 2): vertical takeoff and electric power both need a shorter range or a slower cruise to close.
  • Sizing (Module 3): \(f_v\) and the battery fraction enter the sizing equation, and \(W_0\) grows fast.
  • Constraints (Module 5): hover power, hot and high, becomes the propulsion's sizing case, far above any airplane constraint.
  • Layout, engines and gear (Modules 6 to 8): rotor clearance and booms, turboshafts or motors in place of turbofans, a landing gear for vertical landings at full weight.
  • Drag, loads, weights and stability (Modules 10 to 13): stopped rotors and booms add drag; rotor and hover loads reach the structure; the weight equations must take the lift system; and hover and transition need control power where the tail has no airspeed.

Modules 10 to 14 carry on with the conventional business jet as Module 8 left it. The electric, hybrid and VTOL versions in these lessons are excursions: they show what such a change would cost, and the numbers in Modules 10 to 14 do not include them.

Key takeaways

  • VTOL configurations run from helicopters (low disk loading, slow) to jet lift (very high disk loading, fast).
  • The business jet's hover needs 1.5 MW with lift rotors, 1.8 as a tiltrotor, 6.0 with lift fans and 17.5 with lift jets.
  • A vertical-lift system of 12% of the weight grows the business jet from 6666 to 16 869 kg, or cuts its range at 6943 kg to 1924 km.
  • A tiltrotor's tips would pass Mach 0.9 at the jet's cruise; lift+cruise and jet lift keep the speed but carry dead weight or burn far more fuel.
  • EASA's SC-VTOL-01 covers VTOL aircraft up to 9 passengers and 3175 kg, unpressurized and below Mach 0.6; the FAA certifies powered-lift as a special class and adds SFAR No. 120 for pilots and operations.
