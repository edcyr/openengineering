Lesson 2 · 30 min

Batteries

A kilogram of jet fuel holds 12 kWh; a kilogram of the best aircraft battery pack holds about a sixtieth of that, and not all of it can be used. This lesson follows a battery from the cell to the pack, compares it with the business jet's fuel, and shows that an aircraft battery must deliver power as well as store energy: for takeoff, the power can set its size.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish specific energy, energy density and specific power, at the cell and at the pack.
  • Compute a pack's usable specific energy from the cell's, the usable share of the charge and the pack's overhead.
  • Compare the energy, and the useful work, that the business jet's fuel and an equal mass of battery hold.
  • Compute a battery's C-rate and size a battery by its energy or by its power, whichever governs.

Specific energy and energy density

A battery's specific energy \(e_b\) is the energy it stores per unit mass (Wh/kg), its energy density the energy per unit volume (Wh/L), and its specific power the power it can deliver per unit mass (kW/kg). Fuel is compared on the same terms through its heating value: Jet A-1's 43.2 MJ/kg (Module 7) is \(43.2\times10^6/3600 = 12\,000\) Wh/kg, and at 800 kg/m³ it holds 9600 Wh/L.

From the cell to the usable pack

Cells are rated at their full charge, but an aircraft cannot use all of it. Charging to 100% and draining to 0% shortens a cell's life, and near empty its voltage falls fast, so the power it can give drops. A pack also needs structure, cooling, wiring, contactors and a battery management system, none of which store energy. NASA's air-taxi study [8] took cells rated at 650 Wh/kg, left 5% of the charge unused at the top and 15% at the bottom, and added 30% to the cells' mass for the pack, which gave 400 Wh/kg:

The usable specific energy of a pack

\[ e_b = \frac{e_{\text{cell}}\,f_{\text{use}}}{k_{\text{pack}}} = \frac{650(0.80)}{1.30} = 400\ \text{Wh/kg} \]

\(f_{\text{use}}\) is the usable share of the rated charge (its depth of discharge), \(k_{\text{pack}}\) the pack's mass over its cells' mass. In these lessons \(e_b\) always means the usable energy of the pack, per kilogram of pack.

Example 2.1 — A cell for 200 Wh/kg

With NASA's usable share and pack overhead, what must the cells be rated at for a pack to deliver 200 Wh/kg?

Show solution
\[ e_{\text{cell}} = \frac{e_b\,k_{\text{pack}}}{f_{\text{use}}} = \frac{200(1.30)}{0.80} = 325\ \text{Wh/kg} \]

At the top of what the best cells available today reach (Table 2.1): 200 Wh/kg usable at the pack is optimistic, not conservative.

Fuel against batteries

The business jet carries \(W_f = 1990.6\) kg of fuel (Module 3), in tanks of 2.49 m³ (Module 7). Figure 2.2 puts the same mass of battery beside it, both as stored energy and as useful work, the stored energy times the efficiency of the chain that turns it into thrust power (Lesson 1).

Example 2.2 — The business jet's fuel as batteries

Compare the energy and the useful work in the business jet's 1990.6 kg of fuel and in the same mass of battery at 200 Wh/kg.

Show solution
\[ E_f = 1990.6(12\,000) = 23\,887\ \text{kWh}, \qquad E_b = 1990.6(200) = 398.1\ \text{kWh} \]

The useful work is the stored energy times the chain's efficiency:

\[ \text{fuel: } 23\,887(0.2968) = 7090\ \text{kWh} \] \[ \text{battery: } 398.1(0.7076) = 281.7\ \text{kWh} \]

The fuel stores 60 times as much energy, and even after the electric chain's better efficiency it does 25 times as much work. The battery would also fill about 1.0 m³ at 400 Wh/L, so volume is not its problem; mass is.

Power, C-rate and heat

A battery must also deliver its energy fast enough. The C-rate is the power drawn over the energy stored, \(C = P/E\) (per hour): at 1C a full battery empties in an hour, at 4C in 15 minutes. High C-rates heat the cells and age them: today's cells are limited to about 2C "without excessive heating or cell damage", and 2–3C is a reasonable expectation for the near term [8]. The heat is the battery's loss, \((1 - \eta_{\text{bat}})\) of the power, and it must be removed while the cells stay below about 60 °C.

Battery sized by energy or by power

\[ C = \frac{P}{E}, \qquad m_b = \max\!\left(\frac{E_{\text{need}}}{e_b},\ \frac{P_{\max}}{C_{\max}\,e_b}\right) \]

The first term carries the energy the mission needs, the second delivers the largest power at no more than the allowed C-rate. Short flights at high power are sized by power.

Example 2.3 — Can the battery take off?

The all-electric business jet has 2083 kW of motor shaft power (0.30 kW per kg of takeoff weight, Lesson 4), and the 398.1 kWh battery of Example 2.2. Find the battery power and the C-rate at takeoff and in cruise (936.8 kW, Lesson 1), and the battery mass needed to take off at no more than 3C.

Show solution
\[ P_{\text{bat}} = \frac{2083}{0.95(0.98)(0.95)} = 2355\ \text{kW}, \qquad C = \frac{2355}{398.1} = 5.92 \] \[ \text{cruise: } C = \frac{936.8}{398.1} = 2.35 \] \[ E = \frac{P_{\text{bat}}}{C_{\max}} = \frac{2355}{3} = 785\ \text{kWh}, \qquad m_b = \frac{785\,000}{200} = 3925\ \text{kg} \]

Takeoff asks almost 6C of the battery, three times what today's cells allow, and even cruise is above 2C. To take off at 3C the battery would need 785 kWh and 3925 kg, twice the fuel's mass, whatever the range. The heat at takeoff is 5% of 2355 kW, 118 kW, in the cells alone.

Living with a battery

Three more properties shape an aircraft battery. A lithium-ion cell that overheats can enter thermal runaway and set its neighbors alight, so packs are divided and the divisions protected: EASA's special condition for VTOL aircraft, SC-VTOL-01, asks that energy storage systems be independent, so that a failure "including fire" of one component in one system does not lose the others (VTOL.2430 of [5]). Cells lose capacity with every cycle and with heat, so an aircraft sized with new cells must still fly its mission with old ones. And a battery does not get lighter as it empties: the aircraft lands as heavy as it took off, which Lesson 3 shows changes the range equation and Module 8's landing gear must allow for.

Key takeaways

  • Usable pack specific energy: \(e_b = e_{\text{cell}} f_{\text{use}}/k_{\text{pack}}\); NASA's projected 650 Wh/kg cells give 400 Wh/kg [8], and 200 Wh/kg needs 325 Wh/kg cells.
  • Jet fuel holds 12 000 Wh/kg: the business jet's fuel stores 60 times the energy of the same mass of battery and does 25 times the work.
  • C-rate is power over energy; cells are limited to about 2–3C. The all-electric business jet would need about 6C to take off, so its battery is sized by power.
  • Batteries need cooling, protection against thermal runaway, margins for aging, and land as heavy as they took off.
