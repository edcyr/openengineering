Lesson 7 · 35 min

Vertical Flight

To hover, an aircraft must push down a column of air fast enough to make a thrust equal to its weight. Pushing a large mass of air slowly costs far less power than pushing a small mass of air fast, so the size of the rotor, fan or jet decides the power, and with it almost everything else about a vertical-takeoff aircraft. This lesson builds the momentum theory of hover and applies it to a business jet that takes off vertically.

Learning objectives

  • Derive the induced velocity and ideal power of a hovering actuator disk.
  • Use disk loading, power loading, the figure of merit and download to find a VTOL aircraft's hover power and rotor size.
  • Compute the power for vertical climb and for hover in hot, high conditions.
  • Compare rotors, fans and jets across the disk-loading spectrum.

The actuator disk

Model the rotor as a thin disk of area \(A\) that adds momentum to the air passing through it. In hover the air above is still; it reaches the disk at the induced velocity \(v_i\) and leaves far below at \(w\). The mass flow is \(\dot m = \rho A v_i\), the thrust is the momentum it gains, \(T = \dot m w\), and the power is the kinetic energy it gains, \(P = \tfrac12\dot m w^2\). The power is also the thrust times the velocity at the disk, \(P = T v_i\), so \(w = 2v_i\): the slipstream doubles its speed below the disk and contracts to half the disk's area. Then \(T = 2\rho A v_i^2\), and

Momentum theory of hover

\[ v_h = \sqrt{\frac{T}{2\rho A}}, \qquad P_{\text{ideal}} = T v_h = \frac{T^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2\rho A}}, \qquad P = \frac{P_{\text{ideal}}}{FM} \]

\(T/A\) is the disk loading. The figure of merit \(FM\) is the ideal power over the real one: it takes in the blades' profile drag, tip losses and swirl. NASA's six air-taxi concepts have figures of merit of 0.68 to 0.74 [8].

Disk loading and power loading

The power per unit of thrust is \(P/T = v_h/FM\), and the thrust per unit of power, the power loading, its inverse:

\[ \frac{T}{P} = \frac{FM}{v_h} = FM\sqrt{\frac{2\rho}{T/A}} \]

Doubling the disk loading costs 41% more power for the same thrust. Helicopters, with large, lightly loaded rotors, hover on the least power; a jet, whose "disk" is its small exhaust, on the most. The thrust a VTOL aircraft must make is more than its weight: part of the slipstream strikes the wing or fuselage below the rotor, the download, and there must be a margin for control and for climbing.

Example 7.1 — A tiltrotor business jet in hover

Give the business jet (6943 kg) two tilting proprotors loaded at 800 N/m² (16.7 lb/ft²), a download of 10% of its weight and \(FM = 0.75\). Find the rotor diameter and the hover power at sea level.

Show solution
\[ T = 1.10(6943)(9.807) = 74.90\ \text{kN} \] \[ A = \frac{74\,900}{800} = 93.62\ \text{m}^2 \] \[ D = \sqrt{\frac{4(93.62/2)}{\pi}} = 7.72\ \text{m} \] \[ v_h = \sqrt{\frac{800}{2(1.225)}} = 18.07\ \text{m/s} \] \[ P_{\text{ideal}} = 74\,900(18.07) = 1.353\ \text{MW} \] \[ P = \frac{1.353}{0.75} = 1.805\ \text{MW} \]

Two 7.7-m rotors need 1.8 MW: 41.5 N of thrust per kilowatt. In cruise at 12 km the jet's engines deliver about 1.06 MW of thrust power; hover needs nearly twice that as shaft power, at sea level.

Vertical climb and hot, high hover

Climbing vertically at \(V_c\), the air arrives at the disk with \(V_c\) already, so the induced velocity falls but the useful power \(TV_c\) is added:

\[ v_i = -\frac{V_c}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{V_c^2}{4} + v_h^2}, \qquad P = \frac{T\,(V_c + v_i)}{FM} \]

For slow climbs, \(P \approx P_h + TV_c/(2FM)\): only half the climb power appears as extra power. The hover power grows as \(1/\sqrt\rho\), so a hot day or a high landing site raises it, and it is the hot, high case that usually sizes a VTOL aircraft's engines or motors.

Example 7.2 — Climbing, and on a hot day

For Example 7.1's tiltrotor, find the power to climb vertically at 500 ft/min (2.54 m/s) at sea level, and to hover at 1500 m on a day 20 K hotter than standard (\(\rho = 0.9872\) kg/m³).

Show solution
\[ v_i = -1.27 + \sqrt{1.27^2 + 18.07^2} = 16.84\ \text{m/s} \] \[ P = \frac{74\,900(2.54 + 16.84)}{0.75} = 1.936\ \text{MW} \] \[ P_{\text{hot}} = 1.805\sqrt{\frac{1.225}{0.9872}} = 2.010\ \text{MW} \]

The climb adds 7%, the hot, high site 11%. The engines must also keep a margin if one fails: Lesson 8's certification rules set how much.

Jet lift

A jet engine lifting vertically is the same momentum machine with a very small disk. Its exhaust leaves at \(V_j\), so it gives the air the power \(\tfrac12 T V_j\); an exhaust at about 400 m/s is an actuator disk with \(v_h = 200\) m/s, a disk loading near 100 000 N/m². That is why jet-lift aircraft such as the Harrier and the F-35B need a thrust-to-weight ratio above one, burn fuel fast in hover, and direct hot gas at the ground that erodes surfaces and can be re-ingested.

Example 7.3 — Rotors or jets, and the fuel to hover

With lift jets at 100 000 N/m², a download of 2% and an efficiency of 0.80 in place of the figure of merit, the business jet's hover needs 17.5 MW (Lesson 8's jet-lift variant). Compare the fuel for two minutes of hover with the tiltrotor's 1.805 MW, both from fuel at a thermal efficiency of 0.30 (Lesson 1).

Show solution
\[ m_f = \frac{P\,t}{\eta_{th}\,h}: \qquad \frac{1.805\times10^6(120)}{0.30(43.2\times10^6)} = 16.7\ \text{kg}, \qquad \frac{17.5\times10^6(120)}{0.30(43.2\times10^6)} = 162\ \text{kg} \]

Ten times the fuel, and ten times the installed power. For the tiltrotor, two minutes of hover burn less than 1% of the business jet's fuel: for air taxis too, the hover energy is small beside the cruise energy [8]. Hover's cost is the power it demands, and the mass of what delivers it.

For an electric VTOL aircraft hover sets the battery's power, not its energy. NASA's battery-powered lift+cruise concept, with lift rotors at 13.1 lb/ft² (627 N/m²), draws its battery at 2.4C in hover against 0.7C in cruise [8]: Lesson 2's C-rate limit, not the energy, is what high disk loading strains.

Key takeaways

  • \(v_h = \sqrt{T/(2\rho A)}\), \(P_{\text{ideal}} = Tv_h\), \(P = P_{\text{ideal}}/FM\): the slipstream doubles its speed and halves its area below the disk.
  • Power loading \(T/P = FM/v_h\) falls as \(\sqrt{T/A}\): rotors hover on far less power than fans, and fans than jets.
  • A tiltrotor business jet at 800 N/m² needs two 7.7-m rotors and 1.8 MW to hover, 2.0 MW hot and high.
  • Jet lift at 100 000 N/m² needs about ten times the power and fuel; hover energy is small, hover power is what costs.
