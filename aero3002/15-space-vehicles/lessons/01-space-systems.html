From Aircraft to Space Vehicles

Fourteen modules have taken a business jet from a requirement to a closed design. A space vehicle is designed by the same method, starting from a mission, sizing with a few governing equations, then closing a loop of weights and performance. But the numbers are far harsher. This lesson sets out what a space system is, how much energy an orbit takes, and how a space project is organized and kept within its mass.

Learning objectives

  • Name the segments of a space system and the parts of a spacecraft, and state a mission as the requirement for a launch vehicle.
  • Compute the specific energy of an orbit and compare it with an aircraft's cruise.
  • List how launch vehicle design differs from aircraft design: energy, structure, safety factors, rules and reuse.
  • Place conceptual design in the NASA life cycle, and use technology readiness levels and mass margins.

The mission and the space system

An aircraft program starts from a request for proposal, as in Module 2. A space program starts from a mission: what must be done in space, for whom, for how long, and in which orbit. The mission is carried out by a space system, which has several segments:

  • the space segment: the spacecraft, made of the payload (the camera, the radio or the instrument that does the mission) and the bus that supports it (structure, power, thermal control, attitude control, propulsion, communications and the computer);
  • the launch segment: the launch vehicle and the launch site that put the spacecraft in its orbit;
  • the ground segment and operations: the stations, the people and the software that command the spacecraft and receive its data.

Space mission engineering, from the mission to the segments and their trades, is treated in full in [2]. The segments trade against each other. A heavier spacecraft needs a larger launcher, and a lower orbit puts more mass in orbit but shortens the spacecraft's life. For the launcher, the mission is a requirement with three main numbers: a payload mass, a target orbit, and a launch site. The lessons follow a small two-stage launcher that must put a 300 kg spacecraft into a 500 km circular sun-synchronous orbit from a site at 45° N. That class of mission is common for Earth-observation and communication smallsats. The launcher is a hypothetical design, not a real vehicle.

The energy of an orbit

The business jet cruises at Mach 0.80 at 12 km, at 236 m/s. Each kilogram of it carries a kinetic energy \(v^2/2\) and a potential energy \(gh\):

\[ \frac{v^2}{2} = \frac{236.1^2}{2} = 0.0279\ \text{MJ/kg}, \qquad gh = 9.81(12\,000) = 0.1177\ \text{MJ/kg} \]

In orbit, gravity weakens with distance, so the potential energy relative to the surface is \(\mu/R_E - \mu/r\), with \(\mu = GM\) Earth's gravitational parameter, \(R_E\) its radius and \(r\) the distance from its center. Lesson 2 shows that a circular orbit needs a speed \(v = \sqrt{\mu/r}\).

Specific energy of a circular orbit, relative to Earth's surface

\[ e = \frac{v^2}{2} + \left(\frac{\mu}{R_E} - \frac{\mu}{r}\right) = \frac{\mu}{2r} + \frac{\mu}{R_E} - \frac{\mu}{r} \]

with \(\mu = 3.986\times10^{14}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}^2\) and \(R_E = 6378\ \text{km}\). Earth's rotation is left out here; Lesson 4 adds it.

Example 1.1 — A 500 km orbit against the business jet's cruise

Find the specific energy of a 500 km circular orbit and compare it with the business jet's in cruise.

Show solution
\[ r = 6378 + 500 = 6878\ \text{km}, \qquad v = \sqrt{\frac{3.986\times10^{14}}{6.878\times10^6}} = 7613\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \frac{v^2}{2} = 28.98\ \text{MJ/kg}, \qquad \frac{\mu}{R_E} - \frac{\mu}{r} = 62.50 - 57.95 = 4.54\ \text{MJ/kg} \] \[ e = 28.98 + 4.54 = 33.52\ \text{MJ/kg} \]

That is 230 times the business jet's 0.146 MJ/kg, and almost all of it is speed: only 14% is height. Orbit is a matter of going fast, not of going high.

A jet engine takes its oxygen from the air. A rocket carries its own oxygen, so most of what it burns is oxidizer. Lesson 5 shows that the rocket equation turns this energy into a propellant fraction of more than 90%.

The table explains most of this module. With no wing to multiply the thrust, a launcher must reach orbital speed by burning propellant. The rocket equation makes that propellant grow exponentially with the speed. Every kilogram of structure therefore costs far more than on an aircraft, which is why launchers are built with thin walls and small safety factors, and verified by tests. A launch is also hazardous to people who did not choose to be near it, so it is regulated by the risk it puts on the public rather than by certifying the vehicle.

The handbook stresses that the analysis and optimization of systems engineering have their largest effect in the early stages [8]. That is where conceptual design works, and it is why the methods of this course are worth learning.

Technology readiness

A technology readiness level (TRL) measures how mature a technology is. TRLs range from 1, basic technology research, to 9, systems test, launch and operations; typically a technology must reach TRL 6, demonstrated in a relevant environment, before it is integrated into a design [8]. Two rules from the same handbook matter most in conceptual design:

  • A system's TRL is the lowest TRL of its parts: one immature component holds back the whole vehicle.
  • A component with a flight history (heritage) is no longer at TRL 9 if it is used in a new environment or a new architecture.

Margins

The handbook [8] defines a margin as an allowance carried in the budget, the schedule and the technical parameters, such as weight, power or memory, to account for uncertainties and risks. Margins are allocated early and are consumed as the project goes on. NASA's systems engineering requirements (NPR 7123.1) make mass margins and power margins minimum technical performance measures that every project with hardware must track.

For a launcher, the mass margin is the most important one. A spacecraft that grows from 280 kg to 320 kg no longer fits a launcher sized for 300 kg. Lesson 5 shows what each extra kilogram costs at liftoff: about 42 kg for the small launcher, against about 5.7 kg on the business jet in Module 3. Lesson 5 sizes the launcher for a payload of exactly 300 kg; a real project would hold a margin on top of that and track it as the spacecraft's design matured.

Key takeaways

  • A space system has a space segment (payload and bus), a launch segment and a ground segment. For the launcher, the mission sets a payload, an orbit and a site.
  • A 500 km orbit takes 33.5 MJ/kg relative to the surface, 230 times the business jet's cruise, and 86% of it is speed.
  • Launchers carry their oxidizer, have no \(L/D\), and are built light with factors near 1.4 verified by tests. Each launch is licensed by its risk to the public.
  • NASA projects run from Pre-Phase A to Phase F between Key Decision Points; conceptual design is Pre-Phase A and Phase A.
  • A system's TRL is its lowest element's, and TRL 6 is typically needed before integration. Mass and power margins are tracked through the project.
