Rocket Engines and Propellants

The specific impulse in the rocket equation comes from two places: the propellants, which set how much energy each kilogram releases and how light the exhaust gases are, and the nozzle, which turns that energy into speed. This lesson takes the specific impulse apart, sizes the small launcher's engines, and compares the propellants a designer can choose, which differ as much in density as in performance.

Learning objectives

  • Apply the thrust equation and relate thrust, mass flow, exhaust velocity and specific impulse.
  • Split the specific impulse into the characteristic velocity and the thrust coefficient, and size a throat.
  • Explain how the expansion ratio and the ambient pressure change the thrust, and why first and upper stages use different nozzles.
  • Compare propellants by specific impulse, mixture ratio and bulk density, and name the engine cycles.

The thrust equation

A rocket engine burns its propellants in a chamber at a pressure \(p_c\) and expands the hot gas through a converging-diverging nozzle to a high speed. The momentum it ejects each second, plus the pressure difference across the nozzle's exit, is the thrust:

Thrust and specific impulse

\[ T = \dot m\,v_e + (p_e - p_a)A_e = \dot m\,c, \qquad I_{sp} = \frac{T}{\dot m\,g_0} = \frac{c}{g_0} \]

\(v_e\) is the exhaust speed at the exit, \(p_e\) and \(A_e\) the exit pressure and area, \(p_a\) the ambient pressure, and \(c\) the effective exhaust velocity of Lesson 5.

For a given engine, the mass flow and the exit pressure are fixed by the chamber, so only the term \(-p_aA_e\) changes with altitude. The thrust grows as the air thins, up to its vacuum value \(T_{\text{vac}} = T + p_aA_e\).

The characteristic velocity and the thrust coefficient

The thrust can be written as the product of the chamber pressure, the throat area \(A_t\) and a thrust coefficient \(C_F\) that measures how well the nozzle expands the gas. The mass flow through the choked throat is \(\dot m = p_cA_t/c^*\), where the characteristic velocity \(c^*\) measures the combustion and depends almost only on the propellants:

Splitting the specific impulse

\[ T = C_F\,p_c\,A_t, \qquad c = c^*C_F, \qquad I_{sp} = \frac{c^*C_F}{g_0} \] \[ C_F = \sqrt{\frac{2\gamma^2}{\gamma - 1}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma + 1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma + 1}{\gamma - 1}}\left[1 - \left(\frac{p_e}{p_c}\right)^{\frac{\gamma - 1}{\gamma}}\right]} + \left(\frac{p_e}{p_c} - \frac{p_a}{p_c}\right)\frac{A_e}{A_t} \]

\(\gamma\) is the gas's ratio of specific heats. The expansion ratio \(\varepsilon_e = A_e/A_t\) fixes \(p_e/p_c\) through the isentropic area-ratio relation. These are the ideal-rocket relations [3, 4]; real engines reach about 95% of the ideal values, which the \(c^*\) used below takes in.

Example 6.1 — The first stage's engine

The first stage has nine identical engines, each giving 17.81 kN at sea level (160.3 kN in all, \(T/W = 1.30\)). They burn LOX/RP-1 with \(c^* = 1760\) m/s and \(\gamma = 1.24\) (typical values) at \(p_c = 8\) MPa, with \(\varepsilon_e = 14\). The area ratio gives \(p_e/p_c = 0.00714\), so \(p_e = 57.1\) kPa. Find the specific impulses and the throat and exit areas.

Show solution

The square root evaluates to 1.6552, so in vacuum

\[ C_{F,\text{vac}} = 1.6552 + 0.00714(14) = 1.7552 \] \[ I_{sp,\text{vac}} = \frac{1760(1.7552)}{9.80665} = 315.0\ \text{s} \] \[ C_{F,\text{SL}} = 1.7552 - \frac{101\,325}{8\times10^6}(14) = 1.5779 \] \[ I_{sp,\text{SL}} = 283.2\ \text{s} \] \[ A_t = \frac{T}{C_Fp_c} = \frac{17\,810}{1.5779(8\times10^6)} = 1.411\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2 \] \[ A_e = 14A_t = 0.01975\ \text{m}^2 \]

The throat is 42 mm across and the exit 159 mm. Each engine burns \(\dot m = 17\,810/(283.2 \cdot 9.807) = 6.41\) kg/s, and gives 19.82 kN in vacuum.

Expansion and altitude

A longer nozzle (a larger \(\varepsilon_e\)) expands the gas to a lower exit pressure and a higher speed, which raises the vacuum thrust. In the atmosphere, though, the term \(-p_aA_e\) grows with the exit area. At sea level, a nozzle that expands far below ambient pressure (overexpanded) loses thrust, and its flow can separate from the wall, with violent side loads. A common rule of thumb puts separation where \(p_e\) falls below about 0.4 \(p_a\). A first stage therefore uses a moderate expansion ratio, a compromise over its climb. An upper stage, which fires only in vacuum, uses a large one.

Example 6.2 — The first stage at 11 km, and the upper stage's nozzle

(a) What is the first-stage engine's specific impulse at 11 km, where \(p_a = 22.63\) kPa? (b) The second stage uses the same chamber with \(\varepsilon_e = 85\), for which \(p_e/p_c = 6.71\times10^{-4}\). Find its vacuum specific impulse.

Show solution
\[ \text{(a)}\quad C_F = 1.7552 - \frac{22\,632}{8\times10^6}(14) = 1.7156 \] \[ I_{sp} = \frac{1760(1.7156)}{9.80665} = 307.9\ \text{s} \] \[ \text{(b)}\quad C_{F,\text{vac}} = 1.8921 \] \[ I_{sp,\text{vac}} = \frac{1760(1.8921)}{9.80665} = 339.6\ \text{s} \]

The upper-stage nozzle gains 25 s in vacuum, 8%, the value of 340 s used in Lesson 5. At sea level its exit pressure of 5.4 kPa would be far below the separation limit: it could not be fired there.

Propellants

The propellant sets \(c^*\), and with it the specific impulse; it also sets the density, and with it the size of the tanks. A mixture ratio \(O/F\) of oxidizer to fuel by mass, chosen near the best specific impulse, gives the bulk density of the pair:

\[ \rho_{\text{bulk}} = \frac{1 + O/F}{(O/F)/\rho_O + 1/\rho_F} \]

Densities used: LOX 1141, RP-1 810, methane 423, LH2 71, NTO 1440 and MMH 875 kg/m³. A high specific impulse saves propellant mass, while a high density saves tank volume and structure. Hydrogen has the best specific impulse, but its tanks hold 2.8 times the volume of kerosene's for the same propellant mass, and must be insulated. Hydrogen therefore pays best in upper stages, where the specific impulse matters most and the tanks are smaller. Dense propellants pay in first stages, which must also push through the air.

Engine cycles

Liquid propellants must be fed into a chamber at more than its pressure. The ways of doing it are the engine's cycle:

  • Pressure-fed: gas pressure in the tanks pushes the propellants. It is simple, but the tanks must hold the chamber pressure, so the chamber pressure is low and the tanks heavy. It suits small upper stages and spacecraft thrusters.
  • Gas generator: a small flow of propellant burns to drive the turbopumps and is dumped overboard. This is the common cycle of kerosene first stages, and it loses a few percent of specific impulse.
  • Staged combustion: the turbine exhaust goes into the main chamber, so no propellant is wasted. It allows high chamber pressures, at the cost of complexity.
  • Expander: hydrogen, heated in the cooling jacket, drives the turbine. It is clean, but limited in thrust.
  • Electric pump-fed: battery-powered electric motors drive the pumps. It is simple, and suits small engines whose batteries are a small share of their mass.

An engine's thrust-to-weight ratio (vacuum thrust over its weight on Earth) sets the engine mass in Lesson 8. Lesson 8 takes 70 for the first-stage engines and 40 for the upper stage with its large nozzle, typical of small pump-fed engines; large engines reach well above 100.

Key takeaways

  • \(T = \dot mv_e + (p_e - p_a)A_e = \dot mc\), and \(I_{sp} = c/g_0\). Vacuum thrust is \(T + p_aA_e\).
  • \(I_{sp} = c^*C_F/g_0\): \(c^*\) from the propellants, \(C_F\) from the nozzle. \(T = C_Fp_cA_t\) sizes the throat.
  • The first-stage engine (\(\varepsilon_e = 14\), 8 MPa) gives 283 s at sea level and 315 s in vacuum. The upper-stage version (\(\varepsilon_e = 85\)) gives 340 s but could not fire at sea level.
  • Hydrogen has the best \(I_{sp}\) but a third of kerosene's density. Dense propellants suit first stages and high \(I_{sp}\) suits upper stages.
