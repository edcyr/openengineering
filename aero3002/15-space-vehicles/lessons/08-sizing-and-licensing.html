Sizing the Launcher and Licensing It

Lesson 5 sized the small launcher with assumed structural coefficients, as Module 3 sized the business jet with an assumed empty-weight fraction. This lesson closes the loop. It estimates each stage's inert mass from its tanks, engines and equipment, resizes until the masses agree, and draws the result. It then turns to what a launcher needs before it may fly: a license, which judges the risk the launch puts on the public.

Learning objectives

  • Set out a launcher's sizing loop and estimate a stage's inert mass from the bottom up.
  • Close the loop on the structural coefficients and find how the liftoff mass grows with the payload.
  • State the public-safety criteria of a launch license under 14 CFR Part 450, and when flight abort is needed.
  • Explain the license's rules for collision avoidance and for the disposal of an upper stage, and size a disposal burn.

The launcher's design loop

The inert mass from the bottom up

Estimate each stage's inert mass from five parts. The tanks and engines follow from Lessons 6 and 7; take the other parts as allowances typical of a small launcher:

  • Tanks: wall area times thickness times density, with the thickness the larger of the pressure's (Lesson 7, protoflight factors) and the minimum gauge (1.5 mm on the first stage, 1.2 mm on the second). The result is multiplied by 1.6 for stiffeners, insulation and fittings. The second stage's tanks share a common bulkhead.
  • Engines: vacuum thrust over \(g_0\) times the engines' thrust-to-weight ratio, 70 for the first stage and 40 for the second.
  • Residual propellant: 1% of the propellant, left unburned in the tanks and lines.
  • Structure, avionics and pressurization: the thrust structure, intertank, interstage, wiring, computers and helium, as 250 kg on the first stage and 15 kg on the second.
  • Payload fairing: 50 kg, carried by the second stage until it is jettisoned (counted to burnout here, which is conservative).

Example 8.1 — Checking Lesson 5's structural coefficients

Lesson 5's stages carry 9709.9 and 1546.1 kg of propellant, with vacuum thrusts of 178.4 and 17.8 kN. The tanks of Lesson 7 have wall areas of 35.6 and 6.7 m², and every wall is at its minimum gauge. Estimate each stage's inert mass and structural coefficient.

Show solution

First stage, in aluminium 2219 (2840 kg/m³):

\[ m_{\text{tanks}} = 35.6(0.0015)(2840)(1.6) = 242.7\ \text{kg} \] \[ m_{\text{eng}} = \frac{178\,400}{9.807(70)} = 259.9\ \text{kg} \] \[ m_{s1} = 242.7 + 259.9 + 97.1 + 250 = 849.7\ \text{kg} \] \[ \varepsilon_1 = \frac{849.7}{849.7 + 9709.9} = 0.0805 \]

Second stage:

\[ m_{\text{tanks}} = 36.7\ \text{kg}, \qquad m_{\text{eng}} = \frac{17\,810}{9.807(40)} = 45.4\ \text{kg} \] \[ m_{s2} = 36.7 + 45.4 + 15.5 + 15 + 50 = 162.6\ \text{kg}, \qquad \varepsilon_2 = 0.0952 \]

The first stage is close to its assumed 0.08. The second is lighter than its assumed 0.10, so the launcher can shrink.

Feeding the new coefficients back into the staging and repeating (steps 4 to 8) converges in about 20 turns, with each change damped to 70%. The loop settles at \(\varepsilon_1 = 0.0824\) and \(\varepsilon_2 = 0.0920\). The first stage's coefficient rises a little because it shrinks while its fixed allowance of 250 kg does not. The closed launcher weighs 12 087 kg at liftoff, 485 kg less than Lesson 5's:

Because the allowances do not grow with the vehicle, the closed launcher grows more slowly than in proportion to its payload. Thirty more kilograms of payload (10%) cost 754 kg at liftoff (6.2%), where Lesson 5's fixed coefficients predicted 10%. As in Module 3, the structural fractions are not constants of the design, and a sizing method must say how they change.

What the loop has not yet included

This is one turn of a conceptual design. The second stage's trajectory and its losses were assumed rather than simulated, and the first stage was flown in a simple gravity turn. The fairing and the payload's environment (acoustics, shock, the 6 g at burnout) were not designed. The cost was not estimated, and its model would favor fewer, simpler engines. A preliminary design would simulate the whole ascent with an optimized steering program, recompute the losses, and go around the loop again, as Module 14 did for the business jet's drag and weights.

Licensing the launch

An airliner is certified: its type design is shown to meet airworthiness standards such as 14 CFR Part 25 (Module 2). In the United States a commercial launch is licensed instead. 14 CFR Part 450 "prescribes requirements for obtaining and maintaining a license to launch, reenter, or both" (450.1) [7]. The license does not certify that the vehicle will work. It requires the operator to show that the launch's risk to people and property who have not chosen to accept it stays below set limits. Its safety criteria, in 450.101 [7], include:

Table 8.2 Launch risk criteria of 14 CFR 450.101(a), from liftoff to orbital insertion

Source: [7], § 450.101(a).

The risks count inert and explosive debris, toxic release and far-field blast overpressure. 450.101(c) [7] adds protection against a high consequence event. In every phase of flight the operator must either use flight abort as a hazard control (450.108), or show that the consequence of any reasonably foreseeable failure is no more than \(1\times10^{-3}\) conditional expected casualties, or show sufficient demonstrated reliability. The public must also be told of the regions that contain, with 97% probability, all the debris from normal flight events, such as spent stages and the fairing.

Example 8.2 — Is flight abort needed?

Suppose a risk analysis of the small launcher's flight finds that a failure during its first 30 s has a probability of 0.5%. If it happened, the debris near the site would cause 0.004 expected casualties. The rest of the flight contributes \(3\times10^{-5}\) expected casualties. Does the launch meet 450.101?

Show solution
\[ E_C = 0.005(0.004) + 3\times10^{-5} = 2\times10^{-5} + 3\times10^{-5} = 5\times10^{-5} \lt 1\times10^{-4} \]

The collective risk passes. But the conditional expected casualties of a failure in the first 30 s, 0.004, exceed \(1\times10^{-3}\). So unless the vehicle has enough demonstrated reliability, which a new launcher does not, that phase needs flight abort: a system that ends the flight before the vehicle can endanger the public (450.108). A low average risk does not excuse a high consequence.

Collision avoidance and disposal

Part 450 also protects people and property in orbit [7]. Under 450.169, the operator must set launch window closures so that the launcher, anything it sheds and its payload keep clear of orbiting objects during the screening time. That time runs from 150 km up to orbital insertion, and for at least 3 hours from liftoff. For inhabitable objects the probability of collision must not exceed \(1\times10^{-6}\), or the vehicle must keep 200 km away (or 200 km in-track and 50 km cross-track and radially). For other active objects the limits are \(1\times10^{-5}\) or 25 km. For tracked debris (radar cross sections above 0.1 m²) they are \(1\times10^{-5}\) or 2.5 km.

At the end of the launch, 450.171 [7] requires that any stage left in orbit must not contact its payload after separation, and must not break up from its stored energy. It must be passivated: residual propellant depleted with the valves left open, pressurized systems vented, batteries left permanently discharged. The small launcher's second stage reaches 500 km with its payload, where drag would take years to bring it down. The cleaner choice is to deorbit it with a final burn that lowers its perigee into the atmosphere, then passivate it.

Example 8.3 — Deorbiting the second stage

After releasing the spacecraft, the second stage (167.0 kg inert) fires retrograde at 500 km to lower its perigee to 50 km. Find the \(\Delta v\) and the propellant (\(I_{sp} = 340\) s) it must keep in reserve.

Show solution
\[ a = \frac{6878 + 6428}{2} = 6653\ \text{km} \] \[ v_a = \sqrt{\mu\left(\frac{2}{6.878\times10^6} - \frac{1}{6.653\times10^6}\right)} = 7482.8\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \Delta v = 7612.7 - 7482.8 = 129.8\ \text{m/s} \] \[ m_p = 167.0\left(e^{129.8/3334} - 1\right) = 6.6\ \text{kg} \]

Six and a half kilograms, about 0.4% of the stage's propellant, which a real design would add to the second stage's budget. The burn also depletes most of the residual propellant before passivation.

Key takeaways

  • A launcher's loop runs mission → \(\Delta v\) → staging → engines → tanks → inert masses → new \(\varepsilon\), until the coefficients agree with the masses.
  • Bottom-up (tanks at minimum gauge ×1.6, engines by their \(T/W\), 1% residuals, allowances) the small launcher closes at \(\varepsilon = 0.0824\) and \(0.0920\), and 12 087 kg at liftoff. Fixed allowances make it grow less than in proportion to the payload.
  • A launch is licensed, not certified. Under 450.101: \(E_C \le 10^{-4}\), \(P_C \le 10^{-6}\) per launch, aircraft \(10^{-6}\). Flight abort (or demonstrated reliability) is needed where a failure's conditional expected casualties exceed \(10^{-3}\).
  • 450.169 keeps the launch clear of orbiting objects; 450.171 requires passivation of any stage left in orbit. Deorbiting the second stage costs 130 m/s, 6.6 kg of propellant.
