The Rocket Equation and Staging

The rocket equation plays the role for launchers that Breguet's range equation played for the business jet. It turns a \(\Delta v\) into a mass ratio. Its exponential makes reaching orbit with one stage nearly impossible, and it explains why every launcher drops its empty tanks on the way up. This lesson derives it, splits the small launcher's 9.20 km/s between two stages in the lightest way, and finds the launcher's masses.

Learning objectives

  • Derive the rocket equation and compute mass ratios and propellant fractions.
  • Define the structural coefficient and the payload ratio, and find the most \(\Delta v\) one stage can give.
  • Explain why launchers stage, and compute the payload fraction of identical stages.
  • Split the \(\Delta v\) between unequal stages optimally with a Lagrange multiplier, and find each stage's mass.

The rocket equation

A rocket of mass \(m\) ejects propellant at the rate \(\dot m\) with an effective exhaust velocity \(c\) relative to itself. With no gravity or drag, its momentum balance over a time \(dt\) is \(m\,dv = -c\,dm\). Integrating from the initial mass \(m_0\) to the final mass \(m_f\) gives the rocket equation, published by Tsiolkovsky in 1903 [5] and derived in Chapter 13 of [1]:

The rocket equation

\[ \Delta v = c\ln\frac{m_0}{m_f}, \qquad c = I_{sp}\,g_0, \qquad \frac{m_0}{m_f} = e^{\Delta v/c} \]

\(I_{sp}\) is the specific impulse in seconds and \(g_0 = 9.80665\ \text{m/s}^2\). Gravity and drag appear as the losses of Lesson 4, already in the \(\Delta v\).

Compare Breguet's range for a jet, \(R = (V/C)(L/D)\ln(W_0/W_1)\), from Module 3. Both are logarithms of a mass ratio, because both vehicles burn the mass they carry. But the jet multiplies its fuel's effect by \(L/D\) and by \(V/C\), roughly 15 and 1300 km for the business jet, so it needs to burn less than a third of its weight. The rocket has nothing to multiply with: it must reach 9.2 km/s with exhaust velocities of about 3 km/s.

Example 5.1 — The mass ratio of orbit

What mass ratio and propellant fraction does 9199 m/s need with \(I_{sp} = 340\) s, a good kerosene engine in vacuum?

Show solution
\[ c = 340(9.80665) = 3334\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \frac{m_0}{m_f} = e^{9199/3334} = e^{2.759} = 15.79 \] \[ \frac{m_p}{m_0} = 1 - \frac{1}{15.79} = 0.937 \]

94% of the vehicle must be propellant, leaving 6% for the tanks, the engines and the payload together.

One stage

Divide a stage's mass into propellant \(m_p\), structure and engines (its inert mass) \(m_s\), and the payload \(m_{pl}\) it carries. Two ratios describe it:

Structural coefficient and payload ratio

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{m_s}{m_s + m_p}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{m_{pl}}{m_s + m_p}, \qquad \Delta v = c\ln\frac{1 + \lambda}{\varepsilon + \lambda} \]

With no payload (\(\lambda = 0\)) a stage reaches its largest \(\Delta v\), \(c\ln(1/\varepsilon)\).

Example 5.2 — Can one stage reach orbit?

A stage has \(\varepsilon = 0.08\) and an average \(I_{sp}\) of 320 s. What is the most \(\Delta v\) it can give?

Show solution
\[ \Delta v_{\max} = 320(9.80665)\ln\frac{1}{0.08} = 3138(2.526) = 7926\ \text{m/s} \]

Even empty, it falls 1.3 km/s short of the 9.2 km/s to orbit. A single stage to orbit needs a much lower structural coefficient, a much higher specific impulse, or both: hydrogen (450 s) with \(\varepsilon = 0.10\) gives 10.16 km/s, with almost nothing left for payload.

Why stage

Once most of a stage's propellant has burned, it carries large, nearly empty tanks and engines sized for liftoff. Dropping them and continuing with a smaller stage means the later \(\Delta v\) no longer has to accelerate that dead mass. Each stage's payload is everything above it. For \(N\) identical stages that share the \(\Delta v\) equally, each has the mass ratio \(n = e^{\Delta v/(Nc)}\), and the payload fraction is

\[ \frac{m_{pl}}{m_0} = \left(\frac{1 - n\varepsilon}{n(1 - \varepsilon)}\right)^N \]

Example 5.3 — Two and three identical stages

With \(I_{sp} = 320\) s and \(\varepsilon = 0.08\) for every stage, find the payload fraction for 9199 m/s with two and with three stages.

Show solution
\[ N = 2: \quad n = e^{9199/(2 \cdot 3138)} = 4.331 \] \[ \frac{m_{pl}}{m_0} = \left(\frac{1 - 0.3465}{4.331(0.92)}\right)^2 = 0.1640^2 = 0.0269 \] \[ N = 3: \quad n = e^{9199/(3 \cdot 3138)} = 2.657 \] \[ \frac{m_{pl}}{m_0} = \left(\frac{1 - 0.2126}{2.657(0.92)}\right)^3 = 0.3221^3 = 0.0334 \]

Two stages reach orbit with 2.7% payload where one stage cannot reach it at all. A third stage adds a quarter more payload, but also another set of engines, another separation and more that can fail. Most launchers to low orbit use two stages; three are common for solid-propellant launchers and for higher orbits.

Unequal stages: the optimal split

Real stages differ. A first stage works in the atmosphere at a lower average \(I_{sp}\); an upper stage has a vacuum nozzle but a heavier structure for its size. The \(\Delta v\) should then be split so that the liftoff mass is least. With a Lagrange multiplier \(\eta\) on the \(\Delta v\) constraint (Chapter 13 of [1]), each stage's mass ratio at the optimum is

The optimal staging

\[ n_i = \frac{c_i\eta - 1}{c_i\varepsilon_i\eta}, \qquad \sum_i c_i\ln n_i = \Delta v \]

Solve the second equation for \(\eta\) (by trial or bisection), then build the vehicle from the top down. Each stage's propellant, for a mass \(m\) above it, is \(m_p = m(n - 1)(1 - \varepsilon)/(1 - n\varepsilon)\), and its inert mass is \(m_s = m_p\,\varepsilon/(1 - \varepsilon)\).

Example 5.4 — The small launcher's stages

The first stage burns kerosene and oxygen at an average \(I_{sp}\) of 300 s over its ascent (283 s at sea level, 315 s in vacuum) with \(\varepsilon_1 = 0.08\). The second has \(I_{sp} = 340\) s and \(\varepsilon_2 = 0.10\), including the payload fairing. Size them for 300 kg and 9199 m/s. Take both structural coefficients as first estimates; Lesson 8 checks them.

Show solution

With \(c_1 = 2942\) and \(c_2 = 3334\) m/s, the multiplier that satisfies the \(\Delta v\) is \(\eta = 5.2406\times10^{-4}\) s/m:

\[ n_1 = \frac{2942\eta - 1}{2942(0.08)\eta} = 4.3925 \] \[ n_2 = \frac{3334\eta - 1}{3334(0.10)\eta} = 4.2770 \] \[ \Delta v_1 = 2942\ln 4.3925 = 4354\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \Delta v_2 = 3334\ln 4.2770 = 4846\ \text{m/s} \]

Second stage, with the 300 kg payload above it:

\[ m_{p2} = \frac{300(3.2770)(0.90)}{1 - 0.4277} = 1546.1\ \text{kg} \] \[ m_{s2} = 171.8\ \text{kg}, \qquad m_{02} = 2017.8\ \text{kg} \]

First stage, with the 2017.8 kg second stage above it:

\[ m_{p1} = \frac{2017.8(3.3925)(0.92)}{1 - 0.3514} = 9709.9\ \text{kg} \] \[ m_{s1} = 844.3\ \text{kg} \] \[ m_0 = 2017.8 + 9709.9 + 844.3 = 12\,572\ \text{kg} \]

The payload fraction is 2.39%. The upper stage, with the better \(I_{sp}\), takes the larger share of the \(\Delta v\).

What each kilogram costs

Because every stage's masses scale with what it carries, the liftoff mass is proportional to the payload: \(m_0/m_{pl} = 12\,572/300 = 41.9\) kilograms at liftoff per kilogram of payload, against 5.7 for the business jet (Module 3). The other sensitivities, from resizing with the optimal split, show where engineering effort pays:

A hundredth of structural coefficient on the upper stage, about 17 kg of structure, costs 815 kg at liftoff. Every kilogram on the upper stage must be carried by the first stage as well. This is the launcher's equivalent of the business jet's empty-weight trend, and the reason the structures of Lesson 7 are built so thin.

Key takeaways

  • \(\Delta v = c\ln(m_0/m_f)\), with \(c = I_{sp}g_0\): orbit at 340 s needs a mass ratio of 15.8, 94% propellant.
  • One stage gives at most \(c\ln(1/\varepsilon)\): 7.93 km/s at 320 s and \(\varepsilon = 0.08\), short of orbit.
  • Staging drops empty tanks and engines: two identical stages give a payload fraction of 2.7%, three 3.3%.
  • The optimal split has \(n_i = (c_i\eta - 1)/(c_i\varepsilon_i\eta)\): the small launcher weighs 12 572 kg (9710 + 844 kg first stage, 1546 + 172 kg second stage), 41.9 kg per kilogram of payload.
