Orbital Maneuvers

A spacecraft changes its orbit by firing its engine, and what that costs is the change of velocity, \(\Delta v\). It is the currency of space missions: every maneuver has a price in \(\Delta v\), and the rocket equation turns the total into propellant. This lesson prices the standard maneuvers (raising an orbit, changing its plane, catching up with a target) and adds them into a budget.

Learning objectives

  • Compute the two burns and the time of flight of a Hohmann transfer between circular orbits.
  • Compute the \(\Delta v\) of a plane change, and explain why plane changes are made at low speed and combined with other burns.
  • Design a phasing orbit to move a spacecraft along its orbit.
  • Build a \(\Delta v\) budget and estimate the propellant it needs.

Impulsive burns

Most orbital maneuvers last minutes, against orbits that last hours, so they are treated as impulsive: the velocity changes by \(\Delta\vec v\) at one point, and the spacecraft then follows the new orbit through that point. A burn along the direction of motion (prograde) adds energy and raises the far side of the orbit; a retrograde burn lowers it; a burn across the plane turns the plane. The cost of a maneuver is the magnitude \(\Delta v = |\Delta\vec v|\), whatever the spacecraft's mass. Chapter 6 of [1] develops all the maneuvers of this lesson.

The Hohmann transfer

To go from a circular orbit of radius \(r_1\) to a larger one of radius \(r_2\) in the same plane, the Hohmann transfer uses an ellipse that touches both: its perigee is at \(r_1\) and its apogee at \(r_2\). One prograde burn at \(r_1\) puts the spacecraft on the ellipse; half an orbit later, a second prograde burn at \(r_2\) makes the orbit circular.

The Hohmann transfer

\[ a_t = \frac{r_1 + r_2}{2}, \qquad \Delta v_1 = \sqrt{\mu\left(\frac{2}{r_1} - \frac{1}{a_t}\right)} - \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_1}} \] \[ \Delta v_2 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_2}} - \sqrt{\mu\left(\frac{2}{r_2} - \frac{1}{a_t}\right)}, \qquad t_{\text{transfer}} = \pi\sqrt{\frac{a_t^3}{\mu}} \]

Each burn is the difference between a circular speed and the transfer ellipse's speed at the same radius (vis-viva, Lesson 2).

Example 3.1 — Raising the spacecraft from 500 to 700 km

The small launcher's spacecraft must later climb from its 500 km orbit to 700 km. Find the two burns and the time it takes.

Show solution
\[ r_1 = 6878\ \text{km}, \quad r_2 = 7078\ \text{km}, \qquad a_t = 6978\ \text{km} \] \[ v_{c1} = 7612.7\ \text{m/s} \] \[ v_p = \sqrt{3.986\times10^{14}\left(\frac{2}{6.878\times10^6} - \frac{1}{6.978\times10^6}\right)} = 7667.0\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \Delta v_1 = 54.4\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \Delta v_2 = v_{c2} - v_a = 7504.4 - 7450.4 = 54.0\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \Delta v = 108.3\ \text{m/s} \] \[ t = \pi\sqrt{\frac{(6.978\times10^6)^3}{3.986\times10^{14}}} = 2900\ \text{s} = 48.3\ \text{min} \]

Small changes of altitude in low orbit are cheap: about 0.54 m/s per kilometer.

Example 3.2 — From low orbit to geostationary orbit

Find the Hohmann transfer from a 300 km parking orbit to geostationary orbit (35 786 km), in the equatorial plane.

Show solution
\[ r_1 = 6678\ \text{km}, \quad r_2 = 42\,164\ \text{km}, \qquad a_t = 24\,421\ \text{km} \] \[ \Delta v_1 = 10\,151.6 - 7725.8 = 2425.8\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \Delta v_2 = 3074.7 - 1607.8 = 1466.8\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \Delta v = 3893\ \text{m/s}, \qquad t = 18\,990\ \text{s} = 5.28\ \text{h} \]

Reaching geostationary orbit from low orbit costs almost 3.9 km/s, about half of what it takes to reach low orbit from the ground (Lesson 4).

The Hohmann transfer is the cheapest two-burn transfer between coplanar circles. For a very large ratio \(r_2/r_1\), above about 12, a three-burn bi-elliptic transfer that first climbs beyond \(r_2\) can cost less, at the price of a much longer flight (a classical result; see Chapter 6 of [1]).

Plane changes

To turn an orbit's plane by an angle \(\Delta i\) without changing its speed, the burn must rotate the velocity vector. The \(\Delta v\) is the chord of the isosceles triangle with two sides \(v\):

Plane change, and a combined burn

\[ \Delta v = 2v\sin\frac{\Delta i}{2}, \qquad \Delta v_{\text{comb}} = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 - 2v_1v_2\cos\Delta i} \]

The combined burn changes the speed from \(v_1\) to \(v_2\) and the plane by \(\Delta i\) at once (the law of cosines).

At 500 km a plane change of just 1° costs \(2(7613)\sin 0.5^\circ = 132.9\) m/s, more than the whole climb of Example 3.1. Plane changes are expensive because orbital speeds are large. They should therefore be made where the speed is lowest, at the apogee of an ellipse, and combined with a burn that is needed anyway. Better still, the launch should put the spacecraft into the right plane directly (Lesson 4).

Example 3.3 — Geostationary orbit from a 28.5° parking orbit

A launch from 28.5° latitude leaves the spacecraft in a 300 km orbit inclined at 28.5°. Compare three ways to reach geostationary orbit: change the plane in low orbit and then transfer; transfer and then change the plane at GEO; or transfer and combine the plane change with the second burn.

Show solution

Plane change in low orbit, then the Hohmann transfer of Example 3.2:

\[ 2(7725.8)\sin 14.25^\circ = 3803\ \text{m/s}, \qquad \Delta v = 3803 + 3893 = 7696\ \text{m/s} \]

Hohmann transfer, then a plane change at GEO speed:

\[ 2(3074.7)\sin 14.25^\circ = 1514\ \text{m/s}, \qquad \Delta v = 3893 + 1514 = 5406\ \text{m/s} \]

Hohmann transfer, with the plane change combined with the second burn at apogee (\(v_1 = v_a = 1607.8\) m/s):

\[ \Delta v_2 = \sqrt{1607.8^2 + 3074.7^2 - 2(1607.8)(3074.7)\cos 28.5^\circ} = 1830\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \Delta v = 2426 + 1830 = 4256\ \text{m/s} \]

The combined burn at apogee saves 3.4 km/s against the plane change in low orbit. Launching from a site near the equator saves more still: the spacecraft then needs little or no plane change.

Phasing

Two spacecraft in the same orbit stay the same angle apart. To move one of them along the orbit, for a rendezvous or to space out a constellation, it enters a phasing orbit with a different period. It then returns to the burn point after one or more revolutions and resumes the original orbit. To gain an angle \(\Delta\theta\) on the original orbit in one revolution, the phasing orbit's period must be \(T_{ph} = T_0(1 - \Delta\theta/360^\circ)\): shorter, so the phasing orbit is lower.

Example 3.4 — Moving 10° ahead

A spacecraft in the 500 km circular orbit (\(T_0 = 94.61\) min) must move 10° ahead in one revolution. Find the phasing orbit and the total \(\Delta v\).

Show solution
\[ T_{ph} = 94.61\left(1 - \frac{10}{360}\right) = 91.99\ \text{min} \] \[ a_{ph} = \left(\mu\left(\frac{T_{ph}}{2\pi}\right)^2\right)^{1/3} = 6750\ \text{km} \]

The burn point stays the apogee (6878 km), so the perigee is at \(2(6750) - 6878 = 6622\) km, an altitude of 244 km.

\[ v_a = \sqrt{\mu\left(\frac{2}{6.878\times10^6} - \frac{1}{6.750\times10^6}\right)} = 7540.2\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \Delta v = 2(7612.7 - 7540.2) = 145.0\ \text{m/s} \]

A retrograde burn enters the phasing orbit, and a prograde burn of the same size leaves it. Spreading the phasing over several revolutions needs a smaller change of period and less \(\Delta v\).

The \(\Delta v\) budget

A mission's \(\Delta v\) budget lists every maneuver with its \(\Delta v\), plus a margin, and is kept like a mass budget (Lesson 1). The rocket equation of Lesson 5, \(m_p = m_0(1 - e^{-\Delta v/c})\) with \(c\) the engine's exhaust velocity, gives the propellant. Take the budget of Table 3.1 for the launcher's 300 kg spacecraft. Key takeaways

  • A maneuver's cost is its \(\Delta v\). A Hohmann transfer uses two prograde burns on an ellipse tangent to both circles: 108 m/s from 500 to 700 km, 3.89 km/s from 300 km to GEO.
  • A plane change costs \(2v\sin(\Delta i/2)\): 133 m/s per degree at 500 km. Make it at low speed and combine it with another burn.
  • A phasing orbit with period \(T_0(1 - \Delta\theta/360^\circ)\) moves a spacecraft ahead along its orbit: 145 m/s for 10° in one revolution at 500 km.
  • A \(\Delta v\) budget lists every maneuver plus a margin; the rocket equation turns it into propellant.
  • Next, Lesson 4 finds the largest item of all: the \(\Delta v\) to reach orbit from the ground.
