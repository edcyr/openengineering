Lesson 7 · 30 min

Launch Vehicle Structures

Lesson 5 showed that a hundredth of structural coefficient on the upper stage costs 815 kg at liftoff. Launch vehicle structures are therefore designed closer to their limits than aircraft structures. Their factors of safety are smaller, and every design is verified by test. This lesson finds the small launcher's loads, applies NASA's factors of safety, and sizes the wall of its first-stage oxygen tank for pressure and for buckling.

Learning objectives

  • Name the main loads on a launch vehicle, and find its maximum dynamic pressure and axial acceleration.
  • Apply the design and test factors of NASA-STD-5001B, compare them with an aircraft's, and compute margins of safety.
  • Size a propellant tank's wall for its pressure with the membrane stresses.
  • Check a tank in axial compression for buckling with a knockdown factor.

The loads on a launcher

Where an aircraft is sized by maneuvers and gusts (Module 11), a launcher is sized by:

  • Thrust and axial acceleration. Each part carries the mass above it times the acceleration. The acceleration grows as the propellant burns, to its maximum at burnout.
  • Dynamic pressure \(q = \tfrac12\rho v^2\). Its maximum, max-q, comes about a minute into flight. Winds there turn the vehicle's angle of attack into bending, and the aerodynamic heating of the fairing begins.
  • Pressure in the tanks, which carries hoop stress but also stiffens the thin walls against buckling.
  • Vibration and acoustics at liftoff and in the transonic region, and the shocks of stage and fairing separation. They usually size the payload's own structure.

At its design point the first stage reaches max-q of 30.5 kPa at 70 s, at 11.0 km and Mach 1.39, and its acceleration rises to 6.35 g at burnout. At that moment the 2017.8 kg second stage and payload press down on the first stage with \(2017.8(6.35)(9.807) = 125.7\) kN. This is the limit load at the top of the first stage, and the 300 kg spacecraft must be designed for 6.35 g along the axis too. Launchers that need a gentler ride throttle their engines or shut some of them down near burnout.

Factors of safety

The limit load is defined as in Module 11, in the words of NASA-STD-5001B [6]: "the maximum anticipated load, or combination of loads", that the structure may meet in its service life. The standard sets minimum design factors on the limit load, for yield and for ultimate strength, and test factors for the tests that verify the design. It applies to launch vehicle structures, including propellant tanks and solid rocket motor cases, and to their payloads. Its factors depend on how the structure is verified:

  • Prototype: a separate, dedicated test article identical to the flight structure is tested to ultimate load. The standard calls this the accepted practice for launch vehicles [6].
  • Protoflight: the flight structure itself is tested above limit load but below yield. It is common for spacecraft, since no test article is needed. The flight article must not be damaged in its own test, so it needs a higher yield factor.

The standard [6] defines the margin of safety on the factored limit load, for yield and for ultimate:

Margin of safety (NASA-STD-5001B)

\[ MS = \frac{\text{allowable load (yield or ultimate)}}{\text{limit load} \times \text{factor of safety (yield or ultimate)}} - 1 \ \ge 0 \]

The load may be a force, a stress or a strain. Stresses from pressure and temperature are added to the limit stress before the factor is applied.

A launcher's ultimate factor of 1.4 is 7% below an aircraft's 1.5. That is affordable because each design is tested to its factored loads, and because a launcher meets its design loads a few times rather than over tens of thousands of flights. The standard also excludes some things a designer must still supply [6]: the determination of the design loads, fracture control, and specific factors such as fitting factors, buckling knockdown factors and load uncertainty factors.

Sizing a tank for pressure

A pump-fed stage keeps its tanks at a modest ullage pressure, which feeds the pumps without cavitation and stiffens the walls. Below the liquid's surface, the axial acceleration \(n g\) adds the head \(\rho_{\text{liquid}}\,n\,g\,h\). A thin cylinder of radius \(R\) and wall \(t\) carries the pressure \(p\) as membrane stresses:

Membrane stresses and the wall a pressure needs

\[ \sigma_{\text{hoop}} = \frac{pR}{t}, \qquad \sigma_{\text{axial}} = \frac{pR}{2t} \] \[ t = \max\left(\frac{FS_u\,pR}{\sigma_u},\ \frac{FS_y\,pR}{\sigma_y}\right) \]

The hoop stress is twice the axial stress, so it sizes the cylinder. Ellipsoidal heads with a height of \(R/\sqrt2\), as in the examples, carry the same pressure with about the cylinder's wall.

Example 7.1 — The first stage's oxygen tank

The first stage's oxygen, 7013 kg at \(O/F = 2.6\), fills 6.331 m³ with 3% ullage. In a 1.2 m tank with two ellipsoidal heads, that makes a tank 5.88 m long. Take an ullage pressure of 300 kPa; the stage lifts off at 1.30 g. Size the wall in aluminium 2219-T87 (typical values \(\sigma_y = 352\), \(\sigma_u = 455\) MPa) for a protoflight approach, with a minimum gauge of 1.5 mm for handling and manufacture.

Show solution
\[ p = 300 + \frac{1141(1.30)(9.807)(5.88)}{1000} = 300 + 85.5 = 385.5\ \text{kPa} \] \[ t_u = \frac{1.4(385.5\times10^3)(0.6)}{455\times10^6} = 0.712\ \text{mm} \] \[ t_y = \frac{1.25(385.5\times10^3)(0.6)}{352\times10^6} = 0.821\ \text{mm} \]

The protoflight yield factor governs, at 0.82 mm. That is thinner than the minimum gauge, so the wall is 1.5 mm. Its margins at that thickness are:

\[ \sigma_{\text{hoop}} = \frac{385.5\times10^3(0.6)}{0.0015} = 154.2\ \text{MPa} \] \[ MS_u = \frac{455}{1.4(154.2)} - 1 = 1.11 \] \[ MS_y = \frac{352}{1.25(154.2)} - 1 = 0.83 \]

On a small launcher, pressure does not size the tanks: the minimum gauge does, and the next check, buckling, matters more.

Buckling in axial compression

A thin cylinder in axial compression buckles at a stress that classical theory puts at \(\sigma_{cr} = Et/(R\sqrt{3(1 - \nu^2)})\), about \(0.6\,Et/R\). Real shells buckle well below it, because small imperfections in their shape trigger buckling early. NASA-STD-5001B [6] requires that buckling be checked at ultimate loads, and that the analysis account for the differences between the ideal geometry and the real structure. It does so with knockdown factors, for which it refers to NASA SP-8007 [9]. For an unstiffened isotropic cylinder, the knockdown commonly taken from it is

\[ \gamma = 1 - 0.901\left(1 - e^{-\phi}\right) \] \[ \phi = \frac{1}{16}\sqrt{\frac{R}{t}} \]

For composite shells the standard cautions that SP-8007's universal knockdown of 0.65 may be unconservative.

Example 7.2 — The oxygen tank at burnout

At first-stage burnout the top of the 1.5 mm oxygen tank carries the 125.7 kN limit load of the stage above. Taking no credit for the tank's pressure, check it for buckling (take \(E = 73.8\) GPa and \(\nu = 0.33\)).

Show solution
\[ \sigma = \frac{F}{2\pi Rt} = \frac{125.7\times10^3}{2\pi(0.6)(0.0015)} = 22.2\ \text{MPa} \] \[ 1.4\sigma = 31.1\ \text{MPa} \] \[ \sigma_{cr} = \frac{73.8\times10^9(0.0015)}{0.6\sqrt{3(1 - 0.33^2)}} = 112.8\ \text{MPa} \] \[ \phi = \frac{\sqrt{400}}{16} = 1.25 \] \[ \gamma = 1 - 0.901(1 - e^{-1.25}) = 0.357 \] \[ MS = \frac{0.357(112.8)}{31.1} - 1 = \frac{40.3}{31.1} - 1 = 0.30 \]

The knockdown takes away almost two-thirds of the classical strength, and buckling, not pressure, is the tank's real design case. The tank's pressure would add stiffness. But the standard allows only the minimum expected pressure, unfactored, where a load relieves a failure mode, so it is left out here. Stiffeners (stringers or an isogrid pattern machined into the wall) raise the buckling stress far more cheaply than a thicker wall. That is why the mass estimate of Lesson 8 multiplies the bare wall's mass by 1.6.

Key takeaways

  • Launchers are sized by thrust and axial acceleration, max-q, tank pressure, and vibration and shock. The small launcher reaches 30.5 kPa at 70 s and 6.35 g at first-stage burnout.
  • NASA-STD-5001B: metallic ultimate factor 1.4, yield 1.0 (prototype) or 1.25 (protoflight); tests at 1.4 or 1.2; proof 1.05 for propellant tanks. Composites 1.4 or 1.5, 2.0 at discontinuities. Aircraft use 1.5 with far less testing.
  • \(MS = \text{allowable}/(\text{limit} \times FS) - 1\). The hoop stress \(pR/t\) sizes a tank for pressure: 0.82 mm for the oxygen tank, below the 1.5 mm minimum gauge.
  • Buckling at ultimate load with a knockdown factor (0.357 at \(R/t = 400\)) is the tank's design case, with a margin of 0.30.
