Orbits

An orbit is the path of a body that falls freely around Earth. It keeps missing the ground because it moves sideways fast enough. Two numbers, the size and the shape of the orbit, fix its speed at every point and its period. This lesson finds them, and then lets Earth's slight flattening turn the orbit's plane, which gives the sun-synchronous orbit the small launcher must reach.

Learning objectives

  • Compute the speed and period of a circular orbit from Earth's gravitational parameter.
  • Describe an elliptical orbit by its perigee and apogee, and find its speeds with the vis-viva equation.
  • Relate an orbit's energy to its semimajor axis, and its angular momentum to its speeds.
  • Explain how Earth's oblateness turns an orbit's plane, and find the inclination of a sun-synchronous orbit.

The two-body problem

Newton's law of gravitation pulls a spacecraft of mass \(m\) toward Earth's center with a force \(F = GMm/r^2\). The product \(\mu = GM\), Earth's gravitational parameter, is known far more precisely than \(G\) or \(M\) separately. Take \(\mu = 3.986\times10^{14}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}^2\) and \(R_E = 6378\) km. Treat Earth as a point mass and the spacecraft as a particle, with no other forces. This is the two-body problem, and its solutions are the conic sections: circles, ellipses, parabolas and hyperbolas, with Earth's center at a focus. Chapters 2 to 4 of [1] develop it; this lesson uses its results.

Circular orbits

In a circular orbit, gravity supplies exactly the centripetal force: \(\mu m/r^2 = mv^2/r\). The circular speed and the period follow:

Circular speed and period

\[ v_c = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}} \]

\(r = R_E + h\) is the radius of the orbit. The period holds for an ellipse too, with \(a\) its semimajor axis (Kepler's third law).

Example 2.1 — The launcher's target orbit

Find the speed and the period of a 500 km circular orbit, and how many orbits it makes in a day.

Show solution
\[ v_c = \sqrt{\frac{3.986\times10^{14}}{6.878\times10^6}} = 7613\ \text{m/s} \] \[ T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{(6.878\times10^6)^3}{3.986\times10^{14}}} = 5677\ \text{s} = 94.61\ \text{min} \]

That is \(1440/94.61 = 15.2\) orbits a day. A spacecraft in this orbit circles Earth in about an hour and a half at 27 400 km/h.

Elliptical orbits

An ellipse with Earth's center at one focus has its closest point, the perigee, at \(r_p\) and its farthest, the apogee, at \(r_a\). Its semimajor axis and eccentricity are

\[ a = \frac{r_p + r_a}{2}, \qquad e = \frac{r_a - r_p}{r_a + r_p} \]

Two quantities stay constant along the orbit: the specific energy and the specific angular momentum. Their constancy gives the speed at every radius.

Energy, angular momentum and the vis-viva equation

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{v^2}{2} - \frac{\mu}{r} = -\frac{\mu}{2a}, \qquad h = r_p v_p = r_a v_a, \qquad v = \sqrt{\mu\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right)} \]

The energy depends only on the semimajor axis. A larger orbit has more energy (less negative), even though the spacecraft moves more slowly in it.

Example 2.2 — A 500 × 2000 km orbit

A spacecraft has its perigee at 500 km and its apogee at 2000 km. Find \(a\), \(e\), its speeds at perigee and apogee, and its period.

Show solution
\[ r_p = 6878\ \text{km}, \quad r_a = 8378\ \text{km}, \qquad a = 7628\ \text{km} \] \[ e = \frac{1500}{15\,256} = 0.09832 \] \[ v_p = \sqrt{3.986\times10^{14}\left(\frac{2}{6.878\times10^6} - \frac{1}{7.628\times10^6}\right)} = 7978\ \text{m/s} \] \[ v_a = v_p\frac{r_p}{r_a} = 6550\ \text{m/s} \] \[ T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{(7.628\times10^6)^3}{3.986\times10^{14}}} = 6630\ \text{s} = 110.5\ \text{min} \]

At perigee it moves 365 m/s faster than in a 500 km circular orbit. That difference is what a burn at 500 km must add to raise the apogee to 2000 km (Lesson 3).

A geostationary orbit is circular and equatorial, with a period of one sidereal day (23.93 h, Earth's rotation against the stars). A spacecraft there stays above one point on the equator. Setting \(T = 86\,164\) s in the period formula gives its radius, 42 164 km, or 35 786 km above the equator. The orbit's inclination \(i\) is the angle between its plane and the equator. Below 90° the spacecraft moves eastward with Earth's rotation (prograde); above 90° it moves westward (retrograde).

Earth's oblateness and the sun-synchronous orbit

Earth is not a sphere. Its equator bulges, and the extra mass there pulls on an inclined orbit with a small torque that turns the orbit's plane about Earth's axis, the way a torque turns a spinning top. The line where the orbit crosses the equator going north, the ascending node, then drifts around the equator. The bulge is measured by the coefficient \(J_2 = 1.08263\times10^{-3}\), and to first order the node moves at

Regression of the node

\[ \dot\Omega = -\frac{3}{2}\,n\,J_2\left(\frac{R_E}{a(1 - e^2)}\right)^2\cos i, \qquad n = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{a^3}} \]

\(n\) is the mean motion in rad/s. For a prograde orbit (\(\cos i > 0\)) the node moves westward; for a retrograde one it moves eastward. Chapter 10 of [1] treats this and other orbital perturbations.

A space station in a 51.6° orbit at 500 km has its node move 4.75° west each day. If the node instead moves east at exactly the rate Earth goes around the Sun, 360° in 365.2422 days, or 0.9856° a day, the orbit keeps the same angle to the Sun all year. The spacecraft then crosses the equator at the same local solar time on every orbit. This is a sun-synchronous orbit. An Earth-observation satellite sees each place under the same lighting, and its solar arrays see the Sun at a steady angle. The required eastward drift needs \(\cos i \lt 0\): sun-synchronous orbits are slightly retrograde.

Example 2.3 — The inclination of a 500 km sun-synchronous orbit

Find the inclination that makes a 500 km circular orbit sun-synchronous.

Show solution
\[ n = \sqrt{\frac{3.986\times10^{14}}{(6.878\times10^6)^3}} = 1.1068\times10^{-3}\ \text{rad/s} \] \[ \dot\Omega = \frac{2\pi}{365.2422(86\,400)} = 1.9910\times10^{-7}\ \text{rad/s} \] \[ \cos i = -\frac{1.9910\times10^{-7}}{1.5(1.1068\times10^{-3})(1.08263\times10^{-3})(6378/6878)^2} = -0.12882 \] \[ i = 97.40^\circ \]

The launcher must put its payload into an orbit inclined at 97.4°, 7.4° past the pole, flying slightly westward. Lesson 4 shows what that costs in launch speed.

The bulge also turns the ellipse within its plane. The perigee moves at a rate proportional to \(2 - \tfrac52\sin^2 i\), which is zero at \(\sin^2 i = 0.8\), or \(i = 63.4^\circ\). At this critical inclination an elliptical orbit keeps its apogee over the same latitude. Highly elliptical communication orbits use it to dwell over high latitudes.

Key takeaways

  • \(v_c = \sqrt{\mu/r}\) and \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{a^3/\mu}\): 7613 m/s and 94.61 min at 500 km.
  • An ellipse has \(a = (r_p + r_a)/2\) and \(e = (r_a - r_p)/(r_a + r_p)\); the speed anywhere follows from vis-viva, \(v^2 = \mu(2/r - 1/a)\), and \(r_pv_p = r_av_a\).
  • The energy \(-\mu/2a\) depends on the semimajor axis alone: a higher orbit has more energy but a lower speed.
  • Earth's oblateness (\(J_2\)) turns the node at a rate proportional to \(-\cos i\). A sun-synchronous orbit turns 0.9856° east a day: 97.40° at 500 km.
