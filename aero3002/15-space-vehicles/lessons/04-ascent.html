Lesson 4 · 35 min

Getting to Orbit

The largest item in any \(\Delta v\) budget is the first: getting from the launch pad into orbit. The launcher must supply the orbital speed, and on the way it pays for climbing against gravity, pushing through the air and steering. Earth's rotation then helps or hinders, depending on the direction of the launch. This lesson adds these up into the \(\Delta v\) the small launcher's stages must deliver, the number Lesson 5 sizes them for.

Learning objectives

  • Write the ascent \(\Delta v\) budget as the orbital speed plus the gravity, drag and steering losses, corrected for Earth's rotation.
  • Explain where the gravity and drag losses come from, and how the thrust-to-weight ratio trades one against the other.
  • Compute the site's rotational speed, the launch azimuth for an inclination, and the rotation's gain or penalty.
  • Build the small launcher's ascent budget.

The ascent budget

A launcher starts at rest on Earth's surface and must end with the orbit's speed at its altitude. The thrust it delivers must also make up for the losses along the way:

The ascent \(\Delta v\) budget

\[ \Delta v = v_{\text{orbit, rel}} + \Delta v_{\text{gravity}} + \Delta v_{\text{drag}} + \Delta v_{\text{steering}} \]

\(v_{\text{orbit, rel}}\) is the orbit's speed relative to the rotating launch site (below). The losses are integrals over the ascent, and they depend on how the vehicle flies.

The launcher's engines deliver \(\int (T/m)\,dt\), the ideal \(\Delta v\) of the rocket equation. Along the flight path the equation of motion is

\[ \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{T}{m} - \frac{D}{m} - g\sin\gamma \]

with \(\gamma\) the flight-path angle above the horizontal. Integrated over the ascent, the speed gained is the thrust's contribution less the gravity loss \(\int g\sin\gamma\,dt\) and the drag loss \(\int (D/m)\,dt\). The steering loss is the thrust not spent along the velocity, when the vehicle points away from its flight path to shape its trajectory. Typical values for launchers to low orbit are gravity losses of 1.0 to 1.5 km/s, drag losses of 0.05 to 0.2 km/s (more for a small vehicle) and steering losses of 0.05 to 0.2 km/s. Treat them as first estimates, to be replaced by a trajectory simulation.

Gravity and drag losses

The gravity loss is the price of time spent climbing: each second the vehicle points upward, gravity takes \(g\sin\gamma\) out of its speed. A vehicle with more thrust for its weight climbs out sooner, so its gravity loss is smaller. But it reaches high speed in thicker air, so its drag loss, its dynamic pressure and its accelerations are larger, and its engines are heavier (Lessons 6 and 7). A first stage typically lifts off with a thrust-to-weight ratio of 1.2 to 1.5.

Launchers fly a gravity turn. They rise vertically for a few seconds, tilt by a small pitch kick, and then keep their thrust along their velocity. Gravity bends the path over toward the horizontal, with no lift and no angle of attack, which keeps the aerodynamic loads small. Figure 4.1 simulates the small launcher's first stage this way, as a point mass over a round, non-rotating Earth. The simulation uses the 1976 standard atmosphere and a typical drag curve for a slender rocket, so its losses are estimates. The figure finds the pitch kick that gives a chosen flight-path angle at burnout.

At its design point (liftoff \(T/W = 1.30\), burnout at 23° above the horizontal), the first stage loses 1228 m/s to gravity and 100 m/s to drag. A third loss, 90 m/s, comes from the atmosphere's back pressure on the nozzle exits, which keeps the engines below their vacuum thrust (Lesson 6). The engines' ideal \(\Delta v\) of 4572 m/s leaves a burnout speed of 3153 m/s at 83 km. Lesson 5 counts the back-pressure loss by using an average specific impulse of 300 s for the stage rather than its vacuum value. The second stage, flying nearly horizontally above the air, adds little gravity or drag loss.

Earth's rotation

The launch site moves eastward with Earth's rotation, at \(v_E = \omega_E R_E\cos\phi\) at latitude \(\phi\): 465 m/s at the equator, with \(\omega_E = 7.2921\times10^{-5}\) rad/s (one turn per sidereal day). A launch toward the east starts with that speed; a launch toward the west must first cancel it. The direction of the launch, its azimuth \(A_z\) measured from north, is set by the orbit's inclination. For a launch directly into the orbit from latitude \(\phi\),

Launch azimuth and the speed relative to the site

\[ \sin A_z = \frac{\cos i}{\cos\phi}, \qquad v_{\text{rel}} = \sqrt{v^2 - 2vv_E\sin A_z + v_E^2} \]

\(v\) is the orbit's speed (inertial) and \(v_{\text{rel}}\) the speed the launcher must reach relative to the site. A launch can reach an inclination directly only if \(i \ge \phi\) (for prograde orbits), which is why a site's latitude is the lowest inclination it can reach without a plane change.

Example 4.1 — The small launcher's sun-synchronous launch from 45° N

Find the launch azimuth and the effect of Earth's rotation for the 500 km sun-synchronous orbit (\(i = 97.40^\circ\), \(v = 7612.7\) m/s) from 45° N.

Show solution
\[ v_E = 465.1\cos 45^\circ = 328.9\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \sin A_z = \frac{\cos 97.40^\circ}{\cos 45^\circ} = \frac{-0.12882}{0.70711} = -0.18218 \]

\(A_z = 190.5^\circ\), toward the south and slightly west (or 349.5°, slightly west of north).

\[ v_{\text{rel}} = \sqrt{7612.7^2 + 2(7612.7)(328.9)(0.18218) + 328.9^2} = 7679.4\ \text{m/s} \]

Earth's rotation costs this launch 66.7 m/s. Its eastward speed must be cancelled because the orbit runs slightly westward. The simple estimate \(-v_E\sin A_z = 59.9\) m/s is close.

Example 4.2 — Due east from 28.5°

A launcher at 28.5° latitude flies due east into a 200 km orbit inclined at 28.5° (\(v = 7784.3\) m/s). What does Earth's rotation give it?

Show solution
\[ \sin A_z = \frac{\cos 28.5^\circ}{\cos 28.5^\circ} = 1, \quad A_z = 90^\circ \] \[ v_E = 465.1\cos 28.5^\circ = 408.7\ \text{m/s} \] \[ v_{\text{rel}} = v - v_E = 7784.3 - 408.7 = 7375.6\ \text{m/s} \]

A gain of 409 m/s, 5% of the orbital speed. That is why launch sites for low-inclination orbits are near the equator and face east over the sea.

The launch azimuth also has to be safe. A launch must not fly over populated land in its first minutes, when a failure would drop the vehicle near the site (Lesson 8). A site that cannot fly the azimuth an orbit needs must launch on a safe azimuth and then turn the plane during the ascent, a dogleg, which costs \(\Delta v\) like the plane changes of Lesson 3.

The small launcher's budget

Example 4.3 — The \(\Delta v\) the stages must deliver

Take a gravity loss of 1300 m/s (the first stage's 1228 m/s plus the second stage's), a drag loss of 120 m/s and a steering loss of 100 m/s, in line with Figure 4.1 and the typical ranges. Add the speed relative to the site from Example 4.1.

Show solution
\[ \Delta v = 7679.4 + 1300 + 120 + 100 = 9199\ \text{m/s} \]

About 9.20 km/s: the orbital speed of 7613 m/s, 67 m/s for the westward orbit, and 1520 m/s of losses (17% of the total). This is the design \(\Delta v\) of Lesson 5's staging.

Key takeaways

  • The ascent \(\Delta v\) is the orbit's speed relative to the site plus the gravity, drag and steering losses: 9.20 km/s for the small launcher, 17% of it losses.
  • The gravity loss \(\int g\sin\gamma\,dt\) falls with the thrust-to-weight ratio; the drag loss, the dynamic pressure and the accelerations rise with it.
  • A gravity turn keeps the thrust along the velocity after a small pitch kick; the small launcher's first stage burns out at 83 km and 3153 m/s.
  • Earth's rotation gives \(\omega_ER_E\cos\phi\) eastward. With \(\sin A_z = \cos i/\cos\phi\), it gains 409 m/s due east from 28.5° and costs 67 m/s for the sun-synchronous orbit from 45°.
