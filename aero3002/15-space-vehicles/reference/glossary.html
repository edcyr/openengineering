A

Back to top
Apogee and perigee
The farthest and closest points of an orbit from Earth's center, at radii \(r_a\) and \(r_p\). A spacecraft is slowest at apogee and fastest at perigee: \(r_pv_p = r_av_a\).
See: Lesson 2Related: Semimajor axis, Vis-viva equation, Eccentricity

B

Back to top
Bi-elliptic transfer
A three-burn transfer that first climbs beyond the target orbit. It beats the Hohmann transfer only for very large ratios of the radii, at the price of a much longer flight.
See: Lesson 3Related: Hohmann transfer
Bulk density
The density of an oxidizer and a fuel together at their mixture ratio, \((1 + O/F)/((O/F)/\rho_O + 1/\rho_F)\): about 1025 kg/m³ for LOX/RP-1 and 362 for LOX/LH2. It sets the tank volume.
See: Lesson 6Related: Mixture ratio, Specific impulse
Bus spacecraft
The part of a spacecraft that supports the payload: structure, power, thermal control, attitude control, propulsion, communications and the computer.
See: Lesson 1Related: Space segment, Payload

C

Back to top
Characteristic velocity \(c^*\)
\(c^* = p_cA_t/\dot m\): a measure of the combustion that depends almost only on the propellants, about 1760 m/s for the small launcher's kerosene engines. \(c = c^*C_F\).
See: Lesson 6Related: Thrust coefficient \(C_F\), Specific impulse
Circular speed
The speed of a circular orbit, \(v_c = \sqrt{\mu/r}\): 7613 m/s at 500 km and 3075 m/s at geostationary altitude.
See: Lesson 2Related: Period, Vis-viva equation
Collective risk \(E_C\)
The expected number of casualties a launch causes. 14 CFR 450.101 [7] limits it to \(10^{-4}\) for the public and \(2\times10^{-4}\) for neighboring operations personnel.
See: Lesson 8Related: Individual risk \(P_C\), Flight abort, Launch license
Collision avoidance
Launch window closures that keep a launch clear of orbiting objects (14 CFR 450.169 [7]): for inhabitable objects a probability of collision of at most \(10^{-6}\) or 200 km of separation, from 150 km up to orbit and for at least 3 hours.
See: Lesson 8Related: Launch license, Passivation
Combined maneuver
A burn that changes speed and plane at once, \(\Delta v = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 - 2v_1v_2\cos\Delta i}\). Combined at the transfer orbit's apogee, it saves 3.4 km/s on the way from a 28.5° parking orbit to GEO.
See: Lesson 3Related: Plane change, Hohmann transfer
Critical inclination
The inclination, 63.4°, at which Earth's oblateness stops turning an orbit's perigee, so that an elliptical orbit keeps its apogee over the same latitude.
See: Lesson 2Related: J2 \(J_2\), Regression of the node

D

Back to top
Delta-v \(\Delta v\)
The change of velocity a maneuver or a launch requires: the currency of space missions. The rocket equation turns it into propellant.
See: Lesson 3Related: Delta-v budget, Rocket equation
Delta-v budget
The list of a mission's maneuvers with their \(\Delta v\), plus a margin. For a launcher, the ascent budget: orbital speed, Earth's rotation and the losses, 9199 m/s for the small launcher.
See: Lesson 3, Lesson 4Related: Delta-v \(\Delta v\), Gravity loss
Deorbit burn
A retrograde burn that lowers an orbit's perigee into the atmosphere so that the vehicle reenters: 129.8 m/s from 500 km to a 50 km perigee.
See: Lesson 8, Lesson 3Related: Passivation, Apogee and perigee
Design factor of safety
The factor by which the limit load is multiplied for design. NASA-STD-5001B's [6] minimum for metallic structures is 1.4 on ultimate strength and 1.0 (prototype) or 1.25 (protoflight) on yield; an aircraft uses 1.5.
See: Lesson 7Related: Margin of safety, Prototype approach, Protoflight approach, Limit load
Drag loss
The \(\Delta v\) spent against drag in the ascent, \(\int (D/m)\,dt\): about 100 m/s for the small launcher's first stage.
See: Lesson 4, Lesson 4Related: Gravity loss, Delta-v budget, Max-q

E

Back to top
Eccentricity
The shape of an orbit, \(e = (r_a - r_p)/(r_a + r_p)\): 0 for a circle, near 1 for a long ellipse.
See: Lesson 2Related: Apogee and perigee, Semimajor axis
Effective exhaust velocity
\(c = T/\dot m = I_{sp}g_0\): the velocity in the rocket equation, including the pressure thrust.
See: Lesson 5, Lesson 6Related: Specific impulse, Rocket equation
Engine cycle
How a liquid engine feeds its propellants: pressure-fed, gas generator, staged combustion, expander or electric pump. It sets the chamber pressure, the specific impulse and the complexity.
See: Lesson 6Related: Characteristic velocity \(c^*\)
Expansion ratio nozzle
\(\varepsilon_e = A_e/A_t\), which fixes the exit pressure. A larger ratio gains specific impulse in vacuum but overexpands the flow at sea level: 14 for the first stage, 85 for the upper stage.
See: Lesson 6Related: Thrust coefficient \(C_F\), Flow separation

F

Back to top
Flight abort
A system that ends a flight before the vehicle can endanger the public (14 CFR 450.108 [7]). It is needed in any phase where a failure's conditional expected casualties would exceed \(10^{-3}\), unless reliability has been demonstrated.
See: Lesson 8Related: Collective risk \(E_C\), Launch license
Flow separation nozzle
The flow leaving the wall of an overexpanded nozzle, with violent side loads; by a common rule of thumb, below an exit pressure of about 0.4 times ambient.
See: Lesson 6Related: Expansion ratio

G

Back to top
Geostationary orbit
A circular equatorial orbit with a period of one sidereal day, 35 786 km up, where a spacecraft stays over one point of the equator.
See: Lesson 2Related: Period, Circular speed
Gravity loss
The \(\Delta v\) lost to gravity while climbing, \(\int g\sin\gamma\,dt\): 1228 m/s for the small launcher's first stage. A higher thrust-to-weight ratio lowers it.
See: Lesson 4Related: Drag loss, Gravity turn
Gravity turn
An ascent in which, after a vertical rise and a small pitch kick, the thrust stays along the velocity and gravity bends the path over, with no angle of attack.
See: Lesson 4Related: Gravity loss, Max-q
Ground segment
The stations, people and software that command a spacecraft and receive its data.
See: Lesson 1Related: Space segment, Launch segment

H

Back to top
Hohmann transfer
The two-burn transfer between coplanar circular orbits along an ellipse tangent to both: 108.3 m/s from 500 to 700 km, 3893 m/s from 300 km to GEO.
See: Lesson 3Related: Bi-elliptic transfer, Combined maneuver, Delta-v \(\Delta v\)
Hoop stress
The circumferential membrane stress in a pressurized cylinder, \(pR/t\), twice the axial stress \(pR/2t\). It sizes a tank's cylinder for pressure.
See: Lesson 7Related: Minimum gauge, Ullage pressure

I

Back to top
Inclination
The angle between an orbit's plane and the equator. Below 90° the orbit is prograde; above it, retrograde. A direct launch can reach no less than the site's latitude.
See: Lesson 2, Lesson 4Related: Launch azimuth, Sun-synchronous orbit
Individual risk \(P_C\)
The probability that a launch injures a given person: at most \(10^{-6}\) per launch for any member of the public under 14 CFR 450.101 [7].
See: Lesson 8Related: Collective risk \(E_C\)
Inert mass
Everything in a stage except its propellant and the payload: tanks, engines, structure, avionics and residual propellant. The small launcher's first stage: 821 kg when closed.
See: Lesson 5, Lesson 8Related: Structural coefficient

J

Back to top
J2 \(J_2\) oblateness
The coefficient of Earth's equatorial bulge, \(1.08263\times10^{-3}\). It turns inclined orbits' planes and perigees.
See: Lesson 2Related: Regression of the node, Sun-synchronous orbit, Critical inclination

K

Back to top
Key Decision Point
The event at which NASA's decision authority judges whether a project may go on to its next life-cycle phase.
See: Lesson 1Related: Life-cycle phases
Knockdown factor
The factor that lowers a thin shell's classical buckling stress for the effect of imperfections. NASA-STD-5001B [6] refers to NASA SP-8007 [9] for it: 0.357 at \(R/t = 400\).
See: Lesson 7Related: Design factor of safety, Minimum gauge

L

Back to top
Launch azimuth
The direction of a launch, measured clockwise from north. For a direct launch into inclination \(i\) from latitude \(\phi\), \(\sin A_z = \cos i/\cos\phi\): 190.5° for the small launcher's sun-synchronous orbit from 45°.
See: Lesson 4Related: Inclination, Rotation gain or penalty
Launch license
The FAA's permission to launch under 14 CFR Part 450. It requires the operator to keep the risks to the public, to aircraft and to property within the criteria of 450.101, rather than certifying the vehicle.
See: Lesson 8Related: Collective risk \(E_C\), Flight abort, Collision avoidance
Launch segment
The launch vehicle and the launch site that put a spacecraft in its orbit.
See: Lesson 1Related: Space segment, Ground segment
Life-cycle phases NASA
NASA's seven phases of a spaceflight project [8]: Pre-Phase A (concept studies), A (concept and technology development), B (preliminary design), C (final design and fabrication), D (assembly, integration, test and launch), E (operations) and F (closeout).
See: Lesson 1Related: Key Decision Point, Technology readiness level
Limit load
In the words of NASA-STD-5001B [6], the maximum anticipated load, or combination of loads, in the structure's service life. Design factors multiply it.
See: Lesson 7Related: Design factor of safety, Margin of safety

M

Back to top
Margin systems engineering
An allowance carried in the budget, the schedule and the technical parameters to cover uncertainty and risk [8], consumed as a project matures. Mass and power margins are tracked by every NASA hardware project.
See: Lesson 1Related: Payload
Margin of safety
\(MS = \text{allowable}/(\text{limit} \times FS) - 1\), which must not be negative. The small launcher's oxygen tank has a buckling margin of 0.30.
See: Lesson 7Related: Design factor of safety, Limit load
Mass ratio
A stage's initial over final mass, \(m_0/m_f = e^{\Delta v/c}\): 15.8 to reach orbit at 340 s in one burn.
See: Lesson 5Related: Rocket equation, Structural coefficient
Max-q
The maximum dynamic pressure of the ascent, about a minute into flight: 30.5 kPa at 70 s and Mach 1.39 for the small launcher.
See: Lesson 7Related: Gravity turn, Drag loss
Minimum gauge
The thinnest wall that can be made and handled. On a small launcher it, not pressure, sizes the tanks: 1.5 mm against the 0.82 mm the oxygen tank's pressure needs.
See: Lesson 7Related: Knockdown factor, Hoop stress
Mixture ratio
The mass of oxidizer per mass of fuel, \(O/F\): about 2.6 for LOX/RP-1 and 6 for LOX/LH2.
See: Lesson 6Related: Bulk density

P

Back to top
Passivation
Removing the stored energy of a stage left in orbit, so that it cannot break up: residual propellant depleted with the valves open, pressure vented, batteries discharged (14 CFR 450.171 [7]).
See: Lesson 8Related: Deorbit burn, Collision avoidance
Payload
For a spacecraft, the instrument that does the mission; for a launcher, everything it carries to orbit, here the 300 kg spacecraft.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 5Related: Bus, Payload fraction
Payload fraction
Payload over liftoff mass: 2.39% for the small launcher (2.48% when its loop is closed), against 14% for the business jet.
See: Lesson 5Related: Staging, Structural coefficient
Period orbit
The time for one orbit, \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{a^3/\mu}\): 94.61 min at 500 km.
See: Lesson 2Related: Circular speed, Semimajor axis
Phasing orbit
An orbit of a different period used to move a spacecraft along its orbit. To gain \(\Delta\theta\) in one revolution, \(T_{ph} = T_0(1 - \Delta\theta/360^\circ)\).
See: Lesson 3Related: Hohmann transfer
Plane change
A burn that turns an orbit's plane, \(\Delta v = 2v\sin(\Delta i/2)\): 133 m/s per degree at 500 km. Cheapest where the speed is lowest.
See: Lesson 3Related: Combined maneuver, Inclination
Pressure-fed engine
An engine whose propellants are pushed by gas pressure in the tanks: simple, but with heavy tanks and a low chamber pressure.
See: Lesson 6Related: Engine cycle
Proof test
A test of each flight article above its limit load or pressure to screen workmanship: 1.05 for metallic propellant tanks under NASA-STD-5001B [6].
See: Lesson 7Related: Qualification test, Design factor of safety
Protoflight approach
Verifying a structure by testing the flight article itself above limit load but below yield, which needs a yield design factor of 1.25. Common for spacecraft.
See: Lesson 7Related: Prototype approach, Qualification test
Prototype approach
Verifying a structure by testing a separate, identical article to ultimate load; NASA-STD-5001B [6] calls it the accepted practice for launch vehicles.
See: Lesson 7Related: Protoflight approach, Qualification test

Q

Back to top
Qualification test
A test that shows the design meets its requirements, at 1.4 times limit load on a prototype or 1.2 on a protoflight article.
See: Lesson 7Related: Prototype approach, Protoflight approach

R

Back to top
Regression of the node
The drift of an orbit's ascending node caused by Earth's bulge, \(\dot\Omega = -\tfrac32 nJ_2(R_E/p)^2\cos i\): westward for prograde orbits.
See: Lesson 2Related: J2 \(J_2\), Sun-synchronous orbit
Rocket equation
\(\Delta v = c\ln(m_0/m_f)\), published by Tsiolkovsky in 1903 [5]: the launcher's governing equation, the counterpart of Breguet's range for an aircraft.
See: Lesson 5Related: Mass ratio, Effective exhaust velocity
Rotation gain or penalty
The \(\Delta v\) that Earth's rotation saves or costs a launch, through the site's eastward speed \(\omega_ER_E\cos\phi\): a 409 m/s gain due east from 28.5°, a 67 m/s penalty for the small launcher.
See: Lesson 4Related: Launch azimuth, Delta-v budget

S

Back to top
Semimajor axis
Half the long axis of an orbit, \(a = (r_p + r_a)/2\). It alone sets the orbit's energy \(-\mu/2a\) and period.
See: Lesson 2Related: Apogee and perigee, Period, Specific energy
Space segment
The spacecraft of a space system: its payload and its bus.
See: Lesson 1Related: Bus, Launch segment, Ground segment
Specific energy orbit
Energy per unit mass. Relative to the surface, a 500 km orbit takes 33.5 MJ/kg, 230 times the business jet's cruise; along an orbit, \(\varepsilon = v^2/2 - \mu/r = -\mu/2a\).
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 2Related: Semimajor axis, Circular speed
Specific impulse
Thrust per unit weight flow of propellant, \(I_{sp} = T/(\dot mg_0) = c/g_0\), in seconds: 283/315 s for the first-stage engine at sea level and in vacuum, 340 s for the upper stage.
See: Lesson 5, Lesson 6Related: Effective exhaust velocity, Characteristic velocity \(c^*\), Thrust coefficient \(C_F\)
Staged combustion
An engine cycle in which the turbine exhaust is burned in the main chamber, so no propellant is wasted; it allows high chamber pressures.
See: Lesson 6Related: Engine cycle
Staging
Dropping empty tanks and engines during the ascent. The \(\Delta v\) is best split with a Lagrange multiplier, \(n_i = (c_i\eta - 1)/(c_i\varepsilon_i\eta)\): 4354 and 4846 m/s for the small launcher.
See: Lesson 5, Lesson 5Related: Payload fraction, Structural coefficient
Steering loss
The \(\Delta v\) lost when thrust points away from the velocity to shape the trajectory: 100 m/s for the small launcher.
See: Lesson 4Related: Gravity loss, Drag loss
Structural coefficient
\(\varepsilon = m_s/(m_s + m_p)\), a stage's inert share. One stage gives at most \(c\ln(1/\varepsilon)\). The small launcher's close at 0.0824 and 0.0920.
See: Lesson 5, Lesson 8Related: Inert mass, Staging
Sun-synchronous orbit
An orbit whose node drifts east 0.9856° a day, following the Sun, so that it crosses the equator at the same local time: slightly retrograde, 97.40° at 500 km.
See: Lesson 2Related: Regression of the node, J2 \(J_2\), Inclination

T

Back to top
Technology readiness level
A scale of a technology's maturity from 1, basic research, to 9, flight operations [8]. A system's level is its least mature element's; TRL 6 (demonstrated in a relevant environment) is typically needed before integration.
See: Lesson 1Related: Life-cycle phases
Thrust coefficient \(C_F\)
\(C_F = T/(p_cA_t)\), a measure of the nozzle: 1.755 in vacuum and 1.578 at sea level for the first-stage engine.
See: Lesson 6Related: Characteristic velocity \(c^*\), Expansion ratio
Two-body problem
The motion of a particle about a point mass with no other forces. Its orbits are conic sections with the central body at a focus.
See: Lesson 2Related: Vis-viva equation, Circular speed

U

Back to top
Ullage pressure
The gas pressure above the liquid in a tank, which feeds the pumps and stiffens the walls: 300 kPa for the small launcher, plus the liquid's head \(\rho ngh\).
See: Lesson 7Related: Hoop stress

V

Back to top
Vis-viva equation
\(v = \sqrt{\mu(2/r - 1/a)}\): the speed anywhere on an orbit from its radius and semimajor axis.
See: Lesson 2Related: Semimajor axis, Apogee and perigee
