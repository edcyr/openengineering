Engine Failure and the Vertical Tail

When an engine fails on takeoff, the other one pushes the aircraft into a yaw, and the dead one drags on the same side. The rudder must hold it straight down to a minimum control speed the regulations tie to the stall speed. For most twins this, not stability, sizes the vertical tail. This lesson checks the business jet's, then takes stock of what the resized tails cost around the design loop.

Learning objectives

  • Find the yawing moment of a failed engine, and the minimum control speed at which the rudder must balance it.
  • Find the rudder deflection that balances it, and the vertical tail area that keeps the deflection within its limit; check the crosswind case.
  • Trace the resized tails through drag, lift-to-drag ratio and takeoff weight: one more turn of the design loop.

The minimum control speed

With one engine failed, the live engine's thrust \(T\) acts at its lateral distance \(y_E\) from the plane of symmetry, and the failed engine's drag adds to the same yawing moment:

Yawing moment with an engine failed

\[ N_E = T\,y_E, \qquad N_D = k_D\,N_E \]

\(T\): takeoff thrust of one engine. \(k_D\): the failed engine's windmilling drag as a share of \(N_E\), about 0.15 for a low-bypass and 0.25 for a high-bypass turbofan ([5], Chapter 11). Take 0.25 for the business jet.

The rudder's yawing moment grows with the dynamic pressure, so there is a speed below which full rudder can no longer hold the aircraft straight: the minimum control speed \(V_{MC}\). Part 25 (25.149 [8]) requires \(V_{MC}\) no higher than 1.13 times the reference stall speed \(V_{SR}\) in the takeoff configuration, with no more than \(5^\circ\) of bank and no more than 150 lb on the rudder pedals; 25.107 then sets the rotation and takeoff safety speeds at least 5% and 10% above it. The conceptual check: at \(V_{MC} = 1.13\,V_{SR}\), wings level (the bank's help ignored), the rudder must balance \(N_E + N_D\) at no more than about \(25^\circ\).

Example 8.1 — The business jet's engine-out moment

The business jet: \(W_0 = 6943\) kg, \(S = 28\) m², takeoff \(C_{L\max} = 1.7\) (Module 5). Each engine gives 10.56 kN at takeoff (Module 7) on a lateral arm of 1.254 m (Module 6). Find \(V_{MC}\), its dynamic pressure, and the yawing moment to balance.

Show solution
\[ V_{SR} = \sqrt{\frac{2(6943)(9.807)}{1.225(28)(1.7)}} = 48.33\ \text{m/s} \] \[ V_{MC} = 1.13(48.33) = 54.61\ \text{m/s}, \qquad q = \tfrac12(1.225)(54.61)^2 = 1826\ \text{Pa} \] \[ N_E = 10\,560(1.254) = 13\,245\ \text{N·m}, \qquad N_D = 0.25\,N_E = 3311\ \text{N·m} \]

The rudder must balance 16 556 N·m at 54.6 m/s (106 kt).

The rudder

The rudder is a plain flap on the vertical tail; its effectiveness \(\tau\) comes from its chord share, as the elevator's did (Lesson 4). Its yawing moment per radian of deflection, and the balance at \(V_{MC}\):

Rudder power and the vertical tail for engine failure

\[ C_{n\delta_r} = 0.9\,C_{L\alpha,V}\,\frac{S_V}{S}\,\frac{l_V}{b}\,\tau_r \] \[ \delta_r = \frac{N_E + N_D}{q\,S\,b\,C_{n\delta_r}} \le \delta_{r,\max} \] \[ S_V \ge \frac{N_E + N_D}{0.9\,q\,l_V\,C_{L\alpha,V}\,\tau_r\,\delta_{r,\max}} \]

The factor 0.9 allows for the rudder's partial span and the tail's dynamic pressure. \(\delta_{r,\max}\): about \(25^\circ\), leaving a margin to the rudder's stop. After [5], Chapter 11, and [4], Chapter 21, as given in [6].

Example 8.2 — The business jet's rudder

The rudder spans the fin and takes 30% of its chord. With Lesson 7's fin (\(S_V/S = 0.1310\), \(l_V = 6.611\) m at the aft CG, \(b = 14.97\) m; \(C_{L\alpha,V} = 2.687\) at Mach 0.16), find the rudder deflection at \(V_{MC}\) and the smallest fin that keeps it to \(25^\circ\).

Show solution

For a 30% chord, \(\tau_r = 0.596\) (Lesson 4):

\[ C_{n\delta_r} = 0.9(2.687)(0.1310)\frac{6.611}{14.97}(0.596) = 0.0834 \] \[ \delta_r = \frac{16\,556}{1826(28)(14.97)(0.0834)} = 0.259\ \text{rad} = 14.9^\circ \] \[ S_V \ge \frac{16\,556}{0.9(1826)(6.611)(2.687)(0.596)(0.436)} = 2.18\ \text{m}^2 \]

The 3.67 m² fin uses only \(14.9^\circ\) of rudder: engine failure does not size it. The rear engines sit 1.25 m from the centerline, a short arm. The same engines under the wing, at 35% of the semispan (2.62 m), would need \(31^\circ\) of rudder, and a fin of 4.55 m² to bring it back to \(25^\circ\). That is one reason business jets put their engines on the rear fuselage.

Crosswind

The rudder has a second job. Landing or taking off in a crosswind, the aircraft is held aligned with the runway in a sideslip, and the rudder must hold it against the directional stability of Lesson 7. A common sizing case [6] is a crosswind of 20% of the takeoff speed, a sideslip of \(\beta = \arctan 0.2 = 11.3^\circ\), with the rudder within about \(20^\circ\):

Rudder for a steady sideslip

\[ \delta_r = \frac{C_{n\beta}\,\beta}{C_{n\delta_r}} \]

Too much directional stability makes this harder: a fin sized generously for stability needs a rudder to match. After [6].

Example 8.3 — The business jet in a crosswind

At takeoff, \(C_L = 1.7/1.1^2 = 1.40\). With the volume method for the fuselage, the business jet's \(C_{n\beta}\) at the aft CG is then 0.132 (Lesson 7, with the larger wing term). Find the rudder deflection for an \(11.3^\circ\) sideslip.

Show solution
\[ \delta_r = \frac{0.132(11.31^\circ)}{0.0834} = 17.9^\circ \]

Inside \(20^\circ\), with 2° to spare: the tighter of the two rudder cases for the business jet, but still met. With DATCOM's fuselage estimate, \(C_{n\beta} = 0.083\) and the rudder needs \(11.2^\circ\): the pessimistic method for stability is the optimistic one here.

The vertical tail is sized by the largest of three requirements: directional stability (Lesson 7, 2.66 to 3.56 m² by the two fuselage methods), engine failure (2.18 m²) and crosswind (met with the fin as it is). The 3.67 m² fin from Module 4's volume coefficient of 0.060 passes all three, and stays.

Example 8.4 — What the resized tails cost

Module 10's build-up gives the business jet's horizontal tail and fin, as Module 6 drew them, drag coefficients of 0.00189 and 0.00114 (on the wing's reference area), with 5% added for leakage and protuberances, and \(K = 0.04909\). The horizontal tail is now 24.5% larger than Module 6's and the fin 6.3%. Estimate the new zero-lift drag and \((L/D)_{\max}\).

Show solution

Each tail's drag grows with its wetted area, so with its planform area:

\[ \Delta C_{D0} = 1.05\left[0.00189(0.245) + 0.00114(0.063)\right] = 0.00056 \] \[ C_{D0} = 0.02023 + 0.00056 = 0.02079 \] \[ \left(\frac LD\right)_{\max} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{0.02079(0.04909)}} = 15.65 \]

The full build-up, in which the larger tail's longer chords give it slightly less friction, gives 0.02074 and 15.67; the cruise \(L/D\) at 12 km falls from 10.80 to 10.57. The tails weigh 11.9 kg more than Module 6's (Module 12's equations, calibrated). Through Module 3's sizing, the takeoff weight rises by about 172 kg: 2.5%, 104 kg of it from the drag. Twice Module 12's turn, because the stability requirement asked for more tail than the volume coefficient did.

Larger still is the turn Module 12 already pointed to: every weight so far rests on Module 3's fuel fraction, which assumed a cruise \(L/D\) of 14.6, while Module 10 found the business jet cruising at 10.8 (now 10.57) at 12 km. Fed through the sizing, that alone takes the takeoff weight to about 10 700 kg (Module 10, Lesson 8). A heavier aircraft with a larger wing and engines has a different CG range, a different scissor plot and different tails. No estimate is final until all of them agree.

What the course carries forward

The business jet after this module: a horizontal tail of 6.75 m² (volume coefficient 0.82) with a 30% elevator, and a vertical tail of 3.67 m² (0.060) with a 30% rudder. The wing's quarter-MAC is at 7.405 m, 47.8% of the fuselage, the main gear at 8.08 m, and the CG ranges from 21.5% to 37.1% of the MAC. The neutral point is at 46.7% of the MAC at low speed and 42.1% in cruise, a static margin of 5% at the aft CG in cruise. The rudder needs \(14.9^\circ\) at \(V_{MC} = 54.6\) m/s with an engine failed and \(17.9^\circ\) in a 20% crosswind. These tails raise the zero-lift drag to 0.0207 and lower \((L/D)_{\max}\) to 15.67 and the cruise \(L/D\) to 10.57; one turn of the loop adds about 172 kg to \(W_0\). The earlier modules' numbers are the design as it stood then: Module 12's weights, CG range and wing position, Module 10's drag and Module 3's takeoff weight all predate these tails. Module 14 brings the drag, the weights, the engines and the tails together and closes the design.

Key takeaways

  • With an engine failed, the rudder must balance \(N_E + N_D = (1 + k_D)\,T\,y_E\) at \(V_{MC}\), no more than \(1.13\,V_{SR}\) (25.149), within about \(25^\circ\).
  • The engines' lateral position drives it: the business jet's rear engines need \(14.9^\circ\); under the wing they would need \(31^\circ\).
  • The crosswind case needs \(\delta_r = C_{n\beta}\beta/C_{n\delta_r}\): \(17.9^\circ\) for the business jet in a 20% crosswind.
  • The vertical tail takes the largest of the stability, engine-failure and crosswind requirements; the business jet's 3.67 m² fin meets all three.
  • Resizing the tails costs drag and weight: \(C_{D0}\) 0.0207, \((L/D)_{\max}\) 15.67 and about 172 kg more \(W_0\) in one turn of the loop, which Module 14 closes.
