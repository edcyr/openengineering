Directional and Lateral Stability

An aircraft in a sideslip should turn its nose back into the wind, like a weathervane, and roll its low wing back up. The first is directional stability, and it is the vertical tail's job; the second, lateral stability, comes mostly from the wing's dihedral and its place on the fuselage. This lesson estimates both for the business jet.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the signs of the yawing and rolling moments in a sideslip, and which ones make an aircraft stable.
  • Estimate the yawing-moment derivative \(C_{n\beta}\) from the wing, the fuselage (two ways) and the vertical tail, and the tail area it needs.
  • Estimate the rolling-moment derivative \(C_{l\beta}\) from dihedral, the wing's vertical position and the vertical tail.

Sideslip

In a sideslip the relative wind meets the aircraft at an angle \(\beta\) to its plane of symmetry, positive when the wind comes from the right. The aircraft is directionally stable if the yawing moment \(N\) that results turns its nose to the right, into the wind, which closes the sideslip. With \(C_n = N/(qSb)\):

Static directional stability

\[ C_{n\beta} = \frac{\partial C_n}{\partial\beta} \gt 0, \qquad C_{n\beta} \ge 0.0571 \text{ per rad} \]

The second is a design target: 0.001 per degree ([5], Chapter 11, after [2]). Part 25 (25.177 [8]) requires the tendency to recover from a skid with the rudder free to be positive, and the tendency to raise the low wing in a sideslip to be positive too.

The vertical tail is a wing at an angle of attack \(\beta\): its side force, behind the CG, turns the nose into the wind. The fuselage works against it: in a sideslip it makes a side force well forward of the CG, which turns the nose away. The wing adds a little.

The weathercock derivative

The three contributions add:

Yawing moment due to sideslip

\[ C_{n\beta} = C_{n\beta,W} + C_{n\beta,F} + C_{n\beta,V} \] \[ C_{n\beta,V} = C_{L\alpha,V}\left(1 + \frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial\beta}\right)\eta_V\,\frac{S_V}{S}\,\frac{l_V}{b} \]

\(l_V\) is the vertical tail's arm, from the CG to its aerodynamic center; the aft CG is critical. \(\sigma\) is the sidewash at the tail, from the wing and fuselage; \(\eta_V\) its dynamic-pressure ratio. The wing's term is small: \(C_{n\beta,W} \approx C_L^2/(4\pi A)\).

The fin's lift slope comes from Module 4's formula, with an effective aspect ratio: the fuselage below and the horizontal tail act as end plates. Take the effective aspect ratio as 1.55 times the geometric one, a factor that accounts for the fuselage and the horizontal tail [6]. The sidewash and the dynamic-pressure ratio come together from a DATCOM fit:

Sidewash and dynamic pressure at the fin

\[ \left(1 + \frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial\beta}\right)\eta_V = 0.724 + \frac{3.06\,S_V/S}{1 + \cos\Lambda_{c/4}} + 0.4\,\frac{z_w}{d} + 0.009\,A \]

\(z_w\): the wing root's quarter chord below the fuselage axis (positive for a low wing); \(d\): the fuselage depth; \(\Lambda_{c/4}\) and \(A\): the wing's. From [3], after the DATCOM [9], as coded in SUAVE, an open-source design code from Stanford University [10].

Example 7.1 — The business jet's vertical tail

The vertical tail from Module 4's volume coefficient, with the wing in its new place (Lesson 6): \(S_V = 3.67\) m², \(A_V = 1.5\), half-chord sweep \(29.8^\circ\) (of the fin and its mirror image, aspect ratio 3), aerodynamic center at 14.26 m. The aft CG is at 7.649 m. \(S = 28\) m², \(b = 14.97\) m, \(A = 8\), \(\Lambda_{c/4} = 25^\circ\), \(z_w = 0.599\) m, \(d = 1.597\) m. Find \(C_{n\beta,V}\) at low speed, \(M = 0.2\).

Show solution

Effective aspect ratio \(1.55(1.5) = 2.325\); Module 4's formula gives \(C_{L\alpha,V} = 2.693\) per rad. The sidewash factor, with \(S_V/S = 0.1310\):

\[ 0.724 + \frac{3.06(0.1310)}{1 + \cos 25^\circ} + \frac{0.4(0.599)}{1.597} + 0.009(8) = 1.156 \]

With \(l_V = 14.26 - 7.649 = 6.611\) m:

\[ C_{n\beta,V} = 2.693(1.156)(0.1310)\frac{6.611}{14.97} = 0.180 \text{ per rad} \]

The fuselage, two ways

The fuselage's term is the hardest to estimate, and the largest against the tail. Two methods are common. The first scales with the fuselage's volume; the second is DATCOM's, for a fuselage close to a cylinder, with a factor for the CG's position along it and one for the Reynolds number:

Fuselage yawing moment

\[ C_{n\beta,F} = -1.3\,\frac{\text{Vol}_F}{S\,b}\,\frac{d}{w} \quad \text{(volume method)} \] \[ C_{n\beta,F} = -\frac{180}{\pi}\,k_N\,k_{Rl}\,\frac{l_F^2\,d}{S\,b} \quad \text{(DATCOM)} \] \[ k_N = 0.01\left[0.27\,\frac{x_{cg}}{l_F} - 0.168\ln\frac{l_F}{d} + 0.416\right] - 0.0005 \] \[ k_{Rl} = 0.46\log_{10}\frac{Re_{l_F}}{10^6} + 1 \]

Per radian. \(\text{Vol}_F\): the fuselage's volume; \(d/w\): depth over width (1 for the business jet); \(x_{cg}\) from the nose. The volume method follows [1], Chapter 16, as given in [6]; DATCOM's [9] follows [5], Chapter 11.

Example 7.2 — The fuselage and the total

The business jet's fuselage is 15.5 m long and 1.597 m deep, with a volume of 21.90 m³ (Module 6's outline). Its Reynolds number in cruise is \(9.22 \times 10^7\). Find \(C_{n\beta,F}\) both ways at the aft CG, and the total with the fin of Example 7.1 at \(C_L = 0.253\).

Show solution
\[ C_{n\beta,F} = -1.3\,\frac{21.90}{28(14.97)} = -0.0679 \quad \text{(volume)} \]

For DATCOM, \(x_{cg}/l_F = 7.649/15.5 = 0.493\):

\[ k_N = 0.01\left[0.133 - 0.382 + 0.416\right] - 0.0005 = 0.00117 \] \[ k_{Rl} = 0.46\log_{10}92.2 + 1 = 1.904 \] \[ C_{n\beta,F} = -57.30(0.00117)(1.904)\frac{15.5^2(1.597)}{28(14.97)} = -0.117 \]

The wing: \(0.253^2/(4\pi \times 8) = 0.0006\). The totals:

\[ C_{n\beta} = 0.0006 - 0.0679 + 0.180 = 0.113 \quad \text{(volume)} \] \[ C_{n\beta} = 0.0006 - 0.117 + 0.180 = 0.064 \quad \text{(DATCOM)} \]

Both clear 0.0571, DATCOM's barely. The two fuselage estimates differ by a factor of 1.7: an honest measure of how well a conceptual design knows this term. Low speed (the fin's smallest lift slope) and low \(C_L\) (the wing's smallest help) make this the critical case; in cruise at Mach 0.8 the fin's lift slope rises to 3.01 and the totals to 0.134 and 0.085.

Solving for the fin area that just gives 0.0571 (the sidewash factor depends on \(S_V\) too, so it is a quadratic) gives 2.66 m² with the volume method and 3.56 m² with DATCOM's. The fin Module 4's volume coefficient gave, 3.67 m², passes both. Lesson 8 checks it against the rudder's job when an engine fails; the larger of the two areas wins.

Lateral stability: the dihedral effect

In a sideslip to the right the right wing is the one into the wind. An aircraft is laterally stable if the sideslip makes a rolling moment that lifts the right wing, rolling the aircraft left, away from the slip. With \(C_l = L'/(qSb)\), positive right wing down, that is \(C_{l\beta} \lt 0\). It is called the dihedral effect, after its largest source:

Rolling moment due to sideslip

\[ C_{l\beta,\Gamma} = -\frac{C_{L\alpha,W}\,\Gamma}{4}\cdot\frac{2(1 + 2\lambda)}{3(1 + \lambda)} \] \[ C_{l\beta,WF} = 1.2\sqrt{A}\,\frac{z_w}{b}\,\frac{d + w}{b} \] \[ C_{l\beta,V} = -C_{L\alpha,V}\left(1 + \frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial\beta}\right)\eta_V\,\frac{S_V}{S}\,\frac{z_V}{b} \]

Per radian, with the dihedral angle \(\Gamma\) in radians. \(z_w\) is positive for a low wing, which is destabilizing; a high wing is stabilizing. \(z_V\): the fin's aerodynamic center above the CG. After [1], Chapter 16, as given in [6]. Wing sweep adds a further stabilizing term in proportion to \(C_L\); leaving it out makes these estimates conservative for the business jet.

Example 7.3 — The business jet's dihedral effect

The business jet's wing has \(3^\circ\) of dihedral (Module 6), \(\lambda = 0.35\) and \(C_{L\alpha,W} = 4.507\) at low speed. The fin's aerodynamic center is 1.839 m above the CG. Find \(C_{l\beta}\).

Show solution
\[ C_{l\beta,\Gamma} = -\frac{4.507(0.05236)}{4}\cdot\frac{2(1.70)}{3(1.35)} = -0.0495 \] \[ C_{l\beta,WF} = 1.2\sqrt8\,\frac{0.599}{14.97}\,\frac{3.194}{14.97} = +0.0290 \] \[ C_{l\beta,V} = -2.693(1.156)(0.1310)\frac{1.839}{14.97} = -0.0501 \]

\(C_{l\beta} = -0.0495 + 0.0290 - 0.0501 = -0.0706\) per rad: stable. Without its dihedral, the low wing would leave only \(-0.021\); with a high wing and no dihedral, the same aircraft would have about \(-0.066\). Low-wing aircraft have dihedral and high-wing ones often have little or none (anhedral, on swept high wings) for this reason.

More is not better without limit. A large dihedral effect with weak directional stability gives a lightly damped yawing and rolling oscillation, the Dutch roll; a small dihedral effect with strong directional stability lets the aircraft drift into a slowly tightening spiral. Choosing the balance needs the dynamic modes, beyond a conceptual design; here it is enough that both derivatives have the right signs and sensible sizes.

Key takeaways

  • Directional stability: \(C_{n\beta} \gt 0\), and at least 0.0571 per rad as a target. The vertical tail provides it; the fuselage works against it.
  • The fuselage's term is uncertain: two methods differ by a factor of 1.7 for the business jet. Size the fin with the more pessimistic one, or say which you used.
  • The business jet's 3.67 m² fin gives \(C_{n\beta}\) of 0.064 to 0.113 at low speed: enough by either method.
  • Lateral stability: \(C_{l\beta} \lt 0\). Dihedral and a high wing help, a low wing hurts, and the fin helps; the business jet's is \(-0.071\) per rad with \(3^\circ\) of dihedral.
