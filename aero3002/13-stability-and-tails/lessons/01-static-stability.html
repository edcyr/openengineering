Static Stability and Trim

Module 12 found where the business jet balances and moved its wing to put the CG range at 17% to 33% of the mean aerodynamic chord. Balance is not enough: the aircraft must also return to its trimmed attitude after a gust, and the pilot must be able to hold it there. This lesson sets out what stability means and how it is measured.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish static from dynamic stability, about the pitch, yaw and roll axes.
  • Read a pitching-moment curve, and state the two conditions for a stable aircraft that can be trimmed.
  • Define the neutral point and the static margin, and relate the static margin to \(C_{M\alpha}\).
  • Summarize what the certification rules require of stability and control.

Stable, neutral, unstable

Disturb an aircraft in trimmed flight, by a gust or a twitch of the controls. It is statically stable if the first moment that results pushes it back toward where it was; neutral if no moment results; unstable if the moment pushes it further away. Static stability says only which way the aircraft starts to move. Dynamic stability asks what happens next: whether the motion that follows dies out, as a damped oscillation, or grows.

The question is asked about each axis. In pitch (longitudinal stability) a nose-up disturbance must bring a nose-down moment. In yaw (directional stability) a sideslip must bring a moment that turns the nose into the wind. In roll (lateral stability) a sideslip must bring a moment that raises the low wing. The horizontal tail does most of the work in pitch, the vertical tail in yaw, and the wing's dihedral and sweep in roll.

The pitching-moment curve

Plot the pitching-moment coefficient about the CG, \(C_{M,cg}\), against the angle of attack. The aircraft is trimmed where \(C_{M,cg} = 0\). If the slope \(C_{M\alpha} = \partial C_{M,cg}/\partial\alpha\) is negative, a rise in angle of attack brings a nose-down moment and a fall a nose-up one: the aircraft is stable. If the slope is positive, every disturbance grows.

A stable aircraft that can be trimmed

\[ C_{M\alpha} = \frac{\partial C_{M,cg}}{\partial\alpha} \lt 0, \qquad C_{M0} = C_{M,cg}(\alpha = 0) \gt 0 \]

The first condition makes it stable; the second makes it trim at a positive angle of attack, where the wing lifts.

The neutral point and the static margin

Moving the CG aft makes \(C_{M\alpha}\) less negative, because the wing's lift, acting near the quarter-chord, gains leverage about the CG while the tail loses it. At one CG position the slope is zero: the neutral point, the aircraft's aerodynamic center. The distance from the CG forward of it, as a share of the mean aerodynamic chord, is the static margin:

Static margin

\[ \text{SM} = \bar x_{np} - \bar x_{cg}, \qquad C_{M\alpha} = -C_{L\alpha}\,\text{SM} \]

\(\bar x\): a position along the mean aerodynamic chord, as a fraction of it from its leading edge; \(C_{L\alpha}\), the aircraft's lift-curve slope. A positive margin is stable.

A large margin is very stable but heavy to control: the tail must work hard to change the attitude or to trim at high lift. A small one is responsive but sensitive. Designers aim at a margin that varies by class:

The engines take some of it away: their thrust, and the flow they draw through or over the aircraft, typically cost jets 1% to 3% of static margin and propeller aircraft 4% to 10% [5], effects the simple estimates of Lesson 2 leave out.

Example 1.1 — The business jet's static margin

Lesson 3 will find the business jet's neutral point, with Module 12's layout, at 41.0% of the MAC at low speed, where its lift-curve slope, wing and tail together, is 4.956 per radian. Find the static margin and \(C_{M\alpha}\) at Module 12's aft CG, 32.9%, and the pitching moment after a gust raises the angle of attack by \(2^\circ\).

Show solution
\[ \text{SM} = 0.410 - 0.329 = 0.081, \qquad C_{M\alpha} = -4.956(0.081) = -0.401\ \text{per rad} \] \[ \Delta C_M = -0.401\left(\frac{2\pi}{180}\right) = -0.0140 \]

A nose-down moment, back toward trim. The margin, 8.1%, is above the 5% a business jet needs; Lesson 3 finds that it shrinks in cruise.

What the certification rules ask

For transport aircraft the rules state stability as what the pilot feels (14 CFR 25.171 to 25.181 [8]):

  • Longitudinal: a pull must be needed to fly slower than the trim speed and a push to fly faster, with an average stick-force gradient of at least 1 lb per 6 knots; released, the speed must return to within 10% of the trim speed (7.5% in cruise).
  • Directional: with the rudder free, the aircraft must tend to recover from a skid, at every speed from \(1.13\,V_{SR1}\) up.
  • Lateral: with the ailerons free, the low wing must tend to rise in a sideslip, or at least not fall.
  • Dynamic: the short-period pitch oscillation must be heavily damped, and the combined yaw-and-roll oscillation (the Dutch roll) positively damped.

Control is set out alongside: the aircraft must be trimmed in climb, cruise and approach (25.161), the pilot's forces may not exceed set limits (25.143), and it must be controllable with an engine failed (25.149, Lesson 8) [8]. Lessons 2 to 6 build the tools to meet these in pitch; Lessons 7 and 8 in yaw and roll.

Key takeaways

  • Static stability is the first tendency after a disturbance; dynamic stability is what the motion does next.
  • A stable aircraft that trims at positive lift has \(C_{M\alpha} \lt 0\) and \(C_{M0} \gt 0\).
  • The neutral point is the CG position at which \(C_{M\alpha} = 0\); the static margin is the CG's distance ahead of it: \(C_{M\alpha} = -C_{L\alpha}\,\text{SM}\).
  • Business jets and transports need 5% of the MAC at the aft CG; engines take 1% to 3% of it.
