Sizing the Wing Box

The bending moment of Lesson 7 has to be carried by the skins and stringers of the box of Lesson 2, in a material from Lesson 1, with the margins of Lesson 3. This lesson sizes the business jet's wing box along its span, compares the materials, and closes the module with the design loads it hands to the weight estimate.

Learning objectives

  • Turn a bending moment into the load per unit width on the box's upper and lower panels.
  • Size the lower panel by strength and the upper panel by buckling, and explain the role of the rib spacing.
  • Size the spar webs for the shear force.
  • Estimate the mass of a wing box's bending material and compare materials.

From bending moment to panel loads

The box's upper and lower panels, a mean depth \(h\) apart, carry the bending moment as a couple: a compressive force in the upper panel and an equal tension in the lower one. Spread over the box's width \(w\), the force per unit width is

\[ N = \frac{M_u}{h\,w} \]

with \(M_u\) the ultimate bending moment, 1.5 times the limit. The panels, skin and stringers together, have an equivalent thickness \(t = N/\sigma\) at the stress \(\sigma\) they can carry. The spar caps add to the panels near the spars; here they are counted in the panels.

The lower panel is in tension. Its stress is limited by the material's strength, \(\min(\sigma_u, 1.5\sigma_y)\) at ultimate load (Lesson 3), and in practice further by fatigue and crack growth, which a conceptual estimate leaves out. The upper panel is in compression and fails by buckling first. A stringer-stiffened panel of length \(L\) between ribs, with its skin and stringers in their best proportions, buckles at

Sizing the panels

\[ N = \frac{M_u}{hw}, \qquad t_{\text{lower}} = \frac{N}{\sigma_{\text{allow}}}, \qquad \sigma_{\text{upper}} = F\sqrt{\frac{NE}{L}}, \qquad t_{\text{upper}} = \frac{N}{\sigma_{\text{upper}}} \]

\(F\) is Farrar's efficiency factor, typically about 0.81 for a panel with Z-section stringers; \(\sigma_{\text{upper}}\) may not exceed the material's compressive allowable. The more load per unit length, \(N/L\), the higher the stress a panel can work at.

The buckling stress grows with \(\sqrt{E}\) and falls with the rib spacing: closer ribs make a lighter upper panel but add ribs. Take the business jet's ribs 0.6 m apart (Lesson 2), a typical spacing.

Example 8.1 — The panels at the side of the fuselage

The business jet's critical case is the gust at its zero-fuel weight: 226.9 kN·m and 80.4 kN at the side of the fuselage, at limit load (Lesson 7). The box there is 1.161 m wide and 0.279 m deep (Lesson 2). Size its 7075-T6 panels.

Show solution
\[ M_u = 1.5(226.9) = 340.4\ \text{kN·m}, \qquad N = \frac{340\,400}{0.2785(1.1606)} = 1.053\ \text{MN/m} \]

Lower panel, at \(\sigma_u = 572\) MPa:

\[ t_{\text{lower}} = \frac{1.053\times10^6}{572\times10^6} = 1.84\ \text{mm} \]

Upper panel:

\[ \sigma_{\text{upper}} = 0.81\sqrt{\frac{1.053\times10^6\,(71.7\times10^9)}{0.6}} = 287\ \text{MPa}, \qquad t_{\text{upper}} = 3.66\ \text{mm} \]

The upper panel works at half the lower panel's stress and needs twice its thickness. These are equivalent thicknesses of skin and stringers together: a real panel might be a 2 mm skin with stringers adding the rest.

The spar webs

The shear force is carried by the spar webs, the vertical walls of the box: with two webs of height \(h\), their total thickness is \(t_{\text{webs}} = V_u/(\tau_{\text{allow}}h)\). For aluminium the allowable shear stress is roughly 0.6 times the tensile strength.

Example 8.2 — The webs at the side of the fuselage

Size the two 7075-T6 spar webs for the 80.4 kN limit shear, with \(\tau_{\text{allow}} = 0.6(572) = 343\) MPa.

Show solution
\[ V_u = 1.5(80.4) = 120.6\ \text{kN}, \qquad t_{\text{webs}} = \frac{120\,600}{343\times10^6\,(0.2785)} = 1.26\ \text{mm} \]

About 0.6 mm each: less than the 1 to 1.5 mm a web needs to be handled, fastened and kept from buckling, so a practical minimum gauge sizes the business jet's webs. Small aircraft are full of parts sized by such minimums, not by their loads, which is one reason the statistics of Module 12 fit them better than a stress analysis.

The box along the span

Repeating the sizing at every station and adding up the panels' mass gives the box's bending material: \(m = 2\int \rho\,w\,(t_{\text{upper}} + t_{\text{lower}})\,dy\) over the half span, with the carry-through across the fuselage.

Example 8.3 — How heavy is the bending material?

Figure 8.1 gives 130.7 kg for the business jet's 7075-T6 box: 38.1 kg of lower panels and 92.6 kg of upper panels. Compare it with the wing's statistical weight in Module 12, 644 kg once calibrated.

Show solution
\[ \frac{130.7}{644} = 0.20 \]

About a fifth of the wing. The rest is the ribs, spar webs and caps at their minimum gauges, joints and fasteners, the leading and trailing edges, flaps, ailerons and their supports, fuel sealing and access panels: the parts a statistical equation captures and a bending analysis does not. That is why conceptual design estimates wing weight statistically, and why the bending material is the part that responds to the design loads.

The comparison confirms Lesson 1. The lower panel, a tie, favors high \(\sigma/\rho\); the upper panel, sized by buckling at this jet's light load intensity, favors low density: titanium and steel lose to aluminium, and quasi-isotropic carbon fiber saves about 40% before the allowances for impact damage and hot, wet conditions take much of that back. Aluminium-lithium saves 7% with no change of design philosophy. Carbon fiber laid all along the span is lightest of all, but a skin also carries shear and torsion and needs fibers in other directions; it suits spar caps and stringers. Spruce is as light as aluminium here, but needs a 28 mm upper panel: that is how wooden aircraft were built, with thick, lightly stressed structure. A real metal box puts 7075 on top and 2024 below, for its damage tolerance in tension: 137 kg.

These loads rest on the design as Modules 3 to 10 left it, and on assumptions that later modules revisit. Each of them would change the loads:

  • The weights (Module 12). The statistical weight equations take the gust's ultimate load factor, 4.882 rather than the maneuvering 4.573, which makes the wing and fuselage heavier. Module 12's wing weight, 644 kg against the 555 kg used in Lesson 7, adds a little relief.
  • The CG range (Module 12) and the tails (Module 13). Moving the wing and resizing the tail change the tail arm and the CG range, and with them every balanced tail load.
  • The takeoff weight and wing loading (Module 14). A heavier aircraft takes a lower \(n_{\text{limit}}\) from 25.337 [2], but a lower wing loading raises the gust load factor: Module 14's design loop recomputes \(N_z\) for each design, and its final design, at a lower \(W/S\), is even more gust-critical.
  • The choice of \(V_C\). The gust load factor grows with \(V_C\); 25.335 [2] sets its least value near 265 knots.

Key takeaways

  • The panels carry the bending as a couple: \(N = M_u/(hw)\) per unit width.
  • The lower panel is sized by strength (and fatigue): \(t = N/\sigma_{\text{allow}}\). The upper panel by buckling: \(\sigma = F\sqrt{NE/L}\), with \(F \approx 0.81\) and \(L\) the rib spacing.
  • The business jet's 7075-T6 box at the side of the fuselage: 1.84 mm lower, 3.66 mm upper; 131 kg of bending material, about a fifth of the wing's statistical weight. Its webs are at minimum gauge.
  • The design loads handed to Module 12: \(N_z = 4.882\) from the gust (4.573 from maneuvers) and \(N_l = 4.5\).
