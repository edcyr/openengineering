Wing Shear and Bending

The wing carries the aircraft's lift out to its tips and back to the fuselage as a cantilever. What it must be strong enough for is the bending moment where it meets the fuselage, and that depends not only on the lift but on how much weight the wing carries itself. This lesson finds the business jet's wing shear and bending, and the case that sizes it.

Learning objectives

  • Spread the wing's lift along the span with Schrenk's approximation.
  • Find the shear force and bending moment along the span, with the relief from the wing's structure and fuel.
  • Estimate the root bending moment by hand from the centroids of the lift and the masses.
  • Explain why the maximum zero-fuel weight is a structural limit, and find the business jet's critical wing case.

Loads along the span

Module 4 spread a wing's lift along its span with Schrenk's approximation [4]: the average of an elliptic distribution and one proportional to the chord. Per unit span, with \(L_w\) the wing's whole lift (Lesson 6) and \(\eta = 2y/b\),

\[ \ell(y) = \frac{L_w}{b}\,\frac{c(y) + c_e(y)}{2\bar c}, \qquad c_e(y) = \frac{4S}{\pi b}\sqrt{1 - \eta^2} \]

At a load factor \(n\), every kilogram of the wing's structure and of the fuel in its tanks presses down with \(n g\) times its mass: an inertia relief that opposes the lift. The net load per unit span is \(\ell(y) - n g\,m'(y)\), with \(m'\) the mass per unit span. Starting from the tip, where both are zero, the shear force and the bending moment at a station \(y\) are

Shear and bending along the span

\[ V(y) = \int_y^{b/2} \left[\ell(\eta) - n g\,m'(\eta)\right] d\eta, \qquad M(y) = \int_y^{b/2} V(\eta)\, d\eta \]

The wing box is sized at the side of the fuselage, \(y = r\), where it joins the carry-through; across the fuselage the moment changes little.

Take the business jet's wing structure as 8% of the takeoff weight, 555 kg, spread along the span in proportion to the chord, until Module 12 estimates it. Its wing tanks hold 1591 kg between the side of the fuselage and 85% of the half span, spread in proportion to the box's area, so to \(c^2\) (Lesson 2); the other 400 kg of the design fuel is in the center tank, inside the fuselage, where it relieves nothing. The tanks are used last, so they are full whenever the center tank holds fuel.

An estimate by hand

The bending at the side of the fuselage is each load outboard of it times its arm. For the business jet's planform, integrating once gives the centroids: 85.6% of each half wing's lift acts outboard of the side of the fuselage, 2.824 m from it; 84.7% of each half wing's structure, 2.837 m from it; and all the wing-tank fuel, 2.157 m from it, inboard because the box is deepest at the root. An elliptic lift would act at \(4(b/2)/3\pi\) from the centerline, 3.176 m against Schrenk's 3.159 m: close enough for a first estimate.

Example 7.1 — Point A at takeoff weight

At point A (\(n = 3.048\)), with the CG forward, the wing lifts \(L_w = 216.2\) kN (Lesson 6). Find the bending moment at the side of the fuselage.

Show solution

Lift: \(0.856(216.2/2) = 92.5\) kN at 2.824 m. Structure: \(0.847(555.4/2) = 235.3\) kg. Fuel: \(1591/2 = 795.5\) kg. Each weighs \(n g = 29.89\) N/kg:

\[ M = 92.5(2.824) - 235.3(29.89)(2.837) - 795.5(29.89)(2.157) \] \[ M = 261.3 - 20.0 - 51.3 = 190.0\ \text{kN·m} \]

The fuel relieves the lift's bending by 20%, the structure by 8%. The integration of Figure 7.1 gives 190.2 kN·m.

The critical case and the zero-fuel weight

Fuel in the wing reduces its bending; payload in the fuselage does not. An aircraft that has burned its fuel but still carries all its payload has the least relief for its lift. That is why aircraft have a maximum zero-fuel weight (MZFW): above it, the extra weight would have to be carried as fuel in the wings, not as payload. For the business jet, whose design payload is its maximum, the MZFW is its takeoff weight less its design fuel: 4952 kg.

Example 7.2 — The gust at zero-fuel weight

In the upward gust at 20 000 ft at its zero-fuel weight, the business jet pulls \(n = 4.083\) (Lesson 5) and, with the CG forward, its wing lifts 209.9 kN. Estimate the bending at the side of the fuselage.

Show solution
\[ M = 0.856(104.97)(2.824) - 235.3(4.083)(9.807)(2.837) \] \[ M = 253.7 - 26.7 = 227.0\ \text{kN·m} \]

Compare the gust at takeoff weight: its lift bends the wing by 282.6 kN·m, and the wing's structure and fuel take back 21.3 and 54.8. At zero-fuel weight the higher load factor does not make up for the lower weight: the lift's bending is 10% less, 253.7 kN·m. But only the structure is left to relieve it, so the net bending is 10% more than the gust at takeoff weight, and 19% more than maneuver A. This is the case that sizes the business jet's wing box (Lesson 8). Its shear, 80.4 kN, is also the largest.

The relief depends on where the masses are. Fuel and engines far out on the span relieve the most bending, which is one reason airliners hang their engines under the wing. The business jet's engines are on the fuselage, and give its wing no relief; its tip tanks, had it any, would. The wing's own mass of 555 kg is an estimate: Module 12's statement gives it 644 kg once calibrated, which gives a little more relief: 1.9% less bending in the zero-fuel gust.

The wing also twists. Its pitching moment, the flaps' and ailerons' loads, and the sweep, which puts the lift at the tip behind the root, make a torque the closed box carries as shear flow in its skins and webs. It rarely sizes a transport's box in conceptual design, but it sets the stiffness that keeps the ailerons effective and the wing free of flutter, which later design phases check.

Key takeaways

  • Shear and bending come from integrating the net load, lift less \(ngm'\), from the tip inward: \(V = \int(\ell - ngm')\,dy\), \(M = \int V\,dy\).
  • A hand estimate: each load outboard of the station times its arm. The business jet's lift acts 2.824 m outboard of the side of the fuselage; its wing fuel 2.157 m.
  • Fuel in the wing relieves bending; payload does not. The maximum zero-fuel weight limits the weight carried as payload.
  • The business jet's wing is sized by the upward gust at its zero-fuel weight: 226.9 kN·m and 80.4 kN at the side of the fuselage, at limit load.
