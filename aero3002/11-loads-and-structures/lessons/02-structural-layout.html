Structural Layout

A structure is light when every load finds a short, continuous path from where it is applied to where it is balanced. This lesson follows those paths through the wing, the fuselage and their attachments, and lays out the business jet's structure on the configuration of Module 6.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the semimonocoque structure and the job of each of its parts: skin, stringers, spars, ribs, frames and bulkheads.
  • Explain how the wing box carries bending, shear and torsion, and why the spars sit where they do.
  • Trace the load paths between the wing, fuselage, engines, tails and landing gear.
  • Find the dimensions of a wing box from the airfoil and the spar positions.

Load paths and the semimonocoque

Every load on an aircraft has to be carried somewhere: the lift on the wing to the weight of the fuselage and its contents, the thrust of the engines to the drag of the whole aircraft, the landing to the gear. A load path is the chain of parts that carries a load from where it is applied to where it is balanced. A good layout keeps load paths short and continuous, and ends them on heavy members where loads meet: a rib where the gear attaches, a frame where the wing does.

Almost every aircraft is a semimonocoque. A thin skin carries shear and, with the stringers riveted or bonded to it, tension and compression. Stringers also break the skin into narrow panels that do not buckle easily. Frames in the fuselage and ribs in the wing keep the section's shape, shorten the stringers' unsupported length, and spread concentrated loads into the skin. Damage tolerance asks for more than one path for each important load, so that a cracked part leaves the others to carry it until the crack is found: the wing box has two spars and many stringers, the fuselage many frames and stringers.

The wing box

The wing is a cantilever from the fuselage. Its lift, less the weight of its own structure and fuel, bends it upward, shears it, and twists it. These loads are carried by the wing box, the closed box between the front and rear spars:

  • Bending is carried by the upper and lower skin-stringer panels (and the spar caps): in flight the upper panel is compressed and the lower one stretched.
  • Shear is carried by the spar webs, the vertical plates between the caps.
  • Torsion, from the wing's pitching moment, the flaps and the ailerons, is carried by the closed box as a whole, as shear flow around its four walls.

The front spar sits at about 12 to 20% of the chord, behind the leading-edge slats or anti-icing; the rear spar at 55 to 70%, ahead of the flaps and ailerons. The box is deepest near 30% of the chord, where the airfoil is thickest, and as wide as the control surfaces allow. Module 7 put the business jet's spars at 15% and 60% of the chord and its fuel tanks inside the box, out to 85% of the half span, with a center tank in the box where it crosses the fuselage. On this low-wing jet the box passes under the cabin floor as the carry-through, and joins the fuselage at two main frames, one at each spar.

The wing box

\[ w = (x_{rs} - x_{fs})\,c, \qquad h \approx k\,(t/c)\,c, \qquad A_{\text{box}} = w h \]

\(x_{fs}\) and \(x_{rs}\) are the spar positions as fractions of the chord \(c\); \(k \approx 0.9\) turns the airfoil's maximum thickness \(t\) into the box's mean depth (Module 7). Lessons 7 and 8 use \(w\) and \(h\) to carry the bending moment.

Example 2.1 — The box at the side of the fuselage

The business jet's wing has a root chord of 2.772 m, a taper ratio of 0.35 and a span of 14.97 m, with \(t/c = 0.12\). Find the box's width, depth and area at the side of the fuselage, 0.80 m from the centerline, with the spars at 15% and 60%.

Show solution
\[ c = 2.772\left(1 - 0.65\,\frac{2(0.80)}{14.97}\right) = 2.579\ \text{m} \] \[ w = 0.45(2.579) = 1.161\ \text{m}, \qquad h = 0.9(0.12)(2.579) = 0.279\ \text{m} \] \[ A_{\text{box}} = 1.161(0.279) = 0.323\ \text{m}^2 \]

A box about 1.2 m wide and 0.28 m deep: the wing carries its bending on a depth of less than 30 cm. A thicker airfoil would make it lighter, which is why Module 4's statistics make the wing weight fall with \(t/c\).

The fuselage

The fuselage is a pressurized tube with holes in it. Its skin carries the cabin's pressure as hoop stress (Module 8) and, with the stringers, the bending of the fuselage between the wing and the tails and nose. Frames, typically about 0.5 m apart, keep the section round and carry the floor beams; heavier main frames take the wing's spars, the engine mounts and the tail spars. The pressurized cabin is closed by a flat front pressure bulkhead behind the radome and a domed aft pressure bulkhead behind the cabin; a dome carries pressure in tension, like the tube, and is lighter than a flat bulkhead. Doors and windows are holes in a loaded skin: each needs a surround of thicker skin, doublers and heavier frames and longerons to carry the loads around it.

Example 2.2 — The aft pressure bulkhead

Module 8 found a pressure differential of 55.93 kPa at the business jet's cruise. What force does the cabin pressure put on the aft pressure bulkhead, if it closes the 1.448 m inner diameter of the cabin?

Show solution
\[ F = \Delta p\,\frac{\pi d_I^2}{4} = 55\,930\,\frac{\pi(1.448)^2}{4} = 55\,930(1.646) = 92.1\ \text{kN} \]

The weight of more than nine tonnes, on every flight, carried around the bulkhead's edge into the fuselage's skin and frames. A dome carries it as membrane tension; a flat bulkhead must carry it in bending, and needs stiffeners.

Attachments

The heaviest structure is where components meet, so the layout lines them up with each other. The business jet's rear-mounted engines hang on pylons attached to two main frames of the tail cone, behind the aft pressure bulkhead, so that their thrust and weight, and the loads of an engine failure, go straight into the fuselage. The horizontal and vertical tails have spars of their own, which end on main frames of the tail cone. The main landing gear sits behind the wing's rear spar, where Module 8 placed it for tip-back, and is carried by a gear beam from the rear spar to a fuselage frame; the nose gear is carried by the floor and the frames ahead of the cabin.

None of this changes the external layout of Module 6, but it is checked against it: the rear spar must leave room for flaps of the chord used in Module 12, the gear bay must not cut the box, and the carry-through must pass under the cabin floor. A structural layout that cannot be built is a reason to go back to the configuration.

Key takeaways

  • A semimonocoque carries loads in its skin and stringers, with frames and ribs keeping the shape, breaking up panels and spreading concentrated loads; damage tolerance asks for several load paths.
  • The wing box carries bending in its skin-stringer panels, shear in its spar webs and torsion as a closed box. The business jet's spars are at 15% and 60% of the chord.
  • Box width \(w = (x_{rs} - x_{fs})c\), mean depth \(h \approx 0.9(t/c)c\); at the side of the business jet's fuselage, 1.161 m by 0.279 m.
  • The fuselage is a pressurized tube closed by bulkheads, with main frames where the wing, engines and tails attach, and reinforcement around every cutout.
