The Center of Gravity

Module 8 placed the landing gear for a center of gravity assumed to lie between 15% and 35% of the mean aerodynamic chord. With every group's weight known, the CG can now be found: give each group a position, take moments, and divide. For the business jet the answer is a surprise.

Learning objectives

  • Find an aircraft's center of gravity from its weight statement with a moment table.
  • Place each group's weight with rules of thumb, and say which are judgment.
  • Express a CG position as a percentage of the mean aerodynamic chord.
  • Explain what moves the CG, and why a rear-engined aircraft's empty CG lies far aft.

Taking moments

Measure every station \(x\) aft from a datum, here the nose. The CG is where the whole weight could act with the same moment as all its parts:

Center of gravity

\[ x_{cg} = \frac{\sum W_i x_i}{\sum W_i}, \qquad \%\,\text{MAC} = 100\,\frac{x_{cg} - x_{\text{LE,MAC}}}{\cbar} \]

\(x_{\text{LE,MAC}}\) is the station of the mean aerodynamic chord's leading edge and \(\cbar\) its length. The business jet's: 6.471 m and 2.015 m (Module 4's wing at Module 6's position).

Stability and control are set relative to the wing's aerodynamic center, near the quarter-chord of the MAC, so CG positions and limits are quoted as a percentage of the MAC rather than in meters.

Where each group sits

Some positions come from the layout itself: the landing gear legs, the engines, the seats. The rest come from rules of thumb, or from judgment where no rule fits:

The empty business jet balances at 52.1% of the MAC: well behind the 35% that Module 8 assumed as the aft limit.

Why the empty CG is so far aft

Moments, not weights, set the CG. The engines, nacelles and starters are 15% of the empty weight, but they hang 4.1 m behind the CG, at 75% of the fuselage, and the tails and air conditioning add more at the back. Forward of the CG there is little to balance them: the nose gear, the avionics, the cockpit. A rear-engined aircraft, with nothing on the wing, is tail-heavy when empty. Module 6 placed the wing from a statistic for aircraft in general; it did not know where this aircraft's weight is.

The empty aircraft is only the start: the crew, the passengers and the fuel all sit ahead of 7.5 m, and every one of them moves the CG forward. Lesson 8 follows it through loading. But no loading can move the empty aircraft's 52.1% into the range, and an aircraft that must be flyable empty, or with just its crew, needs a CG well ahead of where the wing would make it unstable (Module 12).

Example 7.2 — Moving the engines

How far does the empty CG move if the engines, nacelles and starters (579.6 kg) move 0.5 m forward?

Show solution
\[ \Delta x_{cg} = \frac{579.6(-0.5)}{3972.4} = -0.0730\ \text{m}, \qquad \frac{-0.0730}{2.015} = -3.6\%\ \text{MAC} \]

Half a meter of engine moves the CG only 3.6% of the MAC; and the engines cannot move far forward without their exhaust reaching the wing or the cabin. Moving the wing aft carries the wing's own weight with it, but it moves the MAC, the reference, by the full distance: that is the designer's strongest lever.

Key takeaways

  • \(x_{cg} = \sum W_ix_i/\sum W_i\); CG positions and limits are given as a percentage of the MAC.
  • Positions come from the layout (gear, engines, seats), from rules of thumb (wing at 30% MAC, fuselage at 45% of its length) or from judgment.
  • The business jet's empty CG is at 7.520 m, 52.1% of the MAC: its rear engines make it tail-heavy.
  • Moving the wing moves the reference by the full distance: it is the strongest lever on the CG as a share of the MAC.
