Load Factors

A structure's weight depends on the loads it must carry. Module 10 found the business jet's design loads; the statistical weight equations of Lessons 3 to 5 take them as two numbers, the ultimate load factor and the landing load factor. This lesson collects them, shows how much each group weight depends on them, and measures what the business jet's gust case changes.

Learning objectives

  • State the ultimate and landing load factors the weight equations use, and where they come from.
  • Find the limit maneuvering load factor of a transport aircraft from its takeoff weight, as the sizing loop changes it.
  • Explain how the load factors enter each group weight, and estimate the effect of a change.
  • Explain what the gust load factor changes in the business jet's weight statement, before and after calibration.

The load factors from Module 10

Module 10 set out the loads a structure is designed for. Its limit loads are the largest expected in service; its ultimate loads, 1.5 times larger (14 CFR 25.303), are the loads it must carry without breaking, and the ones a structure is sized for. The weight equations therefore take the ultimate load factor \(N_z = 1.5\,n\) at the design gross weight \(W_{dg}\), here the takeoff weight, with \(n\) the largest limit load factor of the flight envelope.

Two limits compete for that \(n\). The limit maneuvering load factor of 25.337 depends only on the design takeoff weight:

The limit maneuvering load factor

\[ n_{\text{limit}} = 2.1 + \frac{24\,000}{W + 10\,000}, \qquad 2.5 \le n_{\text{limit}} \le 3.8 \]

\(W\) is the design maximum takeoff weight in pounds. As the sizing loop changes the takeoff weight, it changes \(n_{\text{limit}}\) with it: heavier transports are designed for less, down to 2.5 above about 23 tonnes.

The gust load factor depends on the wing loading, the speeds and the altitude. For the business jet Module 10 found that a gust at its design cruising speed at 20 000 ft gives \(n = 3.255\) at its takeoff weight, more than the maneuvering 3.048: its design load factor is set by the gust, and this module uses it. The other three aircraft of this module, whose gust loads Module 10 did not find, keep the maneuvering factor.

Example 2.1 — The business jet's load factors

Find the business jet's limit maneuvering load factor and the ultimate load factor from it at \(\Wo = 6943\) kg, and compare them with Module 10's gust case.

Show solution
\[ W = \frac{6943}{0.4536} = 15\,307\ \text{lb}, \qquad n_{\text{limit}} = 2.1 + \frac{24\,000}{25\,307} = 3.048 \] \[ N_z = 1.5(3.048) = 4.573, \qquad N_{z,\text{gust}} = 1.5(3.255) = 4.882 \]

The gust's ultimate load factor is 6.8% higher, so it is the one the weight equations take: the business jet's structure is designed for 30% more load factor than an airliner's maneuvering 2.5.

The landing gear and the structure around it are sized by the landing instead. The gear must absorb a limit descent velocity of 10 ft/s (3.05 m/s) at the design landing weight (14 CFR 25.473); Module 8 sized the business jet's shock absorbers for a gear load factor \(N_{\text{gear}} = 3\). The gear equations take the ultimate value \(N_l = 1.5\,N_{\text{gear}} = 4.5\), with the landing weight \(W_l = 0.9\Wo = 6249\) kg.

Exponents near 0.5 mean that a 10% higher load factor makes the wing and fuselage about 5% heavier: only the bending material of the structure (Module 10, Lesson 8) responds to the load, while minimum gauges, joints, systems and furnishings do not.

Example 2.2 — What the gust changed

Lessons 3 to 8 build the business jet's statement with Module 10's gust load factor, \(N_z = 4.882\). Compare it with the statement the maneuvering 4.573 alone would give.

Show solution

Before calibration the gust makes the wing 559.4 kg instead of 539.3, the fuselage 1010.7 instead of 978.1 and the vertical tail 52.2 instead of 50.4, and adds about 1 kg each to the horizontal tail and the nacelles: the empty weight is 3503.8 kg instead of 3448.0, 55.8 kg or 1.6% more.

Lesson 6 then calibrates the statement to Module 3's empty weight of 3972.4 kg:

\[ k = \frac{3972.4 - 401.6}{3102.2} = 1.151 \quad (1.172\ \text{with}\ N_z = 4.573\ \text{alone}) \]

The calibrated empty weight does not change at all. The wing gains 11.7 kg, the fuselage 16.8 kg and the vertical tail 1.0 kg; every other group loses a little; and the empty CG moves 5 mm aft, from 51.80 to 52.05% of the MAC.

The calibration absorbs the change because it scales every statistical group to the empty weight of aircraft like this one, whatever their design loads were. Without calibration, as in the sizing loop at the end of Lesson 6, the extra structure goes around the loop: the uncalibrated business jet closes at 5743 kg instead of 5683. Lessons 3 to 8 use the gust's 4.882, and Modules 12 and 13 start from their numbers. The narrow-body, the turboprop and the commuter keep the maneuvering factor in Lesson 6 and in the Weight and Balance tool; a gust case would change their statements in the same way.

Key takeaways

  • The weight equations take the ultimate load factor \(N_z = 1.5\,n\) at the design gross weight, \(n\) the largest of the maneuvering and gust limits, and the landing load factor \(N_l = 1.5\,N_{\text{gear}}\) at the landing weight.
  • \(n_{\text{limit}} = 2.1 + 24\,000/(W + 10\,000)\), \(W\) in pounds, from 2.5 to 3.8: 3.048 for the business jet. Its gust gives 3.255 (Module 10), so its weights use \(N_z = 4.882\), not 4.573.
  • The wing and fuselage weights go as about the square root of \(N_z\); systems and furnishings not at all.
  • The gust's \(N_z\) adds 55.8 kg (1.6%) to the raw empty weight; calibrated, \(k\) falls from 1.172 to 1.151, the empty weight is unchanged, the wing and fuselage gain 11.7 and 16.8 kg and the CG moves 5 mm aft. The other three aircraft keep the maneuvering factor.
