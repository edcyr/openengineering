Design Loads and Margins of Safety

A structure designed for exactly the loads it will meet would break the first time a pilot pulled a little harder or a part came out a little weaker. This lesson sets out how the regulations turn the loads expected in service into the loads a structure is designed for, and how a designer shows that each part is strong enough.

Learning objectives

  • Define the load factor and find it in a level turn and a pull-up.
  • Distinguish limit and ultimate loads, and apply the factor of safety and the special factors.
  • Explain what a material allowable is, and why it is lower than a typical strength.
  • Compute margins of safety at limit and ultimate load, and say which one governs.

The load factor

The load factor \(n\) is the aerodynamic force normal to the aircraft's longitudinal axis divided by its weight (14 CFR 25.321), which for most purposes is the lift divided by the weight. It is 1 in level flight. In a level turn at a bank angle \(\phi\) the lift must also turn the aircraft, and in a pull-up of radius \(R\) at a speed \(V\) it must also bend the flight path:

\[ n_{\text{turn}} = \frac{1}{\cos\phi}, \qquad n_{\text{pull-up}} = 1 + \frac{V^2}{gR} \]

Every mass in the aircraft feels the load factor: at \(n = 3\) the wing carries three times its 1-g lift, and the engines, seats and fuel press on their attachments with three times their weight. That is why loads are stated as load factors: one number scales the whole aircraft.

Limit and ultimate loads

The regulations state the loads a structure must carry in two levels (14 CFR 25.301 to 25.305):

  • Limit loads are the largest loads expected in service. The structure must carry them without detrimental permanent deformation, and its deflection at limit load must not interfere with safe operation. Unless a rule says otherwise, every load it prescribes is a limit load.
  • Ultimate loads are the limit loads times a factor of safety of 1.5 (25.303). The structure must carry them for at least 3 seconds without failing.

The factor of safety covers what the analysis cannot: loads a little above the limit, scatter in the materials and the manufacturing, and the approximations of the analysis itself. Where a part's strength is less certain, it is multiplied by a special factor (25.619): a fitting factor of at least 1.15 for fittings and joints not proven by test (25.625), casting factors of 1.25 to 2.0 depending on how the casting is inspected (25.621), and bearing factors for parts with clearance that may be hammered by vibration (25.623).

Example 3.1 — The bank angle at the limit load factor

At what bank angle does a level turn reach the business jet's limit maneuvering load factor of 3.048, and its ultimate load factor?

Show solution
\[ \phi = \cos^{-1}\frac{1}{3.048} = 70.8^\circ, \qquad N_z = 1.5(3.048) = 4.573 \]

A turn at 4.573 g would need \(\phi = \cos^{-1}(1/4.573) = 77.4^\circ\), which the wing could make only well above \(V_A\) (Lesson 4). An airliner, at \(n = 2.5\), reaches its limit at a bank of only \(66.4^\circ\); passengers would object long before.

Allowables and margins of safety

The strengths a designer may count on are not the typical values of Lesson 1 but allowables: statistically lower values that almost every piece of the material will exceed. 14 CFR 25.613 asks for a value that 99% of the material exceeds, with 95% confidence, where a single part's failure would lose the structure (commonly called an A-basis value), and 90% with 95% confidence where other parts would carry the load (a B-basis value). Allowables also take into account the temperature and, for composites, the moisture the part will see. Typical values in this module are used only to compare materials.

A part is strong enough when its stress, at the load being checked, does not exceed the allowable. The margin of safety says by how much:

The margin of safety

\[ MS = \frac{\text{allowable}}{\text{applied stress} \times \text{factors}} - 1 \ \ge 0 \]

At limit load the applied stress is checked against the yield strength \(\sigma_y\); at ultimate load (the limit stress times 1.5 and any special factor) against the ultimate strength \(\sigma_u\). \(MS = 0\) is just strong enough; a negative margin is a failure of the design.

Which check governs depends on the material. Limit stress below \(\sigma_y\) means ultimate stress below \(1.5\,\sigma_y\), so the ultimate check governs whenever \(\sigma_u < 1.5\,\sigma_y\), that is when \(\sigma_y/\sigma_u > 2/3\). For 2024-T3, \(\sigma_y/\sigma_u = 0.71\); for 7075-T6, 0.88; for Ti-6Al-4V, 0.93. For the alloys aircraft are built from, the ultimate check governs: the stress allowed at ultimate load is \(\min(\sigma_u,\ 1.5\,\sigma_y)\).

Example 3.2 — A lower wing skin

At limit load the lower skin of a wing, in 2024-T3 (\(\sigma_y = 345\), \(\sigma_u = 483\) MPa, typical), is stressed to 300 MPa. Find its margins of safety at limit and at ultimate load.

Show solution
\[ MS_{\text{limit}} = \frac{345}{300} - 1 = 0.150, \qquad MS_{\text{ult}} = \frac{483}{1.5(300)} - 1 = 0.073 \]

Both positive; the ultimate margin is the smaller, as expected for 2024-T3. In practice a lower wing skin is held to a lower stress than this by fatigue and crack growth: its static margin would be large.

Example 3.3 — A fitting

A machined 7075-T6 fitting carries a limit load of 40 kN in tension. What net area does it need at the ultimate strength of 572 MPa, with the fitting factor?

Show solution
\[ A = \frac{1.5(1.15)(40\,000)}{572\times10^6} = 120.6\ \text{mm}^2 \]

15% more than the same part away from a joint. Joints and fittings are where structures usually fail, and where they gather weight.

Other design loads

Flight loads are the subject of the rest of this module, but they are not the only ones. The landing gear and the structure around it are sized by landings and ground handling (Module 8); the engine mounts by the engine's thrust, torque and a seized engine; the control surfaces by their hinge moments. A pressurized fuselage must carry the cabin's pressure differential, together with the flight loads, and on its own at a higher value: the pressure differential at the maximum setting of the relief valve times 1.33, for aircraft certified to fly up to 45 000 ft (25.365(d)). That is a limit load, so the factor of safety applies on top of it.

Example 3.4 — The fuselage skin under pressure

Module 8 found a pressure differential of 55.93 kPa at the business jet's cruise. Take the relief valve set at 59.3 kPa (8.6 psi, an assumed value). Find the hoop stress at ultimate load in a 1.2 mm skin of 0.80 m radius, and its margin in 2024-T3.

Show solution
\[ p_{\text{ult}} = 1.5(1.33)(59.3) = 118.3\ \text{kPa} \] \[ \sigma = \frac{p_{\text{ult}}\,r}{t} = \frac{118\,300(0.80)}{0.0012} = 78.9\ \text{MPa} \] \[ MS = \frac{483}{78.9} - 1 = 5.1 \]

An enormous static margin. Fuselage skins are not sized by this case but by fatigue and crack growth under one pressure cycle per flight, and by damage tolerance: a skin cracked between two frames must still hold the normal pressure times 1.15 (25.571). That is why Module 8 found real skins thicker than the pressure alone needs.

Key takeaways

  • The load factor \(n\) is the lift over the weight: \(1/\cos\phi\) in a level turn, \(1 + V^2/gR\) in a pull-up. Every mass in the aircraft feels it.
  • Limit loads, the largest expected in service, must not deform the structure permanently; ultimate loads, 1.5 times larger, must not break it for 3 seconds. Special factors (fittings 1.15, castings 1.25 to 2.0) multiply the 1.5.
  • Allowables are statistical lower values: 99% with 95% confidence for single load paths, 90% for redundant structure.
  • \(MS = \text{allowable}/(\text{stress}\times\text{factors}) - 1 \ge 0\). For aircraft alloys (\(\sigma_y/\sigma_u > 2/3\)) the ultimate check governs.
