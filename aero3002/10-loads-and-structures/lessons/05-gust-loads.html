Gust Loads

A pilot can choose not to pull hard; nobody can choose not to meet turbulence. A vertical gust changes the wing's angle of attack in an instant, and at high speed the load factor it gives can exceed anything the pilot may pull. This lesson computes gust load factors, draws the gust envelope, and finds that for the business jet the gusts, not the maneuvers, set the design load factor.

Learning objectives

  • Derive the load factor of a sharp-edged vertical gust.
  • Compute the mass ratio, the gust alleviation factor and the gust load factor with Pratt's formula.
  • Draw the gust envelope with the derived gust velocities, and find the speed \(V_B\) and the least \(V_C\).
  • Compare gust and maneuvering load factors across weights and altitudes, and find which governs.

The sharp-edged gust

An aircraft flying at a true airspeed \(V\) into an upward gust of velocity \(U\) sees its angle of attack rise by \(\Delta\alpha = U/V\). Its lift rises by \(\Delta L = \tfrac12\rho V^2 S\,a\,(U/V) = \tfrac12\rho V U S a\), with \(a\) the lift-curve slope per radian, and its load factor by

\[ \Delta n = \frac{\rho V U a}{2(W/S)} = \frac{\rho_0 V_E U_E a}{2(W/S)} \]

with \(W/S\) in newtons per square meter. The second form uses equivalent speeds, \(V_E = V\sqrt{\sigma}\) and \(U_E = U\sqrt{\sigma}\), the way the regulations state them. The gust load grows with the speed, the gust and the lift-curve slope, and falls with the wing loading: a light, lightly loaded aircraft with a high aspect ratio feels turbulence most.

Pratt's formula

A real gust builds up over a distance, and as it does the aircraft starts to rise with it, which takes back part of the angle of attack. The gust alleviation factor \(K_g\) allows for both. It depends on the mass ratio \(\mu\), the aircraft's mass per unit area against the mass of air it disturbs: a heavy aircraft responds slowly and feels more of the gust.

The gust load factor (Pratt's formula)

\[ n = 1 \pm \frac{K_g\,\rho_0\,U_{de}\,V_E\,a}{2(W/S)}, \qquad K_g = \frac{0.88\,\mu}{5.3 + \mu}, \qquad \mu = \frac{2(W/S)}{\rho\,\bar c\,a\,g} \]

\(U_{de}\) is the derived gust velocity (equivalent), \(V_E\) the equivalent airspeed, \(\rho\) the air density at the altitude, \(\bar c = S/b\) the mean geometric chord and \(a\) the lift-curve slope at the flight Mach number. In US units, with \(V_E\) in knots, \(U_{de}\) in ft/s and \(W/S\) in lb/ft², it is the form in the regulations: \(\Delta n = K_g U_{de} V_E a/(498\,W/S)\).

The derived gust velocities are what the formula needs to match measured accelerations. The rules for light aircraft (14 CFR 23.333(c), until 2017) set them, at altitudes up to 20 000 ft:

  • 50 ft/s (15.24 m/s) at \(V_C\), falling linearly to 25 ft/s at 50 000 ft;
  • 25 ft/s at \(V_D\), falling to 12.5 ft/s;
  • 66 ft/s at \(V_B\) (commuter category), falling to 38 ft/s; the gust load factors vary linearly with speed between \(V_C\) and \(V_D\).

Today's rule for transport aircraft, 14 CFR 25.341, asks instead for a dynamic analysis of the aircraft's response to "1 − cosine" gusts with gradients from 30 to 350 ft, with a reference gust velocity of 56 ft/s at sea level, 44 ft/s at 15 000 ft and 20.86 ft/s at 60 000 ft, and of continuous turbulence. That needs the aircraft's mass distribution and flexibility, which conceptual design does not yet have, so it uses Pratt's quasi-static formula with the derived gust velocities.

Example 5.1 — The business jet in a gust at sea level

The business jet at its takeoff weight, \(W/S = 248.0\) kg/m² (2432 N/m²), meets a 50 ft/s gust at \(V_C = 138.9\) m/s at sea level (Mach 0.408). Its wing (\(A = 8\), \(\bar c = S/b = 1.871\) m) has \(a = 4.720\) per radian at that Mach number, from Module 4's formula. Find \(\mu\), \(K_g\) and the gust load factor.

Show solution
\[ \mu = \frac{2(248.0)}{1.225(1.871)(4.720)} = 45.8, \qquad K_g = \frac{0.88(45.8)}{5.3 + 45.8} = 0.789 \] \[ \Delta n = \frac{0.789(1.225)(15.24)(138.9)(4.720)}{2(2432)} = 1.985 \]

\(n = 2.985\): just under the maneuvering limit of 3.048. (The mass ratio is written with \(W/S\) in kg/m², where the \(g\) cancels.)

Gusts at altitude

The derived gust velocity stays at 50 ft/s up to 20 000 ft, and in equivalent airspeed so does \(V_C\). But as the air thins, the same equivalent airspeed is a higher Mach number, which steepens the lift-curve slope; and the mass ratio rises, so \(K_g\) approaches its limit of 0.88. The gust load factor therefore grows with altitude, until the gust velocity starts to fall above 20 000 ft and \(M_C\) reduces \(V_C\).

Example 5.2 — At 20 000 ft

Repeat Example 5.1 at 20 000 ft (6096 m), where \(\rho = 0.6527\) kg/m³ and \(a_{\text{sound}} = 316.0\) m/s. \(V_C\) is still 138.9 m/s equivalent.

Show solution
\[ V = \frac{138.9}{\sqrt{0.6527/1.225}} = 190.3\ \text{m/s}, \qquad M = 0.602, \qquad a = 5.127 \] \[ \mu = \frac{2(248.0)}{0.6527(1.871)(5.127)} = 79.2, \qquad K_g = 0.825 \] \[ \Delta n = \frac{0.825(1.225)(15.24)(138.9)(5.127)}{2(2432)} = 2.255 \]

\(n = 3.255\), 7% above the maneuvering limit. At its takeoff weight, the business jet's gust load factor exceeds 3.048 from about 1.8 km up to 10.1 km, just above the altitude where \(M_C\) begins to cut \(V_C\).

The gust envelope and the design speed \(V_B\)

Drawn on the V-n diagram, each gust velocity gives a pair of straight lines from \(n = 1\) at zero speed, slightly curved where the lift-curve slope changes with the Mach number. Their values at \(V_B\), \(V_C\) and \(V_D\), joined, make the gust envelope. The structure must carry both envelopes; the flight envelope is their combination.

\(V_B\), the design speed for maximum gust intensity, is the speed at which the aircraft can meet the strongest gusts without stalling. 25.335(d) gives its least value as the speed at which the stall curve reaches the gust load factor at \(V_C\), with the reference gust velocity:

\[ V_B \ge V_{S1}\sqrt{1 + \frac{K_g\,\rho_0\,U_{\text{ref}}\,V_C\,a}{2(W/S)}} \]

and 25.335(a) asks that \(V_C\) be at least \(V_B + 1.32\,U_{\text{ref}}\), with the speeds in knots and \(U_{\text{ref}}\) in ft/s: about 74 knots above \(V_B\) at sea level, so that an upset in severe turbulence does not carry the aircraft from \(V_B\) past \(V_C\). This is the check on the business jet's choice of \(V_C\) promised in Lesson 4.

Example 5.3 — Is 270 knots enough for \(V_C\)?

At sea level and takeoff weight, \(V_{S1} = 55.16\) m/s (Lesson 4) and \(U_{\text{ref}} = 56\) ft/s. With the numbers of Example 5.1, find the least \(V_B\) and check \(V_C = 270\) knots.

Show solution

The gust term scales with the gust velocity: \(1.985(56/50) = 2.224\). Then

\[ V_B = 55.16\sqrt{1 + 2.224} = 99.0\ \text{m/s} = 192.5\ \text{knots} \] \[ V_B + 1.32\,U_{\text{ref}} = 192.5 + 1.32(56) = 266.4\ \text{knots} \le 270 \]

270 knots passes. Because \(V_B\) itself grows with \(V_C\), the least \(V_C\) that satisfies the rule is about 265 knots: business jets cannot choose a low \(V_C\) to escape gust loads.

What the gusts mean for the design

For the business jet, the largest load factor at takeoff weight is the gust's: 3.255 at \(V_C\) at 20 000 ft, against 3.048 for maneuvers. Its design limit load factor at takeoff weight is therefore 3.255, and its ultimate load factor 4.882 rather than 4.573. Aircraft with moderate wing loadings and high cruising speeds, like business and regional jets, are often gust-critical: the gust load factor grows with \(V_C/(W/S)\).

At lighter weights the gust load factor is larger still, 4.083 at the zero-fuel weight, but the lift \(nW\) is smaller: \(4.083(4952)(9.807) = 198\) kN against \(3.255(6943)(9.807) = 222\) kN at takeoff weight. The design load factor, and the ultimate load factor of the weight equations, is the one at the design takeoff weight, where \(nW\) is largest. But which weight is critical depends on the part. Where a part's load is its own mass times \(n\), such as an engine mount, a seat or the floor under the payload, the lighter aircraft's higher load factor governs, which is why 25.321 asks for every weight from the lightest to the heaviest. The wing's bending also depends on the fuel it carries, which Lesson 7 shows makes the zero-fuel gust the wing's design case. Module 11's weight equations take the gust's \(N_z = 4.882\), and Module 13 recomputes it for each design's wing loading.

Key takeaways

  • A sharp-edged gust: \(\Delta n = \rho_0 V_E U_E a/(2W/S)\). Pratt's formula multiplies it by \(K_g = 0.88\mu/(5.3 + \mu)\), \(\mu = 2(W/S)/(\rho\bar c a g)\).
  • Derived gust velocities: 66, 50 and 25 ft/s at \(V_B\), \(V_C\) and \(V_D\) up to 20 000 ft. Transport rules now use a dynamic tuned-gust analysis.
  • \(V_B \ge V_{S1}\sqrt{n_g}\) and \(V_C \ge V_B + 1.32U_{\text{ref}}\): about 265 knots for the business jet, which uses 270.
  • The business jet's gust load factor reaches 3.255 at 20 000 ft at takeoff weight, above the maneuvering 3.048: its ultimate load factor is 4.882. Lighter weights see higher load factors but less lift.
