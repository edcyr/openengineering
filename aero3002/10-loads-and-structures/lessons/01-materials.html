Aircraft Materials

Every kilogram of structure is a kilogram of payload or fuel the aircraft cannot carry, so its materials are chosen for what they do per kilogram. This lesson compares the materials aircraft are built from, shows which property matters for each kind of part, and places them on the business jet.

Learning objectives

  • List the properties an aircraft structure asks of its materials, and why fatigue and damage tolerance matter as much as strength.
  • Compare aluminium alloys, titanium, steel and composites by their specific strength and stiffness.
  • Derive and use the material index for a tie and for a panel that must not buckle.
  • Explain where each material goes on an aircraft, and why.

What the structure needs

An aircraft structure must carry its loads without breaking (strength) and without deflecting or buckling too much (stiffness), and it must do so for tens of thousands of flights. That adds properties a building rarely needs:

  • Low density, so that each part is light for the load it carries.
  • Fatigue strength: every flight is one load cycle on the wing and one pressure cycle on the fuselage, and gusts add many smaller ones.
  • Damage tolerance: a crack, a dent or a corroded patch must grow slowly enough to be found at an inspection, and the damaged part must still carry its loads until then. A tough material, with a high fracture toughness, tolerates longer cracks.
  • Resistance to corrosion and to the temperatures the part sees, from −55 °C in cruise to several hundred degrees near an engine.
  • A cost, and a way of making and repairing the part, that the program can afford.

The yield strength \(\sigma_y\) is the stress at which a metal starts to deform permanently (by convention at 0.2% permanent strain), the ultimate tensile strength \(\sigma_u\) the stress at which it breaks, and the Young's modulus \(E\) the slope of its stress-strain curve while it is still elastic. Lesson 3 shows how the regulations use the first two.

The materials

Wood and fabric built the first aircraft; steel tube took over their fuselages in the 1920s, and in the 1930s the all-metal aluminium semimonocoque, a thin skin stiffened by frames and stringers that carries the loads itself, became the standard it still is. Composites entered on fairings and control surfaces and, with the Boeing 787 and Airbus A350, now make about half of an airliner's structure by weight, with titanium another 14 to 15%.

Specific properties and material indices

A material's specific strength \(\sigma_u/\rho\) and specific stiffness \(E/\rho\) compare it per kilogram. The specific stiffness of aluminium, titanium and steel is the same to within a few percent, about \(25\times10^6\) m²/s² (25 MJ/kg): no metal makes a stiffer tie for its weight. Carbon fiber, laid along the load, is four times better.

Which property wins depends on the part. A tie carrying a force \(F\) over a length \(L\) needs an area \(F/\sigma\), so its mass is \(m = \rho L F/\sigma\): the lightest tie has the largest \(\sigma/\rho\). A flat panel of width \(b\) and length \(L\) in compression buckles at \(\sigma_{cr} = kE(t/b)^2\), so it carries \(P = \sigma_{cr}tb = kEt^3/b\). For a given \(P\) its thickness is \(t = (Pb/kE)^{1/3}\) and its mass \(m = \rho bLt\):

Material indices: the lightest part has the largest index

\[ \text{tie: } \frac{\sigma}{\rho}, \qquad \text{beam: } \frac{\sigma^{2/3}}{\rho}, \qquad \text{panel in buckling: } \frac{E^{1/3}}{\rho} \]

The beam's index is for a beam of given shape scaled in size to carry a bending moment at a stress \(\sigma\); with \(E\) in place of \(\sigma\), the indices of a stiff tie, beam and panel are \(E/\rho\), \(E^{1/2}/\rho\) and \(E^{1/3}/\rho\). Masses compare as the inverse of the indices.

The smaller the exponent on the property, the more density matters. That is why a thin panel in compression, the upper skin of a wing, favors light materials: a material half as dense can be eight times less stiff and still make a panel of the same weight.

Example 1.1 — A tie

A tie 1 m long must carry an ultimate load of 50 kN at its ultimate tensile strength. Compare its area and mass in 7075-T6, Ti-6Al-4V, steel and carbon fiber laid along the load.

Show solution

\(A = F/\sigma_u\) and \(m = \rho A L\). For 7075-T6:

\[ A = \frac{50\,000}{572\times10^6} = 87.4\ \text{mm}^2, \qquad m = 2810(87.4\times10^{-6})(1) = 0.246\ \text{kg} \]

Titanium: 52.6 mm², 0.233 kg. Steel: 26.3 mm², 0.207 kg. Carbon fiber: 40.3 mm², 0.064 kg. For a tie, steel is the lightest metal and carbon fiber is a quarter of aluminium's mass. Steel wins only where a part must be small as well as strong, like a landing-gear leg or a bolt.

Example 1.2 — A skin panel in compression

Compare the mass of a compression panel that must not buckle under a given load, in the materials of Table 1.1, against 7075-T6.

Show solution

Mass goes as \(\rho/E^{1/3}\). For 7075-T6, \(71.7^{1/3}/2.81 = 1.478\), with \(\rho\) in tonnes per cubic meter (only the ratios matter):

\[ \frac{m_{\text{Ti}}}{m_{7075}} = \frac{1.478}{110^{1/3}/4.43} = \frac{1.478}{1.082} = 1.37 \]

Steel 1.97, 2024-T3 0.98, aluminium-lithium 0.92, quasi-isotropic carbon fiber 0.60, spruce 0.27. For panels the dense metals lose and the light materials win: the reason the first aircraft were wooden, and sailplanes are built of foam-cored composite. Spruce is not used on an airliner because the panel would be several centimeters thick, too weak where the load is concentrated, and not durable enough.

Metals, composites and where they go

Strength and stiffness per kilogram are only the start. Aluminium alloys are cheap to make and repair, well understood, and fail gradually: they yield before they break and their cracks grow slowly and predictably. 2024-T3 has the better fatigue and crack-growth properties and the higher toughness, so it goes where the structure is in tension: the fuselage skin, which the cabin pressure loads once a flight, and the lower wing skin. 7075-T6 is stronger but less tough, and goes where the load is compressive: the upper wing skin and stringers. Aluminium-lithium alloys are 3% lighter and 5% stiffer for each 1% of lithium.

Titanium keeps its strength to about 400 °C and does not corrode against carbon fiber, which makes aluminium corrode, so it is used for engine mounts, hot structure and the fittings of composite parts. High-strength steel is for small, heavily loaded parts: landing gear, flap tracks, bolts.

Carbon-fiber composites are lighter and do not corrode or fatigue the way metals do, and a laminate's fibers can be laid along the loads. But they are brittle: an impact can leave barely visible damage inside the laminate that weakens it, especially in compression, so composite structure is designed to carry its loads with such damage, and with the strength it loses when hot and wet. Those allowances take back much of the advantage that Example 1.2 suggests, and composite parts cost more to make and to inspect.

The business jet is designed in aluminium, as the statistical weight equations of Module 11 assume: they were fitted to aluminium aircraft, and Module 11 applies factors for composite parts. Lesson 8 sizes its wing box in each material.

Key takeaways

  • An aircraft structure needs strength and stiffness per kilogram, and fatigue strength, damage tolerance and corrosion resistance over tens of thousands of flights.
  • The metals have nearly the same specific stiffness, about \(25\times10^6\) m²/s²; carbon fiber along the load is four times better.
  • The lightest tie has the largest \(\sigma/\rho\); the lightest panel against buckling the largest \(E^{1/3}/\rho\), which favors light materials.
  • 2024-T3 in tension (fuselage, lower wing skin), 7075-T6 in compression (upper wing skin), titanium for hot or highly loaded fittings, steel for the landing gear, composites where their lightness outweighs their cost and their sensitivity to damage.
