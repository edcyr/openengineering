A

Back to top
A-basis and B-basis values
Material allowables that 99% (A-basis) or 90% (B-basis) of the material exceeds, with 95% confidence. 14 CFR 25.613 asks for the first where one part's failure would lose the structure and allows the second for redundant structure; the names are the industry's.
See: Lesson 3Related: Allowable, Damage tolerance
Allowable
The strength a designer may count on: a statistically low value, taking in temperature and, for composites, moisture, rather than a typical one. At ultimate load a metal's is \(\min(\sigma_u, 1.5\,\sigma_y)\).
See: Lesson 3Related: A-basis and B-basis values, Margin of safety, Ultimate tensile strength \(\sigma_u\)
Aluminium 2024-T3
The damage-tolerant aluminium alloy, with good fatigue and crack-growth properties: fuselage skins and lower wing skins, where the structure is in tension. Typical \(\sigma_y = 345\), \({\sigma_u = 483\ \text{MPa}}\).
See: Lesson 1Related: Aluminium 7075-T6, Fatigue, Damage tolerance
Aluminium 7075-T6
The high-strength aluminium alloy, less tough than 2024: upper wing skins, stringers, spar caps and fittings, where the loads are compressive. Typical \(\sigma_y = 503\), \({\sigma_u = 572\ \text{MPa}}\).
See: Lesson 1Related: Aluminium 2024-T3, Buckling
Aluminium-lithium
Aluminium alloys with up to about 2% lithium: each 1% makes them about 3% lighter and 5% stiffer. They save the business jet's box 7% against 7075-T6 with no change of design.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 8Related: Aluminium 2024-T3, Aluminium 7075-T6

B

Back to top
Balanced maneuver
A steady maneuver with zero pitching acceleration (14 CFR 25.331(b)): the tail load balances the wing's lift and pitching moment about the CG, \({L_t = [nW(x_{cg} - x_{ac}) + M_{ac}]/l_t}\). Balanced conditions at the envelope's corners size the wing.
See: Lesson 6Related: Tail load \(L_t\), Pitching maneuver, Wing pitching moment \(M_{ac}\)
Bending material
The mass of the skin-stringer panels that carry the wing's bending, \(2\int\rho w(t_u + t_l)\,dy\). 130.7 kg for the business jet's 7075-T6 box, about a fifth of the statistical wing weight.
See: Lesson 8Related: Wing box, Panel load \(N\)
Bending moment wing
The moment of the net load outboard of a station, \({M(y) = \int_y^{b/2} V\,d\eta}\). The business jet's design value at the side of the fuselage is 226.9 kN·m at limit load, in a gust at its zero-fuel weight.
See: Lesson 7Related: Shear force, Inertia relief, Side of the fuselage
Buckling
The sudden sideways collapse of a thin part in compression, at a stress set by its stiffness and proportions, not its strength: \({\sigma_{cr} = kE(t/b)^2}\) for a flat plate. It sizes a wing's upper panel.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 8Related: Farrar's efficiency factor \(F\), Panel index, Rib

C

Back to top
Carbon-fiber composite CFRP
Carbon fibers in an epoxy matrix: light, stiff, free of corrosion and metal fatigue, but brittle, weaker in compression, and sensitive to impact damage and to heat and moisture. About half the structure of the 787 and A350.
See: Lesson 1Related: Quasi-isotropic laminate, Damage tolerance
Carry-through
The part of the wing box that crosses the fuselage, joining the two wings; on a low wing it passes under the cabin floor and holds the center tank.
See: Lesson 2Related: Wing box, Main frame, Center tank
Center tank
The fuel tank in the carry-through, inside the fuselage. Its fuel relieves none of the wing's bending, so it is used before the wing tanks; the business jet's holds the 400 kg its wing tanks cannot.
See: Lesson 7Related: Inertia relief, Maximum zero-fuel weight
Crossover altitude
The altitude above which the Mach number, not the equivalent airspeed, limits a design speed: 10.0 km for the business jet's \(V_C\) (270 knots, Mach 0.80) and 8.1 km for its \(V_D\).
See: Lesson 4Related: Design cruising speed \(V_C\), Design dive speed \(V_D\), Equivalent airspeed \(V_E\)

D

Back to top
Damage tolerance
The ability of a structure to carry its loads with a crack or other damage until an inspection finds it (14 CFR 25.571): slow crack growth, tough materials and more than one load path.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 3Related: Fatigue, Load path, Aluminium 2024-T3
Derived gust velocity \(U_{de}\)
The gust velocity that, used in Pratt's formula, reproduces measured accelerations: 66, 50 and 25 ft/s at \(V_B\), \(V_C\) and \(V_D\) up to 20 000 ft, falling linearly to 38, 25 and 12.5 ft/s at 50 000 ft (Part 23 until 2017).
See: Lesson 5Related: Pratt's formula, Reference gust velocity \(U_{\text{ref}}\), Gust envelope
Design cruising Mach number \(M_C\)
The Mach number that limits \(V_C\) at altitude; \(M_D\) must be at least 0.07 above it (0.05 with a rational analysis). 0.80 and 0.87 for the business jet.
See: Lesson 4Related: Design cruising speed \(V_C\), Design dive speed \(V_D\), Crossover altitude
Design cruising speed \(V_C\)
A speed chosen by the designer, at least \(V_B + 1.32\,U_{\text{ref}}\) (knots and ft/s), above which the gust velocity falls. 270 knots equivalent for the business jet, whose least is about 265; the pilots' \(V_{MO}\) is set a little below it.
See: Lesson 4, Lesson 5Related: Design dive speed \(V_D\), Design speed for maximum gust intensity \(V_B\), Design cruising Mach number \(M_C\)
Design dive speed \(V_D\)
The highest speed the structure is designed for: \(V_C/V_D \le 0.8\), or \(M_D \ge M_C + 0.07\) where the Mach number limits it. 337.5 knots and Mach 0.87 for the business jet.
See: Lesson 4Related: Design cruising speed \(V_C\), Design cruising Mach number \(M_C\), V-n diagram
Design limit load factor
The largest load factor the structure must carry, from either envelope. For the business jet at its takeoff weight it is the gust's 3.255, not the maneuver's 3.048.
See: Lesson 5Related: Limit maneuvering load factor, Gust load factor, Ultimate load factor \(N_z\)
Design maneuvering speed \(V_A\)
The speed at which the stall curve reaches the limit load factor, \({V_A = V_{S1}\sqrt{n_{\text{limit}}}}\); below it the wing stalls before it can overload the structure. 96.3 m/s (187 knots) for the business jet at takeoff weight.
See: Lesson 4Related: Stall speed \(V_{S1}\), V-n diagram
Design speed for maximum gust intensity \(V_B\)
The speed at which the aircraft can meet the strongest gusts without stalling: at least \(V_{S1}\sqrt{1 + \Delta n_{\text{ref}}}\) (25.335(d)). 99.0 m/s (192.5 knots) for the business jet at sea level.
See: Lesson 5Related: Design cruising speed \(V_C\), Reference gust velocity \(U_{\text{ref}}\), Gust envelope

E

Back to top
Equivalent airspeed \(V_E\)
The sea-level speed with the same dynamic pressure, \({V_E = V\sqrt{\sigma}}\). Air loads depend on it, so the V-n diagram is drawn in it and holds at every altitude until the Mach number takes over.
See: Lesson 4Related: Crossover altitude, V-n diagram

F

Back to top
Factor of safety
The 1.5 by which limit loads are multiplied to give ultimate loads (14 CFR 25.303). It covers loads above the limit, scatter in materials and manufacture, and the analysis's approximations.
See: Lesson 3Related: Ultimate load, Limit load, Special factor
Farrar's efficiency factor \(F\)
The factor in the buckling stress of an optimum stringer-stiffened panel, \({\sigma = F\sqrt{NE/L}}\): about 0.81 for Z-section stringers (a recalled value). The business jet's upper panel works at 287 MPa.
See: Lesson 8Related: Buckling, Panel load \(N\), Rib
Fatigue
The growth of cracks under repeated loads below the static strength: one wing load cycle and one cabin pressure cycle every flight, and many smaller gust cycles. It, not static strength, sizes fuselage skins and lower wing skins.
See: Lesson 1Related: Damage tolerance, Aluminium 2024-T3
Fitting factor
A special factor of at least 1.15 on fittings and joints not proven by test (14 CFR 25.625), applied with the factor of safety: a fitting is sized for \(1.5 \times 1.15\) times its limit load.
See: Lesson 3Related: Special factor, Factor of safety
Flight envelope loads
The combination of the maneuvering and gust envelopes: every speed and load factor the structure must carry.
See: Lesson 5Related: V-n diagram, Gust envelope
Frame
A ring inside the fuselage, typically about 0.5 m apart, that keeps the section's shape, carries the floor beams and supports the stringers.
See: Lesson 2Related: Main frame, Stringer, Semimonocoque

G

Back to top
Gust alleviation factor \(K_g\)
\({K_g = 0.88\mu/(5.3 + \mu)}\): the share of a sharp-edged gust's load an aircraft feels, allowing for the gust's build-up and the aircraft's rise. 0.825 for the business jet at 20 000 ft.
See: Lesson 5Related: Mass ratio \(\mu\), Pratt's formula
Gust envelope
The region bounded by the gust load factors at \(V_B\), \(V_C\) and \(V_D\), each from Pratt's formula with its derived gust velocity, joined by straight lines.
See: Lesson 5Related: Flight envelope, Derived gust velocity \(U_{de}\), Design speed for maximum gust intensity \(V_B\)
Gust load factor
The load factor a vertical gust gives, \({n = 1 \pm K_g\rho_0U_{de}V_Ea/(2W/S)}\). It grows with speed and lift-curve slope and falls with wing loading: 3.255 for the business jet at takeoff weight, 4.083 at zero fuel.
See: Lesson 5, Lesson 5Related: Pratt's formula, Sharp-edged gust, Design limit load factor

I

Back to top
Inertia relief
The reduction in a wing's shear and bending from its own structure, fuel and engines, each pressing down with \(ng\) times its mass. The business jet's wing fuel relieves 20% of the lift's bending at point A.
See: Lesson 7Related: Maximum zero-fuel weight, Bending moment, Center tank

L

Back to top
Limit load
The largest load expected in service. The structure must carry it without detrimental permanent deformation (14 CFR 25.305); unless a rule says otherwise, every prescribed load is a limit load.
See: Lesson 3Related: Ultimate load, Factor of safety
Limit maneuvering load factor
\({n_{\text{limit}} = 2.1 + 24\,000/(W + 10\,000)}\), \(W\) the design takeoff weight in pounds, from 2.5 to 3.8 (14 CFR 25.337); \(-1\) on the negative side up to \(V_C\). 3.048 for the business jet.
See: Lesson 4Related: V-n diagram, Ultimate load factor \(N_z\), Design limit load factor
Load factor \(n\)
The aerodynamic force normal to the aircraft's axis divided by its weight, in practice \(L/W\): \(1/\cos\phi\) in a level turn, \(1 + V^2/gR\) in a pull-up. Every mass in the aircraft feels it.
See: Lesson 3Related: Limit maneuvering load factor, Gust load factor
Load path
The chain of parts that carries a load from where it is applied to where it is balanced. A good layout keeps load paths short and continuous; damage tolerance asks for more than one.
See: Lesson 2Related: Semimonocoque, Damage tolerance

M

Back to top
Main frame
A heavy fuselage frame where a major load enters: the wing's spars, the engine mounts, the tails' spars.
See: Lesson 2Related: Frame, Carry-through
Margin of safety
\({MS = \text{allowable}/(\text{stress} \times \text{factors}) - 1}\): zero is just strong enough, negative a failure of the design. Checked at limit load against yield and at ultimate load against strength.
See: Lesson 3Related: Allowable, Factor of safety
Mass ratio \(\mu\)
\({\mu = 2(W/S)/(\rho\bar ca g)}\): the aircraft's mass per unit wing area against the mass of air its wing disturbs. A higher mass ratio means a slower response and more of the gust felt. 45.8 for the business jet at sea level, 79.2 at 20 000 ft.
See: Lesson 5Related: Gust alleviation factor \(K_g\), Mean geometric chord \(\bar c\)
Material index
The combination of properties whose largest value gives the lightest part of a given kind: \(\sigma/\rho\) for a tie, \(\sigma^{2/3}/\rho\) for a beam, \(E^{1/3}/\rho\) for a panel in buckling. Masses compare as the inverse of the indices.
See: Lesson 1Related: Specific strength and stiffness, Panel index
Maximum zero-fuel weight MZFW
The heaviest the aircraft may be without fuel. Payload in the fuselage gets no relief from wing fuel, so the wing is most heavily bent at the zero-fuel weight: 4952 kg for the business jet, whose gust there sizes its wing.
See: Lesson 7Related: Inertia relief, Center tank
Mean geometric chord \(\bar c\)
The wing's area divided by its span, \(S/b\): the chord in the mass ratio of the gust formula. 1.871 m for the business jet, against its 2.015 m mean aerodynamic chord.
See: Lesson 5Related: Mass ratio \(\mu\)

P

Back to top
Panel index
\(E^{1/3}/\rho\), the material index of a flat panel that must not buckle. Its small exponent on \(E\) favors light materials: spruce's panel is 0.27 times the mass of 7075-T6's.
See: Lesson 1Related: Material index, Buckling
Panel load \(N\)
The force per unit width on a wing box's upper and lower panels, \(N = M_u/(hw)\): the bending moment carried as a couple across the box's depth. 1053 N/mm at the side of the business jet's fuselage.
See: Lesson 8Related: Farrar's efficiency factor \(F\), Wing box
Pitching maneuver
A maneuver with an abrupt elevator input, in which the tail load leads the aircraft's response (14 CFR 25.331(c)). Pitching maneuvers and direct gusts usually size the horizontal tail.
See: Lesson 6Related: Balanced maneuver, Tail load \(L_t\)
Pratt's formula
The quasi-static gust load factor \({n = 1 \pm K_g\rho_0U_{de}V_Ea/(2W/S)}\), in US units \({\Delta n = K_gU_{de}V_Ea/(498\,W/S)}\). Conceptual design uses it; today's transport rule asks for a dynamic tuned-gust analysis.
See: Lesson 5Related: Gust alleviation factor \(K_g\), Derived gust velocity \(U_{de}\), Tuned discrete gust
Pressure bulkhead
The wall that closes the pressurized cabin: flat at the front, a dome at the back, which carries the pressure as membrane tension. The business jet's aft bulkhead carries \({\Delta p\,\pi d^2/4 = 92.1}\) kN.
See: Lesson 2Related: Frame, Pressure factor 1.33, Semimonocoque
Pressure factor 1.33
The factor on the pressure differential at the maximum relief-valve setting that a fuselage must carry alone, for aircraft certified to 45 000 ft (14 CFR 25.365(d)): a limit load, so 2.0 times the pressure at ultimate.
See: Lesson 3Related: Pressure bulkhead, Limit load

Q

Back to top
Quasi-isotropic laminate
A composite laminate with its fibers in four directions, 0°, ±45° and 90°, so that it is equally stiff every way in its plane, at about a third of the stiffness of the same fibers laid one way.
See: Lesson 1Related: Carbon-fiber composite

R

Back to top
Reference gust velocity \(U_{\text{ref}}\)
The gust velocity of today's transport rule, 14 CFR 25.341(a)(5): 56 ft/s at sea level, 44 ft/s at 15 000 ft, 20.86 ft/s at 60 000 ft, halved at \(V_D\). It also sets the least \(V_B\) and \(V_C\) (25.335).
See: Lesson 5, Lesson 5Related: Tuned discrete gust, Design speed for maximum gust intensity \(V_B\), Design cruising speed \(V_C\)
Rib
A chordwise wall inside the wing box that keeps the airfoil's shape, shortens the skin panels so that they do not buckle, and brings concentrated loads into the box. The business jet's are taken 0.6 m apart.
See: Lesson 2, Lesson 8Related: Buckling, Farrar's efficiency factor \(F\), Wing box

S

Back to top
Schrenk's approximation
A wing's lift along its span taken as the average of an elliptic distribution and one proportional to the chord (Module 4). It puts 85.6% of each half of the business jet's lift outboard of the side of the fuselage, 2.824 m from it.
See: Lesson 7Related: Bending moment, Shear force
Semimonocoque
The standard aircraft structure since the 1930s: a thin skin that carries loads itself, stiffened by stringers and supported by frames or ribs.
See: Lesson 2Related: Skin, Stringer, Frame
Sharp-edged gust
A gust that arrives all at once, changing the angle of attack by \(U/V\) and the load factor by \(\rho_0V_EU_Ea/(2W/S)\). Real gusts build up, which the gust alleviation factor allows for.
See: Lesson 5Related: Gust alleviation factor \(K_g\), Gust load factor
Shear force wing
The net load outboard of a station, \({V(y) = \int_y^{b/2}(\ell - ngm')\,d\eta}\), carried by the spar webs. 80.4 kN at the side of the business jet's fuselage in its design case, at limit load.
See: Lesson 7Related: Bending moment, Spar web
Side of the fuselage
The station where the wing box meets the fuselage and the carry-through, where it is sized: 0.80 m from the business jet's centerline.
See: Lesson 7Related: Bending moment, Carry-through
Skin
The thin outer shell, which carries shear and the cabin's pressure and, with its stringers, tension and compression.
See: Lesson 2Related: Stringer, Semimonocoque
Spar
A spanwise beam forming a wall of the wing box: caps at top and bottom, a web between them. The business jet's front and rear spars are at 15% and 60% of the chord.
See: Lesson 2Related: Spar web, Wing box
Spar web
The vertical plate of a spar, which carries the wing's shear: \({t = V_u/(\tau_{\text{allow}}h)}\) for the two webs. The business jet's need 1.26 mm in all, less than a practical minimum gauge.
See: Lesson 2, Lesson 8Related: Spar, Shear force
Special factor
A factor multiplying the 1.5 where a part's strength is uncertain (14 CFR 25.619): fittings 1.15, castings 1.25 to 2.0 depending on inspection, bearing factors for parts that may be hammered.
See: Lesson 3Related: Fitting factor, Factor of safety
Specific strength and stiffness
Strength and stiffness per unit density, \(\sigma_u/\rho\) and \(E/\rho\). The metals' specific stiffness is the same to a few percent, about \(25\times10^6\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2\); carbon fiber along the load is four times better.
See: Lesson 1Related: Material index
Stall speed \(V_{S1}\)
The 1-g stall speed with the flaps up, \(\sqrt{2(W/S)/(\rho_0C_{L\max})}\); its negative counterpart at \(C_{L,\min}\) reaches \(-1\) at \(V_G\). 55.16 and 70.45 m/s for the business jet.
See: Lesson 4Related: Design maneuvering speed \(V_A\), V-n diagram
Stringer
A long, light stiffener fixed to the skin along the span or the fuselage, which carries bending stress with the skin and breaks it into narrow panels that resist buckling.
See: Lesson 2Related: Skin, Farrar's efficiency factor \(F\)

T

Back to top
Tail load \(L_t\)
The horizontal tail's lift. In a balanced maneuver it is a download at high speed with the CG forward: 15.3 kN for the business jet at \(V_D\), which the wing must make up.
See: Lesson 6, Lesson 6Related: Balanced maneuver, Wing pitching moment \(M_{ac}\), Pitching maneuver
Tuned discrete gust
The gust of today's transport rule: a \(1 - \cos\) gust with gradients from 30 to 350 ft, whose length is tuned to give each part its largest load in a dynamic analysis of the flexible aircraft (14 CFR 25.341).
See: Lesson 5Related: Reference gust velocity \(U_{\text{ref}}\), Pratt's formula

U

Back to top
Ultimate load
The limit load times the factor of safety (and any special factor). The structure must carry it for at least 3 seconds without failing (14 CFR 25.305); structures are sized at ultimate load.
See: Lesson 3Related: Limit load, Factor of safety, Ultimate load factor \(N_z\)
Ultimate load factor \(N_z\)
1.5 times the design limit load factor, the load factor the statistical weight equations take. 4.882 for the business jet at takeoff weight with the gust, 4.573 with the maneuver alone.
See: Lesson 5, Lesson 8Related: Design limit load factor, Ultimate load
Ultimate tensile strength \(\sigma_u\)
The stress at which a material breaks in tension; with the yield strength, the basis of the static allowables.
See: Lesson 1Related: Yield strength \(\sigma_y\), Allowable

V

Back to top
V-n diagram
The maneuvering envelope (14 CFR 25.333): the load factors the structure must carry at each equivalent airspeed, bounded by the stall curves, the limit load factors and \(V_D\). Its corners are A, D, E, F and G.
See: Lesson 4Related: Flight envelope, Design maneuvering speed \(V_A\), Design dive speed \(V_D\)

W

Back to top
Wing box
The closed box between the front and rear spars that carries the wing's bending, shear and torsion, and holds its fuel. Width \((x_{rs} - x_{fs})c\), mean depth about \(0.9(t/c)c\).
See: Lesson 2, Lesson 8Related: Spar, Rib, Carry-through, Panel load \(N\)
Wing pitching moment \(M_{ac}\)
The wing's moment about its aerodynamic center, \(qS\bar c\,C_{M,ac}\), independent of the lift. \({C_{M,ac} = c_{m,0}A\cos^2\Lambda/(A + 2\cos\Lambda) = -0.067}\) for the business jet (\(c_{m,0} = -0.10\), assumed). It grows with the square of the speed.
See: Lesson 6Related: Balanced maneuver, Tail load \(L_t\)

Y

Back to top
Yield strength \(\sigma_y\)
The stress at which a metal starts to deform permanently, by convention at 0.2% permanent strain. Limit loads must stay below it.
See: Lesson 1Related: Ultimate tensile strength \(\sigma_u\), Limit load
Young's modulus \(E\)
The slope of the stress-strain curve while the material is elastic: its stiffness. It sets deflections and the buckling of panels.
See: Lesson 1Related: Specific strength and stiffness, Buckling
