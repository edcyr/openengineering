Engines and Fuel

The engines are the heaviest items that can go almost anywhere, and where they go shapes the rest of the layout: the tail, the landing gear, the wing's position. The fuel is the largest volume after the cabin, and in most aircraft it must fit in the wing. This lesson places the business jet's engines and checks that its wing holds the fuel the mission needs.

Learning objectives

  • Compare the main engine positions and what each one costs and gains.
  • Explain the constraints on the position of rear-mounted engines.
  • Find the fuel volume from the fuel mass and its density.
  • Estimate the volume of a wing's fuel tanks and check it against the fuel needed.

Where the engines go

Each engine position is a bundle of trade-offs:

  • Under the wing, on pylons, as on most airliners. The engines' weight relieves the wing's bending in flight, they are easy to reach, and their inlets see clean air. But they need long landing gear for ground clearance, they yaw the aircraft hard if one fails, which needs a bigger fin, and their pylons interrupt the flaps.
  • On the rear fuselage, as on most business jets. The wing is clean, with full-span flaps; the engines are high off the ground, so the gear can be short and debris is kept out; one engine failing yaws the aircraft little, because the engines are close to the centerline; the cabin ahead of them is quieter. But their weight far aft pulls the center of gravity back, so the wing moves aft and the nose grows to balance; the tail cone must carry them; and the horizontal tail must be kept out of their exhaust, often by a T-tail.
  • On the wing, for turboprops. The propellers need clearance from the ground and from the fuselage, and the nacelles often house the main landing gear; no passenger may sit in the propellers' plane (Lesson 5).
  • In the nose, for single-engine propeller aircraft: simple and light, with the engine's weight balancing the tail, at the cost of noise and the slipstream over the cabin.
  • Inside the fuselage, for fighters: the least drag and the least yaw on failure, but the inlets and ducts take a great deal of the fuselage's volume.

The business jet's engines

Each of the business jet's engines gives 10.6 kN (Module 5); Module 7 will choose an engine and give its real dimensions. Until then the layout uses a nacelle 0.8 m in diameter and 2.4 m long, about the size of a turbofan of this thrust, on a short pylon each side of the tail cone. Four constraints place it along the fuselage:

  • behind the cabin's rear pressure bulkhead, so that a burst fan or turbine disc, which flies out in the engine's plane, cannot reach the passengers;
  • behind the wing, with the inlet far enough back to stay out of the wing's wake at high angles of attack;
  • ahead of the horizontal tail, so that the exhaust passes under or beside it;
  • as far forward as these allow, because every meter aft moves the center of gravity, and the wing with it.

With the nacelle's center at 75% of the fuselage length, FS 11.6 m, the inlet is at FS 10.4 m, half a meter behind the cabin's end at FS 9.88 m and 2.8 m behind the wing's trailing edge; the exhaust, at FS 12.8 m, is ahead of the horizontal tail.

Fuel volume

Module 3 gave the business jet's fuel as a fraction of the takeoff weight. Its volume follows from the density of jet fuel, about 800 kg/m³; it varies with the grade and the temperature, by a few percent either way. The CJ3 Gen2's published usable fuel, 2136 kg in 2661 L, is 803 kg/m³.

Example 7.1 — The business jet's fuel volume

Find the volume of the business jet's fuel, with \(\Wf/\Wo = 0.2867\) and \(\Wo = 6943\ \text{kg}\).

Show solution
\[ m_f = 0.2867(6943) = 1991\ \text{kg}, \qquad V_f = \frac{1991}{800} = 2.49\ \text{m}^3 \]

About 2490 liters: close to the CJ3's 2661.

Most aircraft carry their fuel in the wing. The wing box between the spars is already a sealed structure, the fuel's weight spread along the span relieves the wing's bending in flight, and the fuel sits close to the center of gravity, so burning it moves the balance little. A statistical fit to transport wings gives the volume of the tanks inside the wing, without a center tank in the fuselage:

Wing tank volume

\[ V_{\text{tank}} = 0.54\,\frac{S^{1.5}}{\sqrt A}\left(\frac tc\right)_{\!r}\frac{1 + \lambda\sqrt\tau + \lambda^2\tau}{(1 + \lambda)^2} \]

\(S\) in m², \(V_{\text{tank}}\) in m³; \((t/c)_r\) the thickness ratio at the root and \(\tau = (t/c)_t/(t/c)_r\) its taper to the tip. The tanks hold about 60% of the wing's whole volume; the rest is structure, leading and trailing edges, and tips.

Example 7.2 — Does the business jet's fuel fit?

The business jet's wing has \(S = 28\ \text{m}^2\), \(A = 8\), \(\lambda = 0.35\) and \(t/c = 0.12\) from root to tip. Find its tank volume, and how full the tanks are with the mission's fuel.

Show solution
\[ V_{\text{tank}} = 0.54\,\frac{28^{1.5}}{\sqrt8}\,(0.12)\,\frac{1 + 0.35 + 0.35^2}{1.35^2} = 0.54(52.38)(0.12)(0.808) = 2.74\ \text{m}^3 \] \[ \frac{V_f}{V_{\text{tank}}} = \frac{2.49}{2.74} = 0.91 \]

The fuel fits, with 9% to spare: room for some growth of the design. No fuselage tank is needed, which keeps the tail cone free for the engines' systems and baggage.

Example 7.3 — A thinner wing

A designer wants a 10% thick wing for a faster cruise. Does the fuel still fit? What if only the tip is thinned, to 10%, with a 12% root?

Show solution

At 10% throughout, the volume scales with the thickness: \(V_{\text{tank}} = 2.74(0.10/0.12) = 2.29\ \text{m}^3\), less than the 2.49 needed. With a 12% root and \(\tau = 0.10/0.12 = 0.833\):

\[ V_{\text{tank}} = 0.54(52.38)(0.12)\,\frac{1 + 0.35\sqrt{0.833} + 0.35^2(0.833)}{1.35^2} = 2.65\ \text{m}^3 \]

The thick root keeps most of the volume, because the root's chord is the longest: the fuel then fills 94% of the tanks. Otherwise the design needs a fuselage tank or a larger wing, and every choice feeds back into the sizing.

Key takeaways

  • Engines under the wing relieve its bending but need long gear and a bigger fin; on the rear fuselage they keep the wing clean but move the balance aft.
  • Rear engines go behind the pressure bulkhead and the wing's wake, ahead of the horizontal tail, and as far forward as those allow.
  • Jet fuel is about 800 kg/m³: the business jet's 1991 kg is 2.49 m³.
  • \(V_{\text{tank}} = 0.54(S^{1.5}/\sqrt A)(t/c)_r(1 + \lambda\sqrt\tau + \lambda^2\tau)/(1 + \lambda)^2\): the business jet's wing holds 2.74 m³, so its fuel fills 91% of the tanks.
