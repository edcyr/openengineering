The Cabin Cross-Section

The fuselage diameter is the most consequential number in a transport's layout: it sets the frontal area, a large share of the wetted area, and the weight of every frame. It is fixed by a drawing of a cut through the cabin, with the seats, the aisle and the floor in it. This lesson draws that cut for the business jet.

Learning objectives

  • Use typical cabin dimensions and the certification rules for aisles.
  • Compute the cabin width from the seats, aisles and wall gaps.
  • Estimate the fuselage wall thickness and the outer diameter.
  • Place the floor and check the headroom in the aisle.

Cabin dimensions

A cabin is designed around the people in it. The seat widths, the spacing of the rows (the seat pitch, Lesson 4), the aisle and the headroom have settled into typical values for each class of service:

Two certification rules (CS 25 and FAR 25 have the same numbers) govern the aisles of a transport cabin:

  • Aisle width (25.815). The aisle must be at least 12 in wide below 25 in above the floor and 15 in above it for 10 passengers or fewer; 12 and 20 in for 11 to 19 passengers; and 15 and 20 in for 20 or more. The armrests are at about 25 in, and above them the seat backs are narrower than the armrests, so the aisle opens up. Short- and medium-range airliners typically have 19 to 20 in at the armrests.
  • Number of aisles (25.817). With one aisle, no more than three seats may sit on each side of it. Up to six abreast needs one aisle; seven to twelve abreast need two.

The cabin width

Across the armrests, the cabin is as wide as its benches and aisles, plus a small gap between each outer seat and the wall:

Inner cabin width

\[ \dI = \sum w_{\text{bench}} + \sum w_{\text{aisle}} + 2\,w_{\text{gap}} \]

With a gap of about 1 in. For a circular cabin this is its inner diameter; 1 in = 0.0254 m.

The formula measures the width at the armrests, so it assumes they are at the widest part of the cabin. Above them the wall curves in, which the drawing must check: the passenger's shoulders and head at the window seat, and the overhead bins above, need room inside the curve.

Example 3.1 — The business jet's cabin width

The business jet seats its passengers one on each side of a center aisle, in seats 20 in wide across their armrests, at the narrow end of first-class seats in the table. With 8 passengers, what aisle width does the rule require? Find the inner cabin width with a 1 in gap at each wall.

Show solution

For 10 passengers or fewer, the aisle must be 12 in wide below 25 in from the floor and 15 in above. Take 15 in at the armrests, which meets both without relying on the seat backs' shape.

\[ \dI = 20 + 20 + 15 + 2(1) = 57\ \text{in} = 57(0.0254) = 1.448\ \text{m} \]

The CJ3 Gen2's cabin is 58 in (1.47 m) wide: a light jet's cabin is as narrow as two seats and an aisle allow.

Wall thickness and outer diameter

Outside the cabin lining are the insulation, the frames, the stringers and the skin. Their depth grows with the size of the fuselage, because a bigger pressurized cylinder needs deeper frames. Statistics of transport fuselages give:

Fuselage wall and outer diameter

\[ \Delta d = 0.084\ \text{m} + 0.045\,\dI, \qquad \dF = \dI + \Delta d \]

\(\Delta d\) is the two walls together, left and right; all lengths in meters.

Example 3.2 — The business jet's fuselage diameter

Find the wall thickness and outer diameter of the business jet's fuselage, with \(\dI = 1.448\ \text{m}\).

Show solution
\[ \Delta d = 0.084 + 0.045(1.448) = 0.149\ \text{m}, \qquad \dF = 1.448 + 0.149 = 1.597\ \text{m} \]

Each wall is about 75 mm deep. Module 3's sketch had a 1.8 m fuselage: the drawn one is 11% narrower, which will show in the wetted area (Lesson 8).

Example 3.3 — A six-abreast airliner

A 150-seat airliner has two benches of three economy seats and a 19 in aisle. Find its inner and outer diameters.

Show solution
\[ \dI = 60 + 60 + 19 + 2(1) = 141\ \text{in} = 3.581\ \text{m} \] \[ \Delta d = 0.084 + 0.045(3.581) = 0.245\ \text{m}, \qquad \dF = 3.827\ \text{m} \]

The A320 family, whose seats are slightly wider than these, is about 3.7 m wide inside and 3.95 m outside.

Pressurized fuselages are nearly always circular, or made of circular arcs, because a circle carries the pressure as pure tension in the skin. An unpressurized light aircraft can use a flatter, boxier section that fits two people sitting side by side more tightly; the wall formula above is for pressurized transports.

The floor and the headroom

The floor is a chord of the circle. In an airliner it sits a little below the axis, leaving room for baggage and cargo under it and full standing height above it. In a small cabin there is no room for both: the floor goes as low as the circle allows, and there is no hold underneath. Some light jets lower the floor of the aisle alone, a dropped aisle, with the seats on ledges either side, to gain standing height.

Example 3.4 — Headroom in the business jet

With the floor \(0.42\,\dI\) below the axis, as low as the seats' feet allow, how much height is there in the business jet's aisle? Compare it with the headroom in the table and with the CJ3's 57 in.

Show solution
\[ h = \frac{\dI}{2} + 0.42\,\dI = 0.724 + 0.608 = 1.33\ \text{m} = 52\ \text{in} \]

Well under the 60 in of a small aircraft's aisle in the table: this is a sit-down cabin, as in every light jet. The CJ3 reaches 57 in with a dropped aisle. Try Figure 3.1 to see how much wider the cabin must be for standing room.

Key takeaways

  • The cabin width is the benches plus the aisles plus about 1 in at each wall: \(\dI = \sum w_{\text{bench}} + \sum w_{\text{aisle}} + 2w_{\text{gap}}\).
  • Aisles: 15 or 20 in above the armrests by passenger count (25.815); one aisle up to six abreast, two up to twelve (25.817).
  • The wall adds \(\Delta d = 0.084 + 0.045\,\dI\) in meters: the business jet is 1.448 m inside and 1.597 m outside.
  • The floor is a chord: in a small cabin it goes low and the headroom is short; the business jet has a 52 in sit-down cabin.
