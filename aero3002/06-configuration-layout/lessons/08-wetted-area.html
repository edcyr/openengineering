Wetted Area and the Refined Estimate

Module 3 estimated the business jet's lift-to-drag ratio from the wetted area of a sketch. The layout now has a fuselage, wing, tails and nacelles drawn to scale, so their wetted areas can be computed from their dimensions. The new total goes back into the drag estimate, and from there into the sizing: the first full turn of the design loop.

Learning objectives

  • Compute the wetted areas of a fuselage, a wing or tail, and a nacelle from their dimensions.
  • Find the exposed area of a wing outside the fuselage.
  • Build up the business jet's wetted area and compare it with Module 3's sketch.
  • Update the zero-lift drag and \(\LDmax\), and say what they change in the sizing.

Wetted areas from the layout

Module 3 wrapped the fuselage's projected views and doubled the exposed planforms. With dimensions from the layout, formulas fitted to real shapes do better:

Fuselage with a cylindrical middle part

\[ S_{\text{wet},F} = \pi\,\dF\lF\left(1 - \frac{2\dF}{\lF}\right)^{2/3}\left(1 + \frac{\dF^2}{\lF^2}\right) \]

For fineness ratios \(\lF/\dF\) of 4.5 or more; a cylinder of the same length and diameter would have \(\pi\dF\lF\).

Wing or tail

\[ S_{\text{wet}} = 2\,S_{\text{exp}}\left[1 + 0.25\left(\frac tc\right)_{\!r}\frac{1 + \tau\lambda}{1 + \lambda}\right] \]

\(S_{\text{exp}}\) is the exposed planform, outside the fuselage; at \(\tau = 1\) the bracket is \(1 + 0.25\,t/c\). Module 3's \(1.977 + 0.52\,t/c\) gives within 1% of this.

The wing's reference area \(S\) runs through the fuselage to the centerline. The part inside is nearly a trapezoid as wide as the fuselage, between the root chord and the chord at the fuselage side:

\[ S_{\text{exp}} = S - \dF\,\frac{c_r + c(\dF/2)}{2} \]

For the tails this module counts their whole area as exposed: a T-tail's horizontal surface is all in the air, and a tail mounted on the slender tail cone hides little of itself. The nacelles are taken as cylinders, \(S_{\text{wet}} = \pi Dl\), until Module 7 gives the engine's shape.

Example 8.1 — The business jet's fuselage and wing

Find the wetted area of the business jet's fuselage (\(\lF = 15.5\ \text{m}\), \(\dF = 1.597\ \text{m}\)) and of its wing (\(S = 28\ \text{m}^2\), \(b = 14.97\ \text{m}\), \(c_r = 2.772\ \text{m}\), \(\lambda = 0.35\), \(t/c = 0.12\) throughout).

Show solution
\[ \frac{\lF}{\dF} = 9.71, \qquad S_{\text{wet},F} = \pi(1.597)(15.5)(1 - 0.206)^{2/3}(1 + 0.0106) = 67.4\ \text{m}^2 \]

At the fuselage side, \(y = 0.80\ \text{m}\), the chord is \(c = 2.772[1 - 0.65(1.597/14.97)] = 2.580\ \text{m}\):

\[ S_{\text{exp}} = 28 - 1.597\,\frac{2.772 + 2.580}{2} = 28 - 4.27 = 23.73\ \text{m}^2 \] \[ S_{\text{wet},W} = 2(23.73)\left[1 + 0.25(0.12)\right] = 48.9\ \text{m}^2 \]

The business jet's wetted area

Example 8.2 — The whole aircraft

Add the tails (\(S_{HT} = 5.42\) and \(S_{VT} = 3.45\ \text{m}^2\), \(t/c = 0.10\)) and two nacelles 0.8 m by 2.4 m. Find the total wetted area and \(\Swet/\Sref\), and compare them with Module 3's sketch: 154.7 m² and 5.52.

Show solution
\[ S_{\text{wet},HT} = 2(5.42)(1.025) = 11.12\ \text{m}^2, \qquad S_{\text{wet},VT} = 2(3.45)(1.025) = 7.08\ \text{m}^2 \] \[ S_{\text{wet},n} = 2\pi(0.8)(2.4) = 12.06\ \text{m}^2 \] \[ \Swet = 67.38 + 48.88 + 11.12 + 7.08 + 12.06 = 146.5\ \text{m}^2 \] \[ \frac{\Swet}{\Sref} = \frac{146.5}{28} = 5.23 \]

5% less than the sketch. Almost all the difference is in the fuselage and tails: the drawn fuselage is 11% narrower than the sketch's (1.6 against 1.8 m), though 1.5 m longer, and the longer arm shrank the tails.

The refined lift-to-drag ratio

With the equivalent skin-friction coefficient of Module 3, the new wetted area gives a new zero-lift drag and maximum lift-to-drag ratio:

\[ \CDz = C_{fe}\,\frac{\Swet}{\Sref}, \qquad \LDmax = \frac12\sqrt{\frac{\pi A e}{C_{fe}\,\Swet/\Sref}} \]
Equivalent skin-friction coefficients for subsonic flight (source: Raymer, Table 12.3)
Type\(C_{fe}\)

Example 8.3 — The business jet's new \(\LDmax\)

With \(A = 8\), \(e = 0.75\) and \(C_{fe} = 0.0030\), find \(\CDz\), \(\LDmax\) and the cruise \(L/D\) for the layout, and compare them with Module 3's 0.0165, 16.9 and 14.6.

Show solution
\[ \CDz = 0.0030(5.23) = 0.0157, \qquad \LDmax = \frac12\sqrt{\frac{\pi(8)(0.75)}{0.0157}} = 17.3 \] \[ \text{Cruise (jet, best range): } L/D = 0.866(17.3) = 15.0 \]

A 2.5% better lift-to-drag ratio. Cruise and loiter fuel fall in about the same proportion, so the fuel fraction, the takeoff weight and the wing shrink a little when Module 3's sizing is run again, and the layout shrinks with them. That is one turn of the design loop; the next modules add the engine (Module 7), the landing gear (Module 8), a proper drag build-up (Module 9) and the weights and balance (Module 11), and each turn changes less.

What the layout did not count

The wetted area is not the whole drag. The equivalent skin-friction coefficient folds in the form drag of typical shapes, the interference where parts meet, and small items like antennas and gaps. A component-by-component build-up, with form factors and interference factors for each part, comes in Module 9; this module's layout gives it the dimensions it needs.

Key takeaways

  • Fuselage: \(S_{\text{wet}} = \pi\dF\lF(1 - 2\dF/\lF)^{2/3}(1 + \dF^2/\lF^2)\); wings and tails: \(2S_{\text{exp}}[1 + 0.25(t/c)_r(1 + \tau\lambda)/(1 + \lambda)]\).
  • The exposed wing is the reference area less the trapezoid inside the fuselage.
  • The business jet's layout has \(\Swet = 146.5\ \text{m}^2\) and \(\Swet/\Sref = 5.23\), 5% less than the sketch, mostly from a narrower fuselage and smaller tails.
  • \(\CDz\) falls to 0.0157 and \(\LDmax\) rises from 16.9 to 17.3: the sizing can now be run again.
