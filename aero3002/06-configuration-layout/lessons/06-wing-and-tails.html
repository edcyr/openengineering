Placing the Wing and Tails

With the fuselage drawn, the wing and tails can be put on it. Where the wing goes depends on the center of gravity, which depends on where everything else goes, so the first position is a reasoned guess that later modules will correct. The tails then follow: their arm is now a real length, not 47% of a guess, and Module 4's tails can be resized.

Learning objectives

  • Explain why the wing must be placed near the center of gravity, and how the engines' position moves it.
  • Make a first estimate of the wing's station from the tail arm, before the weights are known.
  • Resize the tails on the new arm and check that the wing and tails fit the fuselage.
  • Judge whether a longer fuselage, with smaller tails, would reduce the wetted area.

Where the wing goes

The wing carries the weight, so its lift must act close to the center of gravity, with the aerodynamic center a little behind it for stability (Module 13). The center of gravity depends on where the heavy items are: the engines, the fuel, the landing gear, the payload. Heavy engines at the tail pull it aft, so a rear-engine jet has its wing farther back than an airliner with its engines under the wing, and a long nose ahead of the cabin to balance.

The weights come in Module 12. Until then, the tails give a reasoned first position. They sit at the end of the fuselage, with their quarter-MAC points at about 92% of its length, and the tail arm is a known share of the length: about 60% with a propeller in the nose, 52% with engines on the wing, and 45 to 48% with engines at the rear (Module 4). The wing's aerodynamic center is then one arm ahead of the tails:

First wing position

\[ x_{ac,W} \approx (0.92 - L_t/\lF)\,\lF \]

\(x_{ac,W}\) is the station of the wing's quarter-MAC point; \(L_t/\lF\) the tail arm as a share of the fuselage length.

Example 6.1 — The business jet's wing station

Place the business jet's wing on its 15.5 m fuselage, with a tail arm of 47%. Its quarter-MAC point is 2.158 m behind the root leading edge (Module 4), and its root chord is 2.772 m. Where are the root leading and trailing edges, and how do they sit against the cabin, which runs from FS 4.0 to FS 9.88 m?

Show solution
\[ x_{ac,W} = (0.92 - 0.47)(15.5) = 6.98\ \text{m} \] \[ x_{LE} = 6.98 - 2.16 = 4.82\ \text{m}, \qquad x_{TE} = 4.82 + 2.77 = 7.59\ \text{m} \]

The wing root spans FS 4.8 to 7.6 m, under the front half of the cabin. As a low wing it passes under the floor, where its spars cross the fuselage; the main landing gear will fold into the wing behind the rear spar (Module 8). The tails' quarter-MAC points are one arm behind, at \(6.98 + 0.47(15.5) = 14.26\ \text{m}\).

The vertical position follows Module 4's reasoning: the business jet keeps its low wing, which leaves the cabin floor clear, puts the landing gear in the wing, and keeps the wing below the engines' inlets.

Resizing the tails

Module 4 sized the tails from volume coefficients on an arm of 47% of a 14 m fuselage. With the same coefficients and the arm of the drawn fuselage, they shrink:

\[ S_{HT} = \frac{c_{HT}\,\bar c_W\,S_W}{L_{HT}}, \qquad S_{VT} = \frac{c_{VT}\,b_W\,S_W}{L_{VT}} \]

Example 6.2 — The business jet's tails on the new arm

Resize the tails with \(c_{HT} = 0.70\), \(c_{VT} = 0.06\), \(\bar c_W = 2.015\ \text{m}\), \(b_W = 14.97\ \text{m}\), \(S_W = 28\ \text{m}^2\) and an arm of 47% of 15.5 m for both. Find the fin's height with \(A_{VT} = 1.5\) and the horizontal tail's span with \(A_{HT} = 4\).

Show solution
\[ L = 0.47(15.5) = 7.29\ \text{m} \] \[ S_{HT} = \frac{0.70(2.015)(28)}{7.29} = 5.42\ \text{m}^2 \] \[ S_{VT} = \frac{0.06(14.97)(28)}{7.29} = 3.45\ \text{m}^2 \] \[ h_{VT} = \sqrt{1.5(3.45)} = 2.27\ \text{m}, \qquad b_{HT} = \sqrt{4(5.42)} = 4.66\ \text{m} \]

Both tails are 10% smaller than Module 4's 6.00 and 3.82 m², because the arm grew from 6.58 to 7.29 m. The horizontal tail sits on the tail cone, as on the CJ series; a T-tail would ride higher on the fin, with a longer arm and a smaller area, but a heavier fin.

Is a longer fuselage worth it?

Module 4 noted that a longer fuselage buys smaller tails but adds its own weight and wetted area. The layout can now say which wins. Figure 6.2 adds the wetted areas of the fuselage and the two tails as the fuselage is stretched, with the arm at 47% of its length: the fuselage grows by about 5 m² per meter, the tails shrink by about 1.3.

The sum is least at a fuselage of about 7.4 m, half the length the cabin needs. Every meter beyond the cabin's 15.5 m adds nearly 4 m² of wetted area, after the smaller tails are counted. For a passenger aircraft, then, the cabin sets the length, and a designer does not stretch the fuselage to save tail. Only aircraft with little in the fuselage, such as sailplanes and some fighters, choose their length for the tail arm.

A first guess, on purpose

The wing station of Example 6.1 comes from a share of the length, not from a balance. Module 12 adds up the weights and their stations, finds the center of gravity over the range of loadings and fuel, and moves the wing until the aerodynamic center falls where the stability analysis of Module 13 wants it. Moving the wing then changes the arm, and the tails again.

Key takeaways

  • The wing goes near the center of gravity; rear engines move both aft. Before the weights are known, \(x_{ac,W} \approx (0.92 - L_t/\lF)\lF\).
  • The business jet's wing quarter-MAC is at FS 6.98 m, its root under the front of the cabin; its tails' at FS 14.26 m.
  • On the 7.29 m arm, the tails shrink 10% to \(S_{HT} = 5.42\) and \(S_{VT} = 3.45\ \text{m}^2\).
  • A longer fuselage adds far more wetted area than the smaller tails save: the cabin sets the length.
