The Cabin Length

The cross-section fixes how many seats fit in a row; the length fixes how many rows fit in the cabin, and what else must fit between them: lavatories, galleys, doors and the exits the rules require. This lesson lays out the business jet's cabin from front to back, and checks where its baggage goes.

Learning objectives

  • Build up a cabin length from the rows, the seat pitch and the cabin's other items.
  • Find the emergency exits and cabin crew the rules require for a number of passengers.
  • Use the quick rule for an airliner's cabin length, and know when it does not apply.
  • Estimate the volume of the baggage and cargo, and check it against the space for it.

Rows and pitch

The seat pitch is the distance from a point on one seat to the same point on the seat in front: it includes the seat itself and the legroom. Typical values are in Lesson 3's cabin table: 38 to 40 in in first class, 34 to 36 in in economy (many airlines now use 29 to 32), and 30 to 32 in at high density. With \(n_{SA}\) seats abreast, the number of rows is

\[ n_R = \frac{n_{\text{PAX}}}{n_{SA}} \]

rounded up. The rows take \(n_R\) times the pitch; then come the other items, each with a typical length along the cabin [1], Chapter 9:

  • Lavatories, about 40 by 40 in: one per 40 to 60 economy passengers, or 10 to 20 in first class.
  • Galleys, sized by volume: 1 to 2 ft³ per economy passenger, 5 to 8 in first class; a galley unit is about 30 in deep.
  • Doors and exits, which need clear floor space in front of them, and the cross aisles that lead to them.
  • Wardrobes and stowage, for coats and carry-on baggage the overhead bins cannot take.

Cabin length

\[ l_{\text{cabin}} = n_R\,p + n_{\text{lav}}\,l_{\text{lav}} + n_{\text{gal}}\,l_{\text{gal}} + l_{\text{doors, other}} \]

\(p\) the seat pitch; here \(l_{\text{lav}} = 40\ \text{in}\) and \(l_{\text{gal}} = 30\ \text{in}\), with an allowance in meters for the doors and the rest.

Example 4.1 — The business jet's cabin

Lay out the business jet's 8 seats two abreast, in four rows at a first-class pitch of 38 in, as two pairs of club seats facing each other. Add a lavatory at the rear (40 in) and 1.0 m at the front for the entry door, with a refreshment center opposite it. Find the cabin length and compare it with the CJ3 Gen2's 4.78 m [12].

Show solution
\[ l_{\text{cabin}} = 4(38) + 40 = 192\ \text{in} = 4.877\ \text{m}, \qquad + 1.0\ \text{m} = 5.877\ \text{m} \]

About 1.1 m longer than the CJ3's cabin, which seats its passengers more tightly. The generous pitch and the full lavatory are this design's choices, and Lesson 5 shows what they cost in fuselage length. The layout tool lets you try tighter ones.

A quick rule for airliners

Averaged over the whole cabin of an economy-class airliner, every row needs about 1 m, once its share of the lavatories, galleys and doors is included [2], Chapter 6:

\[ l_{\text{cabin}} \approx n_R\,(1\ \text{m}) \]

Example 4.2 — A 150-seat airliner

The six-abreast airliner of Lesson 3 has 150 seats at a 32 in pitch, three lavatories, two galleys and 2.1 m for its four doors and two overwing exits. Find its cabin length and compare it with the quick rule.

Show solution
\[ n_R = \frac{150}{6} = 25, \qquad l_{\text{cabin}} = 25(32) + 3(40) + 2(30) = 980\ \text{in} = 24.89\ \text{m} \] \[ + 2.1\ \text{m} = 26.99\ \text{m}, \qquad \text{rule: } 25(1\ \text{m}) = 25\ \text{m} \]

The rule is 7% short of this build-up; it is a first estimate, before a plan is drawn. The A320 family, with about 150 seats in two classes, has a cabin about 27.5 m long.

The rule does not apply to the business jet: its 4 rows would get 4 m, but a cabin of 8 seats still needs a whole lavatory and a door, whose length is not shared among many rows.

Emergency exits and cabin crew

A transport must be evacuable in 90 seconds with half its exits blocked [6], §25.803 and Appendix J. The rules give the exits by type and size, and the number needed on each side of the fuselage for a given number of passenger seats:

Source: [5], [6], §25.807.

Simplified from §25.807 of [5] and [6], which rate each type of exit for a number of seats and add further conditions.

The exits must be spread along the cabin, so they fix the positions of the doors, and of the cross aisles and wider pitch beside the overwing exits. The operating rules also set a minimum cabin crew. In Europe [9], and for private flights in the United States [7], it is none up to 19 seats, one for 20 to 50, two for 51 to 100, and one more for every 50 seats or part of 50 beyond that; US airline rules [8] ask for the first attendant from 10 seats. Every attendant needs a seat, usually a folding one beside a door.

The business jet, with 8 passenger seats, needs one Type IV exit on each side at least; in practice the entry door counts on one side and an overwing exit serves the other. It needs no cabin attendant.

Baggage and cargo

Everything the passengers check in, and any paying cargo, needs a place too. The volume follows from the mass, with typical densities of 170 kg/m³ for baggage and 160 kg/m³ for cargo [2], Chapter 6. In an airliner the hold under the floor takes them, along with whatever the overhead bins cannot. Below the floor, about 35 to 55% of the fuselage length is usable for cargo; the check is

\[ V_{\text{hold}} \ge V_{\text{cargo}} + (V_{\text{baggage}} - V_{\text{overhead}}) \]

Example 4.3 — The business jet's baggage

Of each passenger's 100 kg, about 20 kg is baggage. What volume does the business jet's baggage need? The CJ3 Gen2 has 1.84 m³ of baggage space, rated for 454 kg [12], in its nose and tail cone.

Show solution
\[ m_B = 8(20) = 160\ \text{kg}, \qquad V_B = \frac{160}{170} = 0.94\ \text{m}^3 \]

A light jet has no hold under its low floor, so baggage goes in the nose, ahead of the cockpit, and in the tail cone behind the cabin. That is one more reason the business jet's tail cone cannot also hold seats, as an airliner's does (Lesson 5).

Key takeaways

  • Cabin length: the rows at their pitch, plus lavatories (40 in), galleys (30 in) and an allowance for doors and stowage.
  • For an economy airliner, about 1 m of cabin per row; small cabins need more, because their lavatory and door are not shared.
  • The exits and their types follow from the passenger seats (25.807); cabin crew, one per 50 seats above 19.
  • Baggage at 170 kg/m³, cargo at 160 kg/m³; a light jet carries its baggage in the nose and tail cone.
  • The business jet's cabin is 5.88 m long. Next, Lesson 5 closes the fuselage around it.
