From Numbers to a Layout

Modules 2 to 5 turned a requirement into numbers: a takeoff weight, a wing area and aspect ratio, a thrust. An aircraft has to be drawn before it can be built, and the drawing answers questions the numbers cannot: whether the passengers, fuel and engines fit, and where they go. This module draws the business jet, from the cabin outward, and uses the drawing to correct two numbers the sizing had to guess, the fuselage length and the wetted area.

Learning objectives

  • Describe what the configuration layout does in the design loop, and what it gives back to the sizing.
  • List the numbers a layout needs from the earlier steps of the design.
  • Name the drawings of a conceptual layout and what each one shows.
  • Locate points on a layout with fuselage stations, waterlines and buttlines.

The layout in the design loop

The sizing so far has been statistical. Module 3 took the empty-weight fraction from aircraft of the same class and estimated the lift-to-drag ratio from a sketch. Module 4 sized the tails on a fuselage length that was only a guess. Module 5 chose the wing loading and thrust-to-weight ratio from performance requirements, without asking whether the wing could hold the fuel. The configuration layout is where these numbers first meet a geometry. It has three jobs:

  • Fit. Everything the aircraft carries must fit inside it: the passengers in a cabin they can stand, or at least sit, in; the fuel in the wing; the engines, landing gear and systems somewhere they can be reached.
  • Place. Each item must be where it works: the wing near the center of gravity, the tails at the end of a long arm, the engines where their noise, exhaust and failure do least harm.
  • Measure. A drawing has dimensions the statistics did not: a fuselage length, a tail arm, a wetted area. Fed back to the sizing, they replace the guesses.

The loop then runs again: a better wetted area gives a better lift-to-drag ratio, which changes the fuel, the weight and the wing, which changes the layout. Each pass moves less than the one before, and a conceptual design stops when the changes are small compared with the uncertainty of the methods. This module makes the first full pass for the business jet. The landing gear (Module 8), the weights and center of gravity (Module 11) and the stability (Module 12) will each move things again.

Inside out, then outside in

A transport is laid out from the inside: the cabin is drawn around the passengers, and the fuselage around the cabin. A fighter or a sailplane is laid out from the outside: its shape is set by the aerodynamics, and the contents are fitted into it. The business jet, like every aircraft that carries people for a living, is laid out from the inside.

The drawings of a layout

A conceptual layout is a small set of drawings, kept to scale and consistent with each other:

  • The three-view: a side view, a top view and a front view, lined up so that each point appears at the same station in the side and top views and at the same height in the side and front views. It shows the outer shape: what the air sees.
  • The cabin cross-section: the seats, aisles, floor and walls in a cut through the cabin, which fixes the fuselage diameter (Lesson 3).
  • The cabin plan: the seats, lavatories, galleys and doors seen from above, which fixes the cabin length (Lesson 4).
  • The inboard profile: a side view cut open to show where the internal items go, such as tanks, landing gear, engines and systems.

Today the drawings live in a computer-aided design model, but the discipline is the same as on a drawing board: every dimension in the drawing is either a requirement, a rule, or a decision someone can explain.

Stations, waterlines and buttlines

Every point on a layout needs an address that does not change when the drawing does. Aircraft use three distances from fixed reference planes:

  • The fuselage station (FS) is the distance aft of a reference plane at or ahead of the nose. Many drawings put the reference ahead of the nose, so that a longer nose added later still has positive stations.
  • The waterline (WL) is the height above a reference plane, often below the aircraft, so that every waterline is positive too. The name comes from flying boats, whose heights were measured from the water.
  • The buttline (BL) is the distance to the left or right of the plane of symmetry.

North American drawings give the stations in inches, so that FS 300 is 300 in aft of the reference. This module uses meters, with the reference plane of the stations at the nose, the waterline zero on the axis of the fuselage's cylindrical part, and the buttline zero on the centerline. A part's position is then a station, a buttline and a waterline, and the arm between two parts is the difference of their stations.

Example 1.1 — The address of the wing's aerodynamic center

Module 4 found that the business jet's mean aerodynamic chord is \(\bar c = 2.015\ \text{m}\) long, \(3.14\ \text{m}\) from the centerline, and starts \(1.654\ \text{m}\) behind the wing's root leading edge. In this module's layout the root leading edge is at FS 4.82 m. Find the station and buttline of the wing's aerodynamic center, the quarter-chord point of the MAC.

Show solution
\[ \text{FS} = 4.82 + 1.654 + 0.25(2.015) = 6.98\ \text{m}, \qquad \text{BL} = 3.14\ \text{m} \]

The aerodynamic center is a point of the wing on each side, at BL 3.14 m left and right; for balance and tail arms only its station matters. Lesson 6 explains why the wing goes there.

Example 1.2 — What a longer fuselage does to the tails

Module 4 sized the tails with an arm of 47% of a 14 m fuselage: \(S_{HT} = 6.00\ \text{m}^2\) and \(S_{VT} = 3.82\ \text{m}^2\). If the layout gives a 15.5 m fuselage and the arm stays at 47%, what are the new tail areas?

Show solution

For a given volume coefficient the tail area is inversely proportional to the arm, so both tails scale by \(14/15.5 = 0.903\):

\[ S_{HT} = 0.903(6.00) = 5.42\ \text{m}^2, \qquad S_{VT} = 0.903(3.82) = 3.45\ \text{m}^2 \]

A tenth less tail: less weight and less wetted area, bought with more fuselage. Lesson 6 checks whether the trade is worth it.

Key takeaways

  • The layout makes everything fit, puts each item where it works, and measures what the statistics guessed.
  • Transports are laid out from the inside: cabin first, then the fuselage around it, then the wing, tails and engines.
  • A conceptual layout is a three-view, a cabin cross-section, a cabin plan and an inboard profile, all to scale.
  • Points are located by fuselage station (aft), waterline (up) and buttline (sideways); an arm is a difference of stations.
