Nose, Tail Cone and Fuselage

A cabin is a tube; a fuselage is a tube with a nose and a tail. The nose holds the cockpit, and the tail cone closes the body gently enough for the flow to follow it and steeply enough for the aircraft to rotate at takeoff. Added to the cabin, they give the fuselage length that Module 4 had to guess.

Learning objectives

  • Describe what sets the length and shape of the nose and the cockpit.
  • Relate the tail cone's length to its angle and to the rotation at takeoff.
  • Build up the fuselage length from the cockpit, the cabin and the tail cone, with or without a cabin overlap.
  • Find the business jet's fuselage length and fineness ratio, and compare them with the statistics and a real light jet.

The nose and the cockpit

Ahead of the cabin are the cockpit and the nose. The cockpit is laid out around two pilots side by side: their seats, the rudder pedals, the instrument panel, and a windscreen that lets them see the runway over the nose at the approach attitude and the airspace around them. Ahead of the pilots the nose holds the weather radar and avionics, and often the nose landing gear; in a light jet it also has a baggage bay.

For transports these items add up to about the same length whatever the aircraft's size, about 4 m from the nose to the start of the cabin [2], Chapter 6. The nose is shaped so that it reaches the full diameter about 1.7 \(\dF\) behind its tip: a shorter nose is blunt and adds drag, a longer one wastes length. The business jet keeps the 4 m, with its baggage bay in the nose.

The tail cone

Behind the cabin, the fuselage must close. The flow follows a gently tapering afterbody but separates from a steep one, so the tail cone, or stern, is long: about 3.5 diameters on transports [2], Chapter 6. Its underside also sets how far the aircraft can rotate at takeoff and landing: with the main wheels near the start of the cone, the tail strikes the runway when the pitch angle reaches the cone's angle.

Tail cone length and angle

\[ l_{TC} \approx 3.5\,\dF, \qquad \varphi_{\text{tail}} = \arctan\frac{\dF}{l_{TC}} = \arctan\frac{1}{3.5} = 15.9^\circ \]

\(\varphi_{\text{tail}}\) is the angle of the line from the bottom of the cylinder to the top of the tail; about the most the aircraft may rotate on the ground (Module 8).

An airliner's tail cone is wide enough for seats for some way: the cabin runs about 1.9 \(\dF\) into it, with the rearmost rows, galleys and lavatories where the cabin narrows, and the rear pressure bulkhead well inside the cone. A rear-engine aircraft is different. Its tail cone carries the engines' mounts, and holds the engines' systems and, in a light jet, a baggage bay, so the cabin ends at the start of the cone.

The fuselage length

Fuselage length

\[ \lF = l_{\text{cockpit}} + l_{\text{cabin}} + l_{TC} - l_{\text{overlap}} \]

For an airliner, \(l_{\text{cockpit}} \approx 4\ \text{m}\), \(l_{TC} \approx 3.5\,\dF\) and \(l_{\text{overlap}} \approx 1.9\,\dF\), so \(\lF \approx l_{\text{cabin}} + 4\ \text{m} + 1.6\,\dF\) [2], Chapter 6.

Example 5.1 — The business jet's fuselage

The business jet has a 5.877 m cabin and a 1.597 m fuselage, with its engines on the tail cone. Find its fuselage length with the airliner rule and with no cabin overlap, its fineness ratio, and the length of its tail cone.

Show solution
\[ \text{Airliner rule: } \lF = 5.877 + 4 + 1.6(1.597) = 12.43\ \text{m} \] \[ \text{No overlap: } \lF = 4 + 5.877 + 3.5(1.597) = 15.47\ \text{m} \approx 15.5\ \text{m} \] \[ \frac{\lF}{\dF} = \frac{15.5}{1.597} = 9.7, \qquad l_{TC} = 3.5(1.597) = 5.59\ \text{m} \]

The airliner rule gives what the statistics gave (12.5 to 12.9 m in Lesson 2), because both describe airliners. With its cabin ending at the tail cone, as a rear-engine jet's must, the business jet is 15.5 m long, close to the CJ3 Gen2's 15.6 m. Rounded up to the next 0.1 m, \(\lF = 15.5\ \text{m}\) is the length the lessons use from here on.

Example 5.2 — A shorter tail cone

To save length, a designer shortens the business jet's tail cone to 3 diameters. Find the new fuselage length and tail angle.

Show solution
\[ \lF = 4 + 5.877 + 3(1.597) = 14.67\ \text{m}, \qquad \varphi_{\text{tail}} = \arctan\frac13 = 18.4^\circ \]

0.8 m shorter, with more room to rotate, but the steeper cone risks separated flow and drag. It also leaves less room for the engines, and a shorter tail arm means bigger tails (Lesson 6).

Propellers and the cabin

On a turboprop, no passenger should sit in the plane of the propellers: ice shed from the blades, or a blade that fails, leaves in that plane. The fuselage beside the propellers is reinforced and lined against noise, and the layout often puts a door, a galley or a lavatory there instead of seats. The tool's regional and commuter layouts show the propeller plane against the cabin.

Key takeaways

  • The cockpit and nose take about 4 m ahead of a transport's cabin, nearly independent of size.
  • The tail cone is about \(3.5\,\dF\) long; its angle, \(\arctan(1/3.5) = 15.9^\circ\), limits the rotation on the ground.
  • \(\lF = l_{\text{cockpit}} + l_{\text{cabin}} + l_{TC} - l_{\text{overlap}}\); for airliners \(\lF \approx l_{\text{cabin}} + 4\ \text{m} + 1.6\,\dF\).
  • A rear-engine jet has no overlap: the business jet is 15.5 m long, with a fineness ratio of 9.7.
