Fuselage Length and Fineness

Before anyone draws a seat, statistics give a first fuselage length from the takeoff weight alone. The estimate is good for most classes and poor for some, and the business jet is one of the poor ones. This lesson uses the statistics, checks them against real aircraft, and looks at the shape behind the length: the fineness ratio, and the choice of seats abreast that sets it.

Learning objectives

  • Estimate a fuselage length from the takeoff weight and the class of aircraft.
  • Explain why the estimate fits some designs well and others badly.
  • Define the fineness ratio and explain how it trades skin friction against pressure drag.
  • Explain how the number of seats abreast sets the fuselage's length, diameter and wetted area.

Fuselage length from statistics

Within a class, bigger aircraft have longer fuselages, and the trend is well described by a power law of the takeoff weight:

Statistical fuselage length

\[ \lF = a\,\Wo^{\,C} \]

\(\lF\) in meters, \(\Wo\) in kilograms; \(a\) and \(C\) depend on the class.

Fuselage length against takeoff weight (source: Raymer, Table 6.3, in SI units; check the values against the edition you use)
Class\(a\)\(C\)\(\lF\) at 7000 kg (m)

The exponent says how the length grows with size. For the small classes, \(C \approx 0.23\): an aircraft twice as heavy is only 17% longer, because a light aircraft's fuselage is set by its pilot and a few passengers, and extra weight goes into fuel, engine and structure. For transports and sailplanes, \(C\) is about 0.43 to 0.5: twice the weight is 35 to 41% more length, close to what geometric similarity gives (\(C = 1/3\) if every dimension scales together) and more, because big transports grow longer faster than they grow wider.

Example 2.1 — The business jet's statistical length

The table has no class for business jets. Estimate the business jet's fuselage length (\(\Wo = 6943\ \text{kg}\)) as a jet transport and as a jet trainer, and compare it with the Cessna Citation CJ3 Gen2, a light business jet of similar size: 6291 kg at takeoff and 15.6 m long.

Show solution
\[ \text{Jet transport: } \lF = 0.287(6943)^{0.43} = 12.9\ \text{m} \] \[ \text{Jet trainer: } \lF = 0.333(6943)^{0.41} = 12.5\ \text{m} \]

Both classes give about 12.5 to 13 m, almost 3 m short of the CJ3. The jet-transport line was fitted to airliners, whose fuselages are mostly cabin; a business jet of this weight carries only 8 or 9 passengers, but its fuselage also holds the engines' mounts, a long tail cone and a cockpit as long as an airliner's. Module 4 guessed 14 m. The statistics are a starting point, and Lessons 3 to 5 replace them with a fuselage drawn around the cabin.

Example 2.2 — A jet transport

The Boeing 737-200 in Module 1's data weighs 53 300 kg at takeoff and is 30.5 m long. What does the jet-transport line give?

Show solution
\[ \lF = 0.287(53\,300)^{0.43} = 30.9\ \text{m} \]

Within 2% of the real aircraft. For the class the line was fitted to, the statistics are good enough to start a layout and to check one.

The fineness ratio

The fineness ratio of a fuselage is its length divided by its diameter, \(\lF/\dF\). It measures how slender the body is, and it decides how the body's zero-lift drag divides between two causes:

  • Skin friction grows with the wetted area. For a given volume, a long thin body has more surface than a short fat one.
  • Pressure drag comes from the flow separating behind a body that closes too abruptly. A short fat body has a steep afterbody and a large wake; a slender one closes gently.

A common empirical fit for subsonic bodies combines the two in a form factor, the drag of the body divided by the skin friction of its wetted area:

\[ FF = 1 + \frac{60}{(\lF/\dF)^3} + \frac{\lF/\dF}{400} \]

For bodies of the same shape and the same volume \(V\), the wetted area grows as \(V^{2/3}(\lF/\dF)^{1/3}\), so the drag grows as \(FF\,(\lF/\dF)^{1/3}\). The table shows the result, relative to its minimum:

The minimum is near a fineness ratio of 8, and it is flat: anywhere from 6 to 10 costs less than 4% more drag. Below 5 the pressure drag climbs fast. That is why transports and business jets have fineness ratios of about 8 to 11, and why short, fat fuselages are rare outside aircraft built for bulky cargo.

A transport's fuselage is not designed for a volume, though. Its diameter is fixed by the seats across the cabin, and its length by the rows. The fineness ratio is the outcome of one decision: how many seats abreast.

Seats abreast

For a given number of passengers, more seats abreast make a wider, shorter fuselage; fewer make a narrower, longer one. Lessons 3 and 4 give the rules for the width and the length; Figure 2.2 uses them in advance to show the trade. The fuselage's wetted area has a broad minimum, but every extra aisle (after six seats abreast, and again after twelve) adds width without adding seats, and makes a step up.

Real transports follow a simple statistic for the number of seats abreast in economy class:

Seats abreast (economy)

\[ n_{SA} \approx 0.45\sqrt{n_{\text{PAX}}} \]

Rounded to a whole layout: 150 passengers give 5.5, so 6 abreast with one aisle.

The statistic sits near the wetted-area minimum up to about 200 passengers, then above it. Big aircraft go wider than drag alone would choose because of limits on the length: a long fuselage needs taller landing gear to rotate at takeoff without striking its tail (Lesson 5), must fit the airport's gates and taxiways, takes longer to board and evacuate through one aisle, and is heavier to make stiff in bending.

The business jet's choice

With 8 passengers, the statistic gives \(0.45\sqrt8 = 1.3\) seats abreast. Light business jets seat one passenger on each side of a center aisle, two abreast in all, so that every seat is a window seat beside the aisle. The cabin is then as narrow as two seats and an aisle allow, and the length follows from the rows; Lessons 3 and 4 size both.

Key takeaways

  • A first fuselage length is \(\lF = a\Wo^{C}\), with \(a\) and \(C\) by class; it fits airliners within a few percent.
  • For the business jet the classes give 12.5 to 13 m, short of the 15.6 m of a real light jet of its weight: a layout is needed.
  • The fineness ratio \(\lF/\dF\) trades skin friction against pressure drag; for a fixed volume the drag is least near 8, and is nearly flat from 6 to 10.
  • For a transport the seats abreast set the fineness ratio; statistically \(n_{SA} \approx 0.45\sqrt{n_{\text{PAX}}}\).
