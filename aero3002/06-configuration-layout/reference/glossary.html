A

Back to top
Aisle height
The height from the floor to the ceiling in the aisle. Airliners give over 76 in; small aircraft about 60 in. The business jet's 52 in makes a sit-down cabin.
See: Lesson 3Related: Cabin floor, Dropped aisle
Aisle width
The clear width of the aisle. CS/FAR 25.815 [5], [6] sets the least width below and above 25 in from the floor by passenger count: 12/15 in for 10 or fewer, 12/20 in for 11 to 19, 15/20 in for 20 or more. Airliners have 19 to 20 in at the armrests.
See: Lesson 3Related: Number of aisles, Inner cabin width \(\dI\)

B

Back to top
Baggage and cargo density
The mass per volume used to size the baggage and cargo holds: about 170 kg/m³ for baggage and 160 kg/m³ for cargo [2].
See: Lesson 4Related: Cabin length
Buttline (BL)
The distance to the left or right of the aircraft's plane of symmetry.
See: Lesson 1Related: Fuselage station (FS), Waterline (WL)

C

Back to top
Cabin crew
The minimum number of cabin attendants: in Europe [9], and for private flights in the United States [7], none up to 19 seats, then one for every 50 seats or part of 50. US airline rules [8] ask for the first from 10 seats.
See: Lesson 4Related: Emergency exits
Cabin cross-section
A cut through the cabin showing the seats, aisle, floor and walls; it fixes the fuselage diameter.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 3Related: Inner cabin width \(\dI\), Wall thickness \(\Delta d\), Cabin plan
Cabin floor
A chord of the cabin's circle. Airliners put it a little below the axis, with a hold underneath; small cabins put it as low as the circle allows.
See: Lesson 3Related: Aisle height, Dropped aisle
Cabin length
The rows times the seat pitch, plus the lavatories (about 40 in each), galleys (about 30 in each) and an allowance for doors, exits and stowage. The business jet's is 5.88 m.
See: Lesson 4Related: Seat pitch, Quick cabin-length rule, Cabin plan
Cabin overlap
The part of an airliner's cabin inside the tail cone, about \(1.9\,\dF\). A rear-engine jet has none: its tail cone carries the engines.
See: Lesson 5Related: Tail cone, Fuselage length \(\lF\)
Cabin plan
The cabin seen from above, with its seats, aisles, lavatories, galleys and doors; it fixes the cabin length.
See: Lesson 1, Lesson 4Related: Cabin length, Cabin cross-section
Cockpit and nose
The part of the fuselage ahead of the cabin, holding the pilots, the instruments, the radar and often the nose gear: about 4 m on transports, nearly independent of size.
See: Lesson 5Related: Fuselage length \(\lF\), Tail cone
Configuration layout
The drawing of the aircraft that makes its contents fit, puts each item where it works, and measures what the sizing had to guess. Transports are laid out from the inside, cabin first.
See: Lesson 1Related: Three-view, Cabin cross-section

D

Back to top
Dropped aisle
A floor lowered in the aisle alone, with the seats on ledges at either side, to gain standing height in a small cabin.
See: Lesson 3Related: Aisle height, Cabin floor

E

Back to top
Emergency exits
Openings sized by type, from Type A (42 by 72 in, floor level) to Type IV (19 by 26 in, over the wing). CS/FAR 25.807 [5], [6] sets how many of each the passenger seats need on each side of the fuselage.
See: Lesson 4Related: Cabin crew
Equivalent skin-friction coefficient \(C_{fe}\)
The zero-lift drag per unit of wetted area, typical of a class: 0.0030 for transports, 0.0045 for light twins, 0.0055 for light singles [1]. \(\CDz = C_{fe}\Swet/\Sref\).
See: Lesson 8Related: Wetted-area ratio, Refined \(\LDmax\)
Exposed area \(S_{\text{exp}}\)
The wing's planform outside the fuselage: the reference area less the nearly trapezoidal part inside, \(S - \dF(c_r + c_{\text{side}})/2\). Only this part is wetted.
See: Lesson 8Related: Wetted area \(\Swet\)

F

Back to top
Fineness ratio
A fuselage's length over its diameter, \(\lF/\dF\). For a fixed volume, subsonic drag is least near 8 and nearly flat from 6 to 10; transports are about 8 to 11. The business jet's is 9.7.
See: Lesson 2Related: Form factor \(FF\), Seats abreast \(n_{SA}\)
First wing position
Before the weights are known: \(x_{ac,W} \approx (0.92 - L_t/\lF)\,\lF\), one tail arm ahead of tails at 92% of the length. The business jet's is FS 6.98 m.
See: Lesson 6Related: Tail arm
Form factor \(FF\)
A body's drag divided by the skin friction of its wetted area. A common fit for subsonic bodies is \(1 + 60/(\lF/\dF)^3 + (\lF/\dF)/400\) [1].
See: Lesson 2Related: Fineness ratio
Fuel density
About 800 kg/m³ for jet fuel, a few percent more or less with the grade and temperature. The business jet's 1991 kg of fuel is 2.49 m³.
See: Lesson 7Related: Wing tank volume
Fuselage diameter \(\dF\)
The outer diameter: the cabin width plus the two walls, \(\dF = \dI + \Delta d\). The business jet's is 1.597 m.
See: Lesson 3Related: Inner cabin width \(\dI\), Wall thickness \(\Delta d\)
Fuselage length \(\lF\)
The cockpit, the cabin and the tail cone, less any overlap of the cabin into the cone. For airliners, about \(l_{\text{cabin}} + 4\ \text{m} + 1.6\,\dF\) [2]. The business jet's is 15.5 m.
See: Lesson 5Related: Statistical fuselage length, Tail cone, Cabin overlap
Fuselage station (FS)
The distance aft of a reference plane at or ahead of the nose. An arm between two parts is the difference of their stations.
See: Lesson 1Related: Waterline (WL), Buttline (BL)

G

Back to top
Galley
The cabin's kitchen, sized by volume: 1 to 2 ft³ per economy passenger, 5 to 8 in first class [1]. A galley unit is about 30 in deep.
See: Lesson 4Related: Lavatory, Cabin length

I

Back to top
Inboard profile
A side view of the layout cut open to show where the internal items go: tanks, landing gear, engines and systems.
See: Lesson 1Related: Three-view
Inner cabin width \(\dI\)
The cabin's width at the armrests: the benches plus the aisles plus about 1 in at each wall. For a circular cabin, its inner diameter. The business jet's is 1.448 m.
See: Lesson 3Related: Seat bench, Aisle width, Wall thickness \(\Delta d\)

L

Back to top
Lavatory
About 40 by 40 in; one per 40 to 60 economy passengers, or 10 to 20 in first class [1].
See: Lesson 4Related: Galley, Cabin length

N

Back to top
Nacelle
The streamlined housing of an engine. Until Module 7 sizes the engine, the lessons take the business jet's as 0.8 m by 2.4 m and their wetted area as a cylinder's, \(\pi Dl\).
See: Lesson 7, Lesson 8Related: Rear-mounted engines, Wetted area \(\Swet\)
Number of aisles
CS/FAR 25.817 [5], [6] allows no more than three seats on each side of a single aisle: one aisle up to six seats abreast, two up to twelve.
See: Lesson 3Related: Aisle width, Seats abreast \(n_{SA}\)

P

Back to top
Propeller plane
The plane swept by the propeller blades. No passenger should sit in it: shed ice and a failed blade leave in that plane.
See: Lesson 5Related: Rear-mounted engines

Q

Back to top
Quick cabin-length rule
For an economy airliner, about 1 m of cabin per row, lavatories, galleys and doors included: \(l_{\text{cabin}} \approx n_R(1\ \text{m})\). It does not suit small cabins.
See: Lesson 4Related: Cabin length

R

Back to top
Rear pressure bulkhead
The dome or wall that closes the pressurized cabin at its rear. Rear-mounted engines go behind it, so that a burst disc cannot reach the passengers.
See: Lesson 5, Lesson 7Related: Rear-mounted engines, Cabin overlap
Rear-mounted engines
Engines on pylons at the sides of the rear fuselage: a clean wing, short gear and little engine-out yaw, at the cost of an aft center of gravity and a tail kept out of the exhaust.
See: Lesson 7Related: Nacelle, Rear pressure bulkhead
Refined \(\LDmax\)
\(\tfrac12\sqrt{\pi Ae/(C_{fe}\Swet/\Sref)}\) with the layout's wetted area. The business jet's rises from 16.9 (sketch) to 17.3.
See: Lesson 8Related: Equivalent skin-friction coefficient \(C_{fe}\), Wetted-area ratio

S

Back to top
Seat bench
A row of one to three seats sharing armrests. Widths across the armrests: economy 21, 40 and 60 in for one, two and three seats; business 25, 48 and 72 in [2].
See: Lesson 3Related: Inner cabin width \(\dI\), Seat pitch
Seat pitch
The distance from a point on one seat to the same point on the next: 38 to 40 in in first class, 29 to 36 in economy, 30 to 32 at high density.
See: Lesson 4Related: Cabin length, Seat bench
Seats abreast \(n_{SA}\)
The seats across one row. Statistically \(n_{SA} \approx 0.45\sqrt{n_{\text{PAX}}}\) in economy [2]; together with the passengers it sets the fuselage's diameter, length and fineness.
See: Lesson 2Related: Fineness ratio, Number of aisles
Statistical fuselage length
\(\lF = a\Wo^{C}\), with \(a\) and \(C\) by class. Good for airliners; for the business jet the classes give 12.5 to 13 m against a real light jet's 15.6 m [12].
See: Lesson 2Related: Fuselage length \(\lF\)

T

Back to top
Tail angle \(\varphi_{\text{tail}}\)
\(\arctan(\dF/l_{TC})\): 15.9° for a cone of 3.5 diameters. About the most the aircraft may rotate on the ground before the tail strikes.
See: Lesson 5Related: Tail cone
Tail arm
The distance from the wing's quarter-MAC point to a tail's: about 60% of the fuselage with a nose propeller, 52% with engines on the wing, 45 to 48% with engines at the rear.
See: Lesson 6Related: First wing position, Tail cone
Tail cone
The tapering rear of the fuselage, about \(3.5\,\dF\) long on transports, gentle enough for the flow to follow it.
See: Lesson 5Related: Tail angle \(\varphi_{\text{tail}}\), Cabin overlap
Thickness taper \(\tau\)
The tip's thickness ratio over the root's, \((t/c)_t/(t/c)_r\).
See: Lesson 7Related: Wing tank volume, Wetted area \(\Swet\)
Three-view
A side, top and front view drawn to scale and lined up, so that each point has the same station in the side and top views and the same height in the side and front views.
See: Lesson 1Related: Inboard profile, Cabin cross-section

W

Back to top
Wall thickness \(\Delta d\)
The two walls together, left and right: \(0.084\ \text{m} + 0.045\,\dI\) for pressurized transports [2].
See: Lesson 3Related: Fuselage diameter \(\dF\), Inner cabin width \(\dI\)
Waterline (WL)
The height above a reference plane, often below the aircraft; the name comes from flying boats.
See: Lesson 1Related: Fuselage station (FS), Buttline (BL)
Wetted area \(\Swet\)
The surface the air touches. Fuselage: \(\pi\dF\lF(1 - 2\dF/\lF)^{2/3}(1 + \dF^2/\lF^2)\); wing or tail: \(2S_{\text{exp}}[1 + 0.25(t/c)_r(1 + \tau\lambda)/(1 + \lambda)]\) [2], [4].
See: Lesson 8Related: Exposed area \(S_{\text{exp}}\), Wetted-area ratio
Wetted-area ratio
\(\Swet/\Sref\): 5.23 for the business jet's layout, against 5.5 for Module 3's sketch. With \(C_{fe}\) it gives \(\CDz\).
See: Lesson 8Related: Wetted area \(\Swet\), Refined \(\LDmax\)
Wing tank volume
\(0.54(S^{1.5}/\sqrt A)(t/c)_r(1 + \lambda\sqrt\tau + \lambda^2\tau)/(1 + \lambda)^2\), without a center tank [2], [4]: about 60% of the wing's volume. The business jet's wing holds 2.74 m³.
See: Lesson 7Related: Thickness taper \(\tau\), Fuel density
