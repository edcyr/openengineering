Parking-lot physics

Drive a car slowly around a tight car park. There is almost no lateral acceleration, so the tires need almost no lateral force, and therefore almost no slip angle (Lesson 2.1). Each wheel simply rolls in the direction it points. That one fact decides the whole steering geometry at low speed, and it gives the baseline that every high-speed handling effect in this module is measured against.

Kinematic turning

If every wheel rolls without slipping sideways, every wheel must travel on a circle, and all those circles must share one center: the turn center. The rear wheels don't steer, so each one moves along its own heading, and the turn center has to lie on the line of the rear axle, extended. For the front wheel to roll on a circle about the same point, its heading must be at right angles to the line joining it to the turn center.

Treat the car as a single track, a “bicycle” with one wheel per axle on the centerline, and the geometry is one right-angled triangle. With wheelbase \(L\) and turn radius \(R\) measured to the rear-axle center:

\[ \begin{aligned} \tan\delta &= \frac{L}{R} \\[4pt] \delta &\approx \frac{L}{R} \quad (R \gg L) \end{aligned} \]

with \(\delta\) in radians in the small-angle form. This is the Ackermann steer angle. For the club racer (\(L = 2.45\) m) it is 13.8° in a 10 m hairpin but only 1.4° in a 100 m corner. It is the road-wheel angle; the steering wheel turns further, by the steering ratio (typically 10–18 to 1).

Inner and outer wheels

A real car has two front wheels a track width \(t\) apart, and they are on different circles. The inside wheel is on the smaller circle, so it needs more steer than the outside wheel:

\[ \tan\delta_i = \frac{L}{R - t/2}, \qquad \tan\delta_o = \frac{L}{R + t/2} \]

Getting both right at once is the Ackermann condition, \(\cot\delta_o - \cot\delta_i = t/L\). The rear wheels, meanwhile, follow a tighter path than the fronts: the front-axle center is on a circle of radius \(\sqrt{R^2 + L^2}\), so the rear off-tracks toward the inside, which is why long vehicles clip kerbs with their rear wheels.

Ackermann, parallel and anti-Ackermann steering

A steering linkage can give the inside wheel more steer by angling the steering arms. The classic rule of thumb, patented for horse-drawn carriages in 1818 by Rudolph Ackermann, is to point both arms so that their extensions meet at the center of the rear axle:

Engineers describe a linkage by its percentage Ackermann: 100% matches the low-speed geometry exactly, 0% (parallel steer) turns both wheels by the same angle, and a negative value (anti-Ackermann) gives the outside wheel more steer. Anything other than 100% makes the front tires disagree about where the turn center is, so at walking pace they scrub sideways against each other.

So why do many race cars run less than 100%, and some run anti-Ackermann? At racing speeds the geometric difference is tiny (a few hundredths of a degree in a 100 m corner), while the tires run several degrees of slip angle. The heavily loaded outside tire typically reaches its peak force at a slightly larger slip angle than the lightly loaded inside tire. Full Ackermann gives the inside tire more steer, dragging it past its peak while the outside tire, which does most of the work, still has more to give. Less Ackermann shares the slip angles more usefully. The cost is tire scrub in very slow corners. Module 8 returns to steering geometry.

The club racer (\(L = 2.45\) m, \(t = 1.50\) m) creeps around a hairpin with its rear-axle center on a 10 m radius. Find the steer angles each front wheel needs, and what parallel steering would do.

Bicycle angle: \(\delta = \arctan(2.45/10) = 13.77°\). The small-angle value \(L/R = 0.245\) rad \(= 14.04°\) is 2% high at this tight radius.

Inside wheel: \(\arctan(2.45/9.25) = 14.83°\). Outside wheel: \(\arctan(2.45/10.75) = 12.84°\). The inside wheel needs 2.0° more.

Parallel steer at 13.77°: the inside wheel is 1.07° short and the outside 0.93° too far, so both front tires run about 1° of slip angle in opposite directions, scrubbing even at walking pace.

Off-tracking: the front-axle center runs on \(\sqrt{10^2 + 2.45^2} = 10.30\) m, so the rear cuts 30 cm inside the front.

“Ackermann geometry is what makes the car turn.” The car turns because the front wheels are steered. Ackermann geometry only decides how the steer is shared between the inside and outside wheels, so that neither scrubs at low speed. At racing speeds slip angles, not geometry, decide where each tire points relative to its path.

Check your understanding

A Formula SAE car with a 1.55 m wheelbase drives around a 9 m radius skidpad at walking pace (radius measured to the rear-axle center). What steer angle does the bicycle model give?

\(\tan\delta = L/R\). The small-angle value \(L/R\) in degrees is close enough too.

\(\delta = \arctan(1.55/9) = 9.77°\) (the small-angle value is \(0.172\) rad \(= 9.87°\)).

For the club racer in the worked example (\(L = 2.45\) m, \(t = 1.50\) m, \(R = 10\) m to the rear-axle center), what steer angle does the inside front wheel need?

The inside wheel is \(t/2\) closer to the turn center: \(\tan\delta_i = L/(R - t/2)\).

\(\delta_i = \arctan(2.45/9.25) = 14.83°\).

A road car with a 2.65 m wheelbase turns with its rear-axle center on a 6 m radius. How far inside the front-axle path does the rear axle run?

The front-axle center is on a circle of radius \(\sqrt{R^2 + L^2}\).

\(\sqrt{6^2 + 2.65^2} - 6 = 6.559 - 6 = 0.559\) m.

At walking pace, why must the turn center lie on the extension of the rear axle?

  • Because the CG is close to the rear axle.The CG position doesn't enter the low-speed geometry.
  • Because the rear wheels aren't steered, so to roll without sliding each one must move along its own heading, at right angles to the line to the turn center.Only points on the rear-axle line are perpendicular to both rear wheels' headings.
  • Because the driver steers the front wheels toward it.The front wheels' steer has to match the center; it doesn't choose its line.

A car with parallel steering creeps around a tight car park. What happens at the front tires?

  • Nothing: the tires are rolling so slowly that geometry doesn't matter.At walking pace geometry is all that matters, because there is no lateral acceleration to explain any slip angle.
  • The inside wheel is steered too little and the outside too much, so both run small slip angles in opposite directions and scrub against each other.They disagree about where the turn center is.
  • The car turns on a larger radius than the driver asked for, but the tires don't slip.With two front wheels pointing at different centers, at least one tire must slip.

Why do many race cars use less than full Ackermann?

  • Because Ackermann linkages are too heavy.The linkage is the same parts with different arm angles.
  • At racing speeds the slip angles dwarf the geometric difference, and the lightly loaded inside tire peaks at a smaller slip angle, so giving it extra steer only drags it past its peak.Less Ackermann shares the slip more usefully between the tires that do the work.
  • Because race cars only turn at high speed.They also use slow hairpins, where less Ackermann does cost some scrub.
