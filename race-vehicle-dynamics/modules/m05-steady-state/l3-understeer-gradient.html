One number for balance

Lesson 1.2 described understeer and oversteer loosely, by which axle “wins”, and promised a critical speed \(\sqrt{L/|K|}\) without saying where \(K\) came from. The bicycle model of Lesson 5.2 now delivers both. This lesson turns the difference between the front and rear slip angles into a single number, the understeer gradient, and follows what it says about stability, the driver's steering, and the car at its limit.

The understeer gradient

From Lesson 5.2, in a right turn \(\delta = L/R + |\alpha_f| - |\alpha_r|\), with \(|\alpha_f| = m a_y (b/L)/C_f\) and \(|\alpha_r| = m a_y (a/L)/C_r\). Writing \(m_f = m\,b/L\) and \(m_r = m\,a/L\) for the mass carried by each axle:

\[ \delta = \frac{L}{R} + K\,a_y, \qquad K = \frac{m_f}{C_f} - \frac{m_r}{C_r} \]

\(K\) is the understeer gradient: the extra steer needed per unit of lateral acceleration. With \(C\) in N/rad and \(a_y\) in m/s², \(K\) is in rad/(m/s²); engineers usually quote it in degrees per g. It defines the three classic behaviors precisely:

  • Understeer (\(K > 0\)): the steer needed grows with lateral acceleration. The front axle needs more slip angle than the rear.
  • Neutral steer (\(K = 0\)): the steer stays at the low-speed value \(L/R\).
  • Oversteer (\(K < 0\)): the steer needed falls as lateral acceleration grows.

Anything that reduces an axle's cornering stiffness relative to the weight it carries pushes the balance toward that end: a heavier front or narrower front tires add understeer. So do the Module 3 set-up tools, because more load transfer on an axle lowers its effective cornering stiffness through load sensitivity. Road cars are typically set up with a few deg/g of understeer; race cars usually run much closer to neutral.

Understeer and oversteer describe how the needed steer changes with lateral acceleration, not whether any tire is sliding. In the linear range no tire is at its limit: the axle that needs more slip angle per unit of the weight it carries sets the balance.

Characteristic speed and critical speed

In a steady turn \(r = V/R\) and \(a_y = V^2/R\), so \(\delta = (L + K V^2)/R\). The car's steady yaw-rate gain, the yaw rate it settles at per unit of steer, is then

\[ \frac{r}{\delta} = \frac{V/L}{1 + K V^2/L} \]
  • Neutral steer: the gain grows in proportion to speed.
  • Understeer: the gain rises to a maximum at the characteristic speed \(V_{\text{ch}} = \sqrt{L/K}\), where it is half the neutral car's, then falls. The car gets steadily less responsive at high speed.
  • Oversteer: the denominator reaches zero at the critical speed \(V_{\text{crit}} = \sqrt{L/|K|}\). The gain becomes infinite: the car is unstable, as Lesson 1.2 showed (where it was written \(U_{\text{crit}}\)).

The same equation explains the constant-radius test: drive faster and faster around one circle and record the steer. An understeering car needs more and more steer, a neutral car the same, and an oversteering car less and less, reaching zero at the critical speed and needing opposite lock above it.

Neutral steer point and static margin

There is another way to see the same balance. Push the car sideways without making it yaw, so that it slides at a small sideslip angle \(\beta\). Both axles get the same slip angle, so the front tires push back with \(C_f\beta\) and the rears with \(C_r\beta\). Their resultant acts at the neutral steer point (NSP), a distance \(x_{\text{NSP}}\) behind the front axle:

\[ \begin{aligned} x_{\text{NSP}} &= \frac{C_r}{C_f + C_r}\,L \\[4pt] \text{SM} &= \frac{x_{\text{NSP}} - a}{L} \end{aligned} \]

The static margin SM is the distance from the CG back to the NSP, as a fraction of the wheelbase. When the NSP is behind the CG (positive margin), a disturbance that swings the car sideways creates tire forces that turn its nose back into line, like the feathers on the arrow in Lesson 1.2: the car understeers and is stable. With the NSP ahead of the CG the forces turn it further away. The two descriptions agree: \(K = m\,\text{SM}\,(C_f + C_r)/(C_f C_r)\), so the static margin and the understeer gradient always have the same sign.

Pair analysis and the handling diagram

The understeer gradient assumes linear tires. Near the limit, each axle's lateral force curve flattens (Lesson 2.1), and the two axles do so at different points. RCVD's pair analysis handles this by plotting each axle's force divided by the weight it carries against its slip angle. In a steady turn both axles must deliver \(F_y/W_{\text{axle}} = a_y/g\) (Lesson 5.2), so at any lateral acceleration you can read off both slip angles, and their difference is the extra steer \(\delta - L/R = |\alpha_f| - |\alpha_r|\).

Plotting lateral acceleration against that extra steer gives the handling diagram. Its slope at the origin is \(K\). Near the limit it shows what the linear model can't: whichever axle runs out of grip first decides the car's behavior at the limit. If the front saturates first, the extra steer grows without bound (limit understeer: the car runs wide, however much the driver steers). If the rear saturates first, it falls without bound (limit oversteer: the car spins). This is the same balance Modules 3 and 4 tuned with load transfer and aero balance.

Find the club racer's understeer gradient, characteristic speed and static margin (\(m = 750\) kg, 52% front weight, \(L = 2.45\) m, \(a = 1.176\) m, \(C_f = 2000\) N/deg, \(C_r = 3000\) N/deg).

Gradient: \(m_f = 390\) kg and \(m_r = 360\) kg, so \(K = 390/2000 - 360/3000 = 0.075\) deg per m/s² \(= 0.075 \times 9.81 = 0.74\) deg/g.

Characteristic speed: \(K = 0.075 \times \pi/180 = 1.309 \times 10^{-3}\) rad/(m/s²), so \(V_{\text{ch}} = \sqrt{2.45/0.001309} = 43.3\) m/s \(= 156\) km/h.

Static margin: \(x_{\text{NSP}} = 2.45 \times 3000/5000 = 1.47\) m, 0.29 m behind the CG, so \(\text{SM} = 0.294/2.45 = 12\%\).

Swap the tires (\(C_f = 3000\), \(C_r = 2000\) N/deg): \(K = 0.13 - 0.18 = -0.05\) deg per m/s², or \(-0.49\) deg/g. The car now oversteers, with a critical speed of \(\sqrt{2.45/0.000873} = 53\) m/s \(= 191\) km/h.

“Understeer means the front tires are sliding.” In the linear range neither axle is sliding: an understeering car simply needs more slip angle at the front than at the rear. Running wide because the front tires have reached their limit is limit understeer, which the handling diagram shows separately. A car can understeer gently in the linear range and still oversteer at the limit.

Check your understanding

The road car has \(m = 1400\) kg, 60% front weight, \(C_f = 2000\) N/deg and \(C_r = 2600\) N/deg. What is its understeer gradient in deg/g?

\(K = m_f/C_f - m_r/C_r\) with \(C\) in N/deg gives deg per m/s²; multiply by 9.81 for deg/g.

\(m_f = 840\) kg, \(m_r = 560\) kg. \(K = 840/2000 - 560/2600 = 0.420 - 0.215 = 0.205\) deg per m/s² \(= 2.01\) deg/g.

A car with a 2.45 m wheelbase has an understeer gradient of 0.736 deg/g. What is its characteristic speed?

Convert \(K\) to rad/(m/s²): multiply by \(\pi/180\) and divide by 9.81. Then \(V_{\text{ch}} = \sqrt{L/K}\).

\(K = 0.736 \times 0.01745/9.81 = 1.309 \times 10^{-3}\) rad/(m/s²). \(V_{\text{ch}} = \sqrt{2.45/0.001309} = 43.3\) m/s \(= 156\) km/h.

A car with a 2.5 m wheelbase has its CG 1.20 m behind the front axle and equal front and rear axle cornering stiffness. What is its static margin, as a percentage of the wheelbase?

With equal stiffness the neutral steer point is at mid-wheelbase.

\(x_{\text{NSP}} = 1.25\) m, so \(\text{SM} = (1.25 - 1.20)/2.5 = 0.02 = 2\%\): a small margin, so the car understeers only slightly.

What happens to an oversteering car as it approaches its critical speed?

  • It needs more and more steer to hold a corner.That describes an understeering car.
  • Its yaw response to steering grows without limit and it becomes unstable; the steer needed for a given corner falls to zero.\(r/\delta = (V/L)/(1 + K V^2/L)\) blows up when \(1 + K V^2/L = 0\).
  • It becomes neutral steer.\(K\) is a property of the car and doesn't change with speed in this model.

A team moves ballast rearward, shifting weight from the front axle to the rear, with the same tires. How does the understeer gradient change?

  • It falls (less understeer, toward oversteer), because \(m_f/C_f\) shrinks and \(m_r/C_r\) grows.The rear now needs more slip angle per unit of its stiffness.
  • It rises, because the rear tires have more grip.More load raises grip, but in the linear range what matters is load per unit of cornering stiffness.
  • It doesn't change; only tire stiffness affects \(K\).\(K\) depends on the mass each axle carries as well as its stiffness.

On a car's handling diagram, the curve bends sharply toward negative extra steer just before the limit. What does that mean?

  • The front axle saturates first: limit understeer.Front saturation makes the extra steer grow, bending the curve the other way.
  • The rear axle saturates first: limit oversteer, and the car will tend to spin at the limit.The rear slip angle grows without bound, so the extra steer \(|\alpha_f| - |\alpha_r|\) falls.
  • The car is neutral at the limit.A neutral car's curve would stay vertical.
