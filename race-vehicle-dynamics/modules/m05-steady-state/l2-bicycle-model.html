Putting the pieces together

You already have every ingredient. Lesson 0.2 gave the slip angles that the car's motion creates at each axle. Lesson 2.1 gave the force a tire makes for a given slip angle. Lesson 0.1 gave the force and moment balance of a car in a steady turn. Put them together and you get the bicycle model, the simplest model that predicts how much a driver must steer, and how the car is angled to its path, at any speed.

Lumping each axle into one tire

The model treats each axle as a single tire on the car's centerline, which is why it is called the bicycle (or single-track) model. That is reasonable when:

  • the angles are small, so the left and right tires on an axle run at nearly the same slip angle;
  • the tires are in their linear range, \(F_y = -C\,\alpha\) (SAE signs, Lesson 0.2), where \(C\) is the axle cornering stiffness, the sum of the two tires' values;
  • the speed is constant, the road is flat, and roll, load transfer and aerodynamics are ignored, or folded into the cornering stiffnesses.

The three equations

Kinematics (Lesson 0.2): the yaw rate adds a sideways velocity \(a\,r\) at the front axle and subtracts \(b\,r\) at the rear, so

\[ \alpha_f = \beta + \frac{a\,r}{V} - \delta, \qquad \alpha_r = \beta - \frac{b\,r}{V} \]

Forces and moments (Lesson 0.1): in a steady turn the tire forces supply the centripetal force, and the yaw moments about the CG balance, because the yaw rate is constant:

\[ \begin{aligned} F_{yf} + F_{yr} &= m\,a_y = m\,\frac{V^2}{R} \\[4pt] a\,F_{yf} - b\,F_{yr} &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

Solving these two together:

\[ F_{yf} = m\,a_y\,\frac{b}{L}, \qquad F_{yr} = m\,a_y\,\frac{a}{L} \]

Each axle supplies a share of the cornering force equal to its share of the car's weight, the result Lesson 4.2 used for aero balance. Tires (Lesson 2.1): with linear tires, each axle's slip angle is its force divided by its cornering stiffness:

\[ \alpha_f = -\frac{F_{yf}}{C_f}, \qquad \alpha_r = -\frac{F_{yr}}{C_r} \]

Steer and sideslip in a steady turn

In a steady turn the yaw rate is \(r = V/R\) (Lesson 0.1). Subtracting the two kinematic equations removes \(\beta\): \(\alpha_f - \alpha_r = L\,r/V - \delta = L/R - \delta\). So the steer angle the driver must hold is

\[ \delta = \frac{L}{R} - \alpha_f + \alpha_r \]

In a right turn both slip angles are negative, so this reads \(\delta = L/R + |\alpha_f| - |\alpha_r|\): the low-speed Ackermann angle of Lesson 5.1, plus the difference between the front and rear slip angles. If the front tires need more slip angle than the rears, the driver has to steer more than at walking pace. Lesson 5.3 turns that difference into the understeer gradient.

The rear kinematic equation then gives the sideslip at the CG:

\[ \beta = \alpha_r + \frac{b}{R} \]

At walking pace \(\alpha_r \approx 0\) and \(\beta = b/R\): the CG moves slightly toward the inside of where the car points. As speed rises the rear slip angle grows, \(\beta\) falls through zero, and at racing speeds the car's nose points into the corner, inside its direction of travel.

At speed, the steer the driver needs is the low-speed geometry plus the difference between the front and rear slip angles. The car's balance lives in that difference.

The club racer (\(m = 750\) kg, 52% front weight, \(L = 2.45\) m, \(C_f = 2000\) N/deg, \(C_r = 3000\) N/deg) takes a 50 m radius at 20 m/s (72 km/h). Find the slip angles, the steer angle and the sideslip.

Lateral acceleration: \(a_y = 20^2/50 = 8.0\) m/s² \((0.82\) g).

Axle forces: \(F_{yf} = 750 \times 8.0 \times 0.52 = 3120\) N and \(F_{yr} = 750 \times 8.0 \times 0.48 = 2880\) N.

Slip angles: \(|\alpha_f| = 3120/2000 = 1.56°\) and \(|\alpha_r| = 2880/3000 = 0.96°\).

Steer: \(L/R = 2.45/50 = 0.049\) rad \(= 2.81°\), so \(\delta = 2.81 + 1.56 - 0.96 = 3.41°\): 0.60° more than at walking pace.

Sideslip: \(b = 0.52 \times 2.45 = 1.274\) m, so \(\beta = -0.96° + (1.274/50)\,\text{rad} = -0.96° + 1.46° = +0.50°\). The CG is still moving slightly inside where the car points.

“A car points in the direction it is going.” Only at one speed. At walking pace the CG travels slightly inside the car's heading; at racing speeds the car points into the corner, with the rear tires running a larger slip angle than the geometry alone would give. A drifting car is the extreme case.

What the model leaves out

  • Saturating tires: real tire forces stop growing near the peak (Lesson 2.1). Lesson 5.3 extends the analysis to the limit.
  • Load transfer and roll: the outside tire is more heavily loaded than the inside one (Module 3), and load sensitivity reduces each axle's effective cornering stiffness. These effects can be folded into \(C_f\) and \(C_r\).
  • Transients: the model above is for a steady turn. Module 6 adds the yaw and lateral dynamics that Lesson 1.2's interactives already used.

Check your understanding

A Formula SAE car (280 kg, 47% front weight) corners steadily at 1.5 g. What lateral force does its front axle supply?

\(F_{yf} = m\,a_y\,b/L\), and \(b/L\) is the static front weight fraction.

\(F_{yf} = 280 \times 1.5 \times 9.81 \times 0.47 = 1937\) N.

The club racer (750 kg, 48% of its weight on the rear axle, \(C_r = 3000\) N/deg) corners at 1.0 g. What is the size of the rear slip angle?

Find \(F_{yr} = m a_y\,a/L\), then divide by \(C_r\).

\(F_{yr} = 750 \times 9.81 \times 0.48 = 3532\) N, so \(|\alpha_r| = 3532/3000 = 1.18°\).

The same club racer (\(C_f = 2000\) N/deg, 52% front weight, \(L = 2.45\) m) takes an 80 m radius at 25 m/s. What steer angle does it need?

Find \(a_y\), then each axle's force and slip angle, then \(\delta = L/R + |\alpha_f| - |\alpha_r|\).

\(a_y = 625/80 = 7.81\) m/s². \(|\alpha_f| = 750 \times 7.81 \times 0.52/2000 = 1.52°\), \(|\alpha_r| = 750 \times 7.81 \times 0.48/3000 = 0.94°\). \(L/R = 0.0306\) rad \(= 1.75°\), so \(\delta = 1.75 + 1.52 - 0.94 = 2.34°\).

In a steady turn, why does each axle supply a share of the cornering force equal to its share of the car's weight?

  • Because tire force is proportional to load.That is the friction limit, but the bicycle model's tires are not at their limit.
  • Because the yaw moments about the CG must balance: \(a\,F_{yf} = b\,F_{yr}\), and the axle closer to the CG carries both more weight and more force.The same lever arms that share out the weight share out the cornering force.
  • Because the front and rear slip angles are equal.They generally aren't; the difference is what the driver steers.

Which assumption lets the bicycle model replace two tires on an axle with one?

  • The car has no mass at the wheels.Unsprung mass doesn't enter this argument.
  • Small angles, so the left and right tires on an axle run at nearly the same slip angle and their forces simply add.Then one tire with the axle's total cornering stiffness behaves the same.
  • The car is turning at walking pace.At walking pace there are no slip angles to model; the bicycle model is for speed.

As a car goes faster around the same corner, what happens to its sideslip angle \(\beta\)?

  • It stays at \(b/R\).That is only the walking-pace value.
  • It falls from \(b/R\) through zero, and at high speed the car's nose points into the corner.\(\beta = \alpha_r + b/R\), and the rear slip angle grows in the negative direction as \(a_y\) rises.
  • It grows, so the car points further out of the corner.The rear slip angle pulls \(\beta\) the other way.

Summary

IdeaKey relation
Slip angles (kinematics)\(\alpha_f = \beta + a r/V - \delta,\quad \alpha_r = \beta - b r/V\)
Axle forces in a steady turn\(F_{yf} = m a_y\,b/L,\quad F_{yr} = m a_y\,a/L\)
Linear tires\(\alpha_f = -F_{yf}/C_f,\quad \alpha_r = -F_{yr}/C_r\)
Steer angle\(\delta = L/R - \alpha_f + \alpha_r\) (right turn: \(L/R + |\alpha_f| - |\alpha_r|\))
Sideslip at the CG\(\beta = \alpha_r + b/R\): positive at low speed, negative at high speed
