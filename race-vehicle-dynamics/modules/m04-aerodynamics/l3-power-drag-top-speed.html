Twice the power, not twice the speed

Give a car twice the engine power and its top speed rises by only about a quarter. Give it a bigger wing and it laps one circuit faster and another slower. Both facts come from the same place: drag, and the power it takes to push the car through the air. This lesson puts power, traction and drag together, and uses them to choose a wing setting for a circuit.

Power is force times speed

An engine delivers power \(P\), not a fixed force. At speed \(V\) the driving force it can put through the wheels is

\[ F_{x,P} = \frac{P}{V} \]

where \(P\) is the power reaching the driven wheels (the engine's output less drivetrain losses, typically 10–15%). This curve falls as speed rises, and at low speed it rises without limit. The tires cannot pass on unlimited force, though: the drive force is also capped by the traction limit of the driven axle. For a rear-wheel-drive car, accelerating moves load onto the rear tires (Lesson 3.1), which raises their limit:

\[ F_{x,\text{trac}} = \frac{\mu\left(W\,a/L + F_{Lr}\right)}{1 - \mu\,h/L} \]

The drive force available is the smaller of the two. Below the crossover speed where they cross, acceleration is traction-limited, and more power would only spin the wheels. Above it, acceleration is power-limited, and more grip would not help.

Derivation: the rear-wheel-drive traction limit Show

1. Moments about the front contact patch

Take drag to act at CG height, and add the inertial load \(m a_x\) at the CG as in Lesson 0.1. Moments about the front contact patch give the rear axle load:

\[ N_r = W\,\frac{a}{L} + F_{Lr} + \left(F_D + m\,a_x\right)\frac{h}{L} \]

2. Use Newton's second law

The net forward force is \(m a_x = F_x - F_D\), so the drag terms cancel and the rear load depends only on the drive force:

\[ N_r = W\,\frac{a}{L} + F_{Lr} + F_x\,\frac{h}{L} \]

3. Set the drive force to the tires' limit

\[ F_x = \mu\left(W\,\frac{a}{L} + F_{Lr} + F_x\,\frac{h}{L}\right) \]

Collect the \(F_x\) terms:

\[ F_x\left(1 - \mu\,\frac{h}{L}\right) = \mu\left(W\,\frac{a}{L} + F_{Lr}\right) \]

The \(1 - \mu h/L\) in the denominator is load transfer helping: the harder the car accelerates, the more load the driven tires carry. It is the mirror image of the rear axle's lock-up limit under braking in Lesson 3.3.

Drag and top speed

What accelerates the car is the drive force minus the drag, \(m a_x = F_x - F_D\). Drag grows with \(V^2\) while the power-limited drive force shrinks with \(1/V\), so they must meet. Where they do, the car can go no faster. Setting \(P/V = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2 C_D A\):

\[ \begin{aligned} P &= \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,C_D A\,V_{\text{top}}^3 \\[4pt] V_{\text{top}} &= \left(\frac{2P}{\rho\,C_D A}\right)^{1/3} \end{aligned} \]

The power needed to overcome drag grows with the cube of speed, so top speed grows only with the cube root of power:

  • 10% more power gives 3.2% more top speed.
  • 10% less drag (\(C_D A\)) gives 3.6% more top speed: a slightly better deal.
  • Twice the power gives 26% more top speed.

This lesson ignores rolling resistance, a force of roughly 1–2% of the tire load. It matters at low speed but is small next to drag at top speed.

The GT car has 370 kW at the wheels and \(C_D A = 1.0\) m². Find its top speed, the power it would need for 320 km/h, and roughly where it stops being traction-limited.

Top speed: \(V_{\text{top}} = \left(\dfrac{2 \times 370\,000}{1.225 \times 1.0}\right)^{1/3} = 84.5\) m/s \(= 304\) km/h.

Power for 320 km/h (88.9 m/s): \(P = 0.5 \times 1.225 \times 1.0 \times 88.9^3 = 430\) kW. That is 16% more power for 5% more speed.

Crossover: ignoring downforce, the traction limit is \(\dfrac{1.5 \times 0.52 \times 12\,753}{1 - 1.5 \times 0.45/2.70} = \dfrac{9947}{0.75} = 13\,263\) N, about 1.04 g. Power takes over when \(P/V\) falls to that: \(V = 370\,000/13\,263 = 27.9\) m/s \(\approx 100\) km/h. (With downforce the traction limit is a little higher, so the widget shows 93 km/h.)

Downforce or drag? Choosing a wing for the circuit

A wing that makes more downforce also makes more drag. Its lift-to-drag ratio \(C_L/C_D\) says how much downforce each newton of drag buys; for a rear wing it is typically around 3 to 5. More wing means:

  • Faster corners (Lesson 4.1), and later braking, because downforce adds grip.
  • Slower straights: lower top speed and weaker acceleration once the car is power-limited.

Which effect wins depends on the circuit. On a tight circuit, most of the lap is spent in or near corners, so teams run the most downforce the car can carry; Monaco is the classic example. On a fast circuit with long straights, such as Monza, they trim the wings back to an optimum somewhere in between.

There is no “best” wing setting, only a best setting for a circuit. It balances the time downforce gains in the corners against the time its drag costs on the straights.

“The car with the highest top speed will set the fastest lap.” Top speed is reached for a moment at the end of the longest straight. On most circuits far more time is spent braking, cornering and accelerating out of corners, where downforce pays. A car trimmed for top speed is often slower over a lap.

Real-world complications

  • Gearing and rev limits can cap top speed before drag does. Formula SAE cars are usually geared for acceleration, not for their theoretical top speed.
  • Slipstreaming: a car close behind another sits in its low-pressure wake and has less drag, so it can pass on the straights, at the cost of downforce in the corners (Lesson 4.1).
  • Adjustable aero: some series let drivers open a slot in the rear wing on the straights to cut drag, partly escaping the trade-off.
  • Cooling: radiators and brake ducts add drag of their own, and hot races need bigger openings.

Check your understanding

A Formula SAE car has 60 kW at the wheels and \(C_D A = 1.2\) m². What is its drag-limited top speed at sea level?

\(V_{\text{top}} = (2P/(\rho C_D A))^{1/3}\) in m/s, then multiply by 3.6.

\(V_{\text{top}} = (2 \times 60\,000/(1.225 \times 1.2))^{1/3} = 81\,633^{1/3} = 43.4\) m/s \(= 156\) km/h. In practice gearing usually limits it below that.

The club racer has \(C_D A = 0.75\) m². How much power does it take just to overcome drag at 200 km/h?

\(P = F_D V = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_D A V^3\).

\(V = 55.56\) m/s. \(P = 0.5 \times 1.225 \times 0.75 \times 55.56^3 = 78\,800\) W \(= 78.8\) kW.

The club racer (no downforce, \(\mu = 1.4\), 52% static front weight, \(h = 0.40\) m, \(L = 2.45\) m) is rear-wheel drive. What is its traction-limited acceleration from a standstill?

At a standstill there is no drag or downforce, so \(a_x = F_{x,\text{trac}}/W = \mu(a/L)/(1 - \mu h/L)\), with \(a/L\) the rear weight fraction.

\(a/L = 0.48\) and \(\mu h/L = 1.4 \times 0.40/2.45 = 0.229\). \(a_x = 1.4 \times 0.48/(1 - 0.229) = 0.672/0.771 = 0.871\) g.

An engine upgrade adds 10% more power. Roughly how much does the drag-limited top speed rise?

  • 10%.The power needed to overcome drag grows with \(V^3\), not \(V\).
  • 5%.That would follow from a \(V^2\) law. Power is force times speed, so it goes as \(V^3\).
  • About 3%.\(V_{\text{top}} \propto P^{1/3}\), and \(1.1^{1/3} = 1.032\).

A team races at a tight street circuit one weekend and a fast circuit with long straights the next. How should their rear-wing setting change?

  • Keep it the same; the best wing setting is a property of the car.The best setting depends on how the lap time splits between corners and straights.
  • Run more wing at the street circuit and trim it back for the fast circuit.Tight circuits reward corner speed; long straights punish drag.
  • Run less wing at the street circuit, because the speeds are low there.Low speeds make the wing's drag cheap, and the corners are where the lap time is.

Why does adding downforce do almost nothing for a car's 0–100 km/h time?

  • Because downforce adds mass.It adds load, never mass.
  • Because at low speed there is very little downforce, and that is where the car is traction-limited.Downforce raises the traction limit only once speed, and dynamic pressure, have built up, by which time power is usually the limit.
  • Because downforce only acts on the front axle.It acts on both, split by the aero balance.
