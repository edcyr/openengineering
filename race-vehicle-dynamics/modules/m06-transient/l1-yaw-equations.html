The journey, not just the destination

Module 5 told you where a car ends up in a steady turn: how much steer, how much yaw rate, how much sideslip. It said nothing about how the car gets there. Yet that is what a driver feels first. Turn the wheel and the car responds in a fraction of a second, crisply or lazily, settling cleanly or swinging past and back. In Lesson 1.2's hands-off test the same car either settled or spun after a gust, depending on speed. This optional module builds the equations that decide all of this. It assumes you have met first-order differential equations.

Yaw moment of inertia

A car resists changes in yaw rate through its yaw moment of inertia \(I_z\), the \(\int \rho^2\,dm\) of its mass about a vertical axis through the CG. Engineers often write it with a radius of gyration \(k\), \(I_z = m k^2\), and compare \(k^2\) with the product of the axle distances \(a\,b\):

\[ I_z = m k^2, \qquad \text{DI} = \frac{k^2}{a\,b} \]

The dynamic index DI is typically 0.8–1.0. With DI = 1, a side force at the front axle produces no instantaneous sideways acceleration at the rear axle, and vice versa: the axles behave as if they were independent. Mid-engined race cars concentrate their mass near the CG, which lowers \(k\) and the dynamic index, and makes the car quicker to yaw. The club racer used here has DI = 0.85, so \(I_z = 0.85 \times 750 \times 1.176 \times 1.274 = 955\) kg·m² (\(k = 1.13\) m).

The two equations of motion

Keep the bicycle model of Lesson 5.2 (constant speed \(V\), small angles, linear tires, SAE signs) but let the sideslip \(\beta\) and yaw rate \(r\) change with time. Newton's law sideways and about the vertical axis give

\[ \begin{aligned} m\,V\,(\dot\beta + r) &= F_{yf} + F_{yr} \\[4pt] I_z\,\dot r &= a\,F_{yf} - b\,F_{yr} \end{aligned} \]

The lateral acceleration is \(a_y = V(\dot\beta + r)\): the direction of travel is \(\psi + \beta\) (Lesson 0.2), and a velocity of fixed size \(V\) turning at rate \(\dot\psi + \dot\beta = r + \dot\beta\) has a sideways acceleration \(V\) times that rate. In a steady turn \(\dot\beta = 0\) and this is the familiar \(a_y = V r\) of Lesson 0.1.

Substituting the tire forces \(F_y = -C\alpha\) and the slip angles of Lesson 0.2 gives two linear first-order equations for the state \((\beta, r)\), driven by the steer \(\delta\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \dot\beta &= -\frac{C_f + C_r}{m V}\,\beta + \left(\frac{b C_r - a C_f}{m V^2} - 1\right) r + \frac{C_f}{m V}\,\delta \\[6pt] \dot r &= \frac{b C_r - a C_f}{I_z}\,\beta - \frac{a^2 C_f + b^2 C_r}{I_z V}\,r + \frac{a\,C_f}{I_z}\,\delta \end{aligned} \]
Derivation: from forces to the state equations Show

1. Tire forces in terms of the state

With \(\alpha_f = \beta + a r/V - \delta\) and \(\alpha_r = \beta - b r/V\):

\[ \begin{aligned} F_{yf} &= -C_f\left(\beta + \frac{a\,r}{V} - \delta\right) \\[4pt] F_{yr} &= -C_r\left(\beta - \frac{b\,r}{V}\right) \end{aligned} \]

2. Total side force and yaw moment

\[ \begin{aligned} F_{yf} + F_{yr} &= -(C_f + C_r)\,\beta + (b C_r - a C_f)\,\frac{r}{V} + C_f\,\delta \\[4pt] a F_{yf} - b F_{yr} &= (b C_r - a C_f)\,\beta - (a^2 C_f + b^2 C_r)\,\frac{r}{V} + a C_f\,\delta \end{aligned} \]

3. Divide through

Divide the first by \(mV\) and subtract \(r\) to get \(\dot\beta\); divide the second by \(I_z\) to get \(\dot r\). Setting both derivatives to zero recovers the steady state of Lesson 5.2, including \(\delta = L/R + K a_y\).

Stability derivatives in plain language

Each coefficient in those equations says how much side force or yaw moment one kind of motion creates. Engineers call them stability derivatives. Four of them carry the physics:

DerivativeValueWhat it means
Side force from sideslip\(-(C_f + C_r)\)A car sliding sideways gets a side force that opposes the slide. Always resists.
Yaw moment from sideslip\(b C_r - a C_f\)Positive when the neutral steer point is behind the CG (it equals \(\text{SM}\cdot L\,(C_f + C_r)\)): sideslip turns the nose toward the direction of travel, like a weathervane.
Yaw damping\(-(a^2 C_f + b^2 C_r)/V\)Yaw rate creates slip angles that oppose the rotation. It weakens as \(1/V\): the reason high-speed handling is harder.
Control moment\(a\,C_f\)The yaw moment per unit of steer. It is what the driver uses to start a turn.

Natural frequency and damping

A pair of linear equations like these behaves like a mass, spring and damper. Its characteristic equation is \(\lambda^2 + 2\zeta\omega_n\lambda + \omega_n^2 = 0\). The two roots \(\lambda\) are the eigenvalues: each mode of motion evolves as \(e^{\lambda t}\), so it dies away when the real part of \(\lambda\) is negative. The coefficients are

\[ \begin{aligned} \omega_n^2 &= \frac{C_f C_r L^2}{m I_z V^2}\left(1 + \frac{K V^2}{L}\right) \\[6pt] 2\zeta\omega_n &= \frac{C_f + C_r}{m V} + \frac{a^2 C_f + b^2 C_r}{I_z V} \end{aligned} \]

where \(K\) is the understeer gradient of Lesson 5.3. Read the trends straight off:

  • Damping falls with speed. Both terms in \(2\zeta\omega_n\) shrink as \(1/V\). At low speed the response is overdamped and settles without oscillating; at high speed it overshoots.
  • Understeer keeps the car stiff. With \(K > 0\), \(\omega_n^2\) levels off at \(C_f C_r L K/(m I_z)\) instead of falling to zero.
  • Oversteer loses stability. With \(K < 0\), \(\omega_n^2\) reaches zero at the critical speed \(\sqrt{L/|K|}\). Above it one mode grows exponentially: Lesson 1.2's spin, now explained.

Find the natural frequency and damping ratio of the club racer (\(m = 750\) kg, \(a = 1.176\) m, \(b = 1.274\) m, \(I_z = 955\) kg·m², \(C_f = 114\,600\) N/rad, \(C_r = 171\,900\) N/rad, \(K = 0.00131\) rad/(m/s²)) at 90 and 180 km/h.

90 km/h (25 m/s):

\[ \begin{aligned} \omega_n^2 &= \frac{114\,600 \times 171\,900 \times 2.45^2}{750 \times 955 \times 25^2}\left(1 + \frac{0.00131 \times 625}{2.45}\right) \\ &= 264.1 \times 1.334 = 352\ \text{s}^{-2} \end{aligned} \]

so \(\omega_n = 18.8\) rad/s (3.0 Hz). \(2\zeta\omega_n = 15.3 + 18.3 = 33.6\ \text{s}^{-1}\), so \(\zeta = 0.90\): barely any overshoot.

180 km/h (50 m/s): \(\omega_n^2 = 154\ \text{s}^{-2}\), \(\omega_n = 12.4\) rad/s (2.0 Hz), and \(2\zeta\omega_n = 16.8\ \text{s}^{-1}\), so \(\zeta = 0.68\). Doubling the speed has halved the damping term, and the car now overshoots noticeably when steered (Lesson 6.2).

“A stable car settles without overshoot.” Stability only means a disturbance dies away eventually. A stable car with a low damping ratio swings past its new heading and back before settling, which drivers feel as a nervous or “floaty” car. Stability and good damping are separate goals.

Check your understanding

A Formula SAE car has \(m = 280\) kg, \(a = 0.8215\) m and \(b = 0.7285\) m, and a dynamic index of 0.8. What is its yaw moment of inertia?

\(I_z = \text{DI} \times m\,a\,b\).

\(I_z = 0.8 \times 280 \times 0.8215 \times 0.7285 = 134\) kg·m².

A car at 30 m/s has a yaw rate of 0.20 rad/s, and its sideslip is changing at 0.05 rad/s. What is its lateral acceleration?

\(a_y = V(\dot\beta + r)\).

\(a_y = 30 \times (0.05 + 0.20) = 7.5\) m/s², or 0.76 g. In a steady turn (\(\dot\beta = 0\)) it would be only 6.0 m/s².

At 180 km/h the club racer has \(\omega_n^2 = 154.2\ \text{s}^{-2}\) and \(2\zeta\omega_n = 16.8\ \text{s}^{-1}\). What is its damping ratio \(\zeta\)?

Find \(\omega_n\) first, then divide.

\(\omega_n = \sqrt{154.2} = 12.42\) rad/s, so \(\zeta = 16.8/(2 \times 12.42) = 0.68\).

The yaw moment from sideslip, \(b C_r - a C_f\), is positive for a car. What does a gust that makes it slide sideways do?

  • It creates a yaw moment that turns the nose toward the direction of travel, reducing the sideslip.That is weathervane stability: the neutral steer point is behind the CG.
  • It turns the nose away from the direction of travel.That happens when the derivative is negative (neutral steer point ahead of the CG).
  • It creates no yaw moment, only a side force.Only a car with its neutral steer point exactly at the CG would behave like that.

Why does yaw damping weaken as speed rises?

  • Because the tires get hotter.Temperature isn't in this model.
  • Because a given yaw rate creates slip angles \(a r/V\) and \(b r/V\), which shrink as \(V\) grows, so the restoring tire forces shrink too.The damping derivative is \(-(a^2 C_f + b^2 C_r)/V\).
  • Because the car's yaw inertia grows with speed.\(I_z\) is a property of the mass distribution and doesn't change with speed.

On the phase portrait, the paths spiral into the steady state at 200 km/h but head straight in at 30 km/h. What does that tell you?

  • The car is stable at both speeds, but overdamped at low speed and underdamped (oscillatory) at high speed.Spirals mean complex eigenvalues; straight approaches mean real ones.
  • The car is unstable at 200 km/h.The paths still end at the steady state, so it is stable.
  • The car oversteers at high speed.An understeering car becomes underdamped at speed too; damping and balance are different questions.
